## 1. Probability Ranking Principle: statement, optimality, failures, diversity and portfolio theory

### Takeaway
Ranking by marginal probability of relevance (PRP) is optimal only for metrics that are linear in per-document relevance, such as expected precision and recall at a cutoff. It assumes each document's relevance can be judged in isolation, the probabilities are calibrated and known, and there is one user need. Set-valued goals ("at least one useful passage", coverage of facets or nuggets, avoiding redundant context) break these assumptions. They make optimal ranking NP-hard and favour diversity-aware greedy selection, which has a (1−1/e) guarantee when the objective is submodular. For RAG, the payoff is a property of the *set* of retrieved passages, so PRP is the wrong optimality target for the final context-assembly step, though it remains the right one for first-stage candidate generation.

### Cited Findings
**PRP statement and stated scope.**
- Statement as given by Robertson & Zaragoza: "If retrieved documents are ordered by decreasing probability of relevance on the data available, then the system's effectiveness is the best that can be obtained for the data." They add that the PRP "can be justified under some further assumptions, for a range of specific measures of effectiveness. It is not, however, completely general", that counter-examples "depend on probabilities defined over a population of users", and that "The PRP is safe for the case considered: an individual user" — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009, FnTIR §2.3](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- The framework's relevance assumptions are explicit. Relevance is "a property of the document given information need only, assessable without reference to other documents", and it is binary. The authors call both assumptions "at the least arguable" and note one can imagine "one document's relevance can only be perceived by the user in the context of another document" — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- Robertson's 1977 paper answers Cooper's challenge. It argues the principle "can be justified under certain assumptions" but fails when they do not hold. Its main weakness is that it "considers each document independently of the rest", and whether that is valid depends on the kind of information the system uses — [Robertson 1977, J. Documentation (abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1108/eb026647)
- Proof of optimality (my sketch of the standard argument). Let p_i = P(rel_i | evidence). For a cutoff n, E[#relevant in top n] = Σ_{j≤n} p_{π(j)} by linearity of expectation, whatever the dependence between documents. An exchange argument shows that sorting by p maximises this sum for every n at once, so it maximises expected precision@n. With costs C_rel < C_nonrel for retrieving a relevant or non-relevant document, the expected cost of the top n, Σ_{j≤n} [C_rel p_{π(j)} + C_nonrel (1 − p_{π(j)})], is minimised by the same ordering. The argument needs the metric to be *additive* over documents. It does not need independence for the expectation itself, but it does need the gain of a document not to depend on which other documents are ranked above it. Chen & Karger state the same result: ranking by probability of relevance "yields the maximum expected number of relevant documents, and thus maximizes the expected values of the well known precision and recall metrics" — [Chen & Karger 2006](https://doi.org/10.1145/1148170.1148245)
- Decision-theoretic conditions: Gordon & Lenk (1991) showed the PRP optimal within a signal-detection/decision-theory framework, maximising expected utility. Gordon & Lenk (1992) list the conditions as "calibration, independent assessment of relevance by the inquirer, and certainty about the computed probabilities of relevance", and show the PRP "can be suboptimal with respect to expected utility when one of these conditions fails to hold" — [Gordon & Lenk 1992, JASIS (abstract via RePEc/catalog)](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-4571(199201)43:1%3C1::AID-ASI1%3E3.0.CO;2-5)

**Failure for set-valued metrics (k-call, %no, MAP).**
- Chen & Karger define *k-call at n* (1 if at least k of the top n are relevant). 1-call is 1 − %no, the TREC robust-track metric, and n-call is perfect precision. For these metrics the expected-value-optimal ranking differs from PRP. Exact optimisation "appears intractable" (the abstract says optimising the quantity is NP-hard), and a greedy algorithm beats the PRP baseline on TREC. Their analysis also concludes "PRP ranking is not optimal for the heavily used mean average precision metric" — [Chen & Karger 2006 (SIGIR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1148170.1148245)
- Diversity emerges from the objective. Greedily maximising P(at least one relevant) conditions each next pick on the earlier picks being non-relevant, which "increas[es] the diversity of documents at the top" without any explicit diversity term. Greedy optimisation of perfect precision reduces to blind positive (pseudo-)relevance feedback — [Chen & Karger 2006](https://doi.org/10.1145/1148170.1148245)

**Diversification theorems.**
- MMR (Carbonell & Goldstein) selects each next document by a linear combination of query relevance and "minimal similarity to previously selected documents" ("marginal relevance"). It is a heuristic and comes with no optimality proof — [Carbonell & Goldstein 1998](https://doi.org/10.1145/290941.291025)
- Agrawal et al. define Diversify(k): choose S, |S| = k, to maximise P(S|q) = Σ_c P(c|q)·[1 − Π_{d∈S}(1 − V(d|q,c))], the probability that the average user (over intents c) finds at least one satisfying document. *Lemma 1:* Diversify(k) is NP-hard. *Lemma 2:* P(S|q) is submodular. *Theorem 1:* greedy IA-Select is optimal when each document belongs to one category. *Corollary 2:* IA-Select is a (1 − 1/e)-approximation (the Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher greedy bound for monotone submodular maximisation). When documents span several categories, no single ordering may be optimal for every k — [Agrawal et al. 2009 (WSDM)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1498759.1498766)
- α-nDCG models gain over information nuggets: a document's gain for nugget i is discounted by (1 − α)^{r_{i,k−1}}, where r is the number of earlier documents containing that nugget. The paper derives this from a probability that the user still values a nugget. "In theory, the computation of the ideal gain vector is NP-complete" (by reduction from minimal vertex cover), so the normaliser is computed greedily — [Clarke et al. 2008 (SIGIR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1390334.1390446)

**Portfolio theory (mean–variance ranking).**
- Wang & Zhu treat a ranked list as a portfolio with E[R_n] = Σ_i w_i E[r_i] and Var(R_n) = Σ_i Σ_j w_i w_j c_{i,j}, where w_1 > w_2 > … are rank-position weights and c_{i,j} is the covariance of the relevance estimates. Maximising E[R_n] alone with decreasing w_i "is equivalent to the PRP". The proposed objective trades the mean against variance with one risk parameter, solved greedily. Diversifying (choosing documents with negatively correlated relevance) reduces variance. A greedy that considers only neighbouring-document correlation is "in fact a special case" — [Wang & Zhu 2009 (SIGIR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1571941.1571963)

**Other departures from PRP that matter for RAG.**
- Fairness of exposure: rankings can be optimised for user utility *subject to* exposure-allocation constraints, solved efficiently, which shows PRP-utility is one objective among several — [Singh & Joachims 2018 (KDD)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)
- Stochastic RAG explicitly "relaxes the simplifying assumptions of marginalization and document independence" by modelling retrieval as sampling without replacement (Gumbel-top-k), and improves 6 of 7 datasets — [Zamani & Bendersky 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02816)
- Relevance labels are a weak proxy for usefulness to the reader: "evaluation of the retrieval model's performance based on query-document relevance labels shows a small correlation with the RAG system's downstream performance". Labelling each document by the LLM's downstream output raised Kendall's τ by 0.168–0.494 — [Salemi & Zamani 2024 (eRAG)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13781)

### Inferences
- **[J] Dedup by canonical reference** (SKB). Near-duplicate chunks have highly correlated relevance, which means large covariance terms c_{i,j}. Under portfolio theory and under α-nDCG/Diversify(k), a second copy adds variance or zero marginal gain. Hard dedup is the limiting case of the redundancy penalty and is justified even when it costs PRP-measured precision. My inference.
- **[B] "Multi-query and MMR did not help"** (SKB, citing ARAGOG). The theory predicts diversity pays only when (a) the query has several intents or facets, or (b) the candidate set is redundant. A single-intent benchmark with deduplicated units satisfies PRP's assumptions closely, so a null MMR result there is expected. It does not show diversity is useless for multi-facet agent questions such as "what applies here" over policies, procedures and exceptions. My inference.
- **[J/B] Set-level context assembly.** For an agent the payoff is whether the *context set* is sufficient (cf. the report's "sufficient context" signal). That is a 1-call or coverage objective, not the expected-precision objective PRP optimises. The principled final step is greedy submodular selection over claims or nuggets with a token budget, which carries a (1 − 1/e) guarantee if the coverage function is monotone submodular. First-stage retrieval can stay PRP-ordered. This is my inference. The submodular guarantee holds only for the modelled objective, not for end-task accuracy.
- **[J] Evaluate on set utility, not only nDCG.** Chen & Karger's MAP result and eRAG's weak correlation together suggest the golden set should score "at least one sufficient unit in top-k" (1-call) and nugget coverage alongside nDCG. My inference.

### Gaps
- Robertson 1977 full text (Cooper's counter-example and the formal proof) is paywalled. Only the abstract and the FnTIR restatement were read.
- Gordon & Lenk 1992 full text not read; conditions taken from the abstract/catalog record.
- No source found that proves PRP-suboptimality specifically for LLM-reader utility in RAG. eRAG and Stochastic RAG are empirical or optimisation papers, not optimality theorems.

## 2. Probabilistic relevance framework, BM25, BIM, language models, DFR and axiomatic IR; why BM25 stays strong

### Takeaway
BM25 is not an ad hoc formula. It is a fitted approximation to a 2-Poisson "eliteness" model inside the PRP framework. IDF is the no-relevance-information limit of the Robertson/Spärck Jones weight, tf-saturation follows from the eliteness mixture, and soft length normalisation reconciles a "verbosity" and a "scope" hypothesis. Language-model smoothing provably reproduces TF-IDF weighting and length normalisation, and DFR derives similar weights from information content. Axiomatic analysis gives checkable constraints that BM25 satisfies except for a known negative-IDF defect. BM25 remains a strong baseline because it has about two free parameters, estimates all its statistics from the target corpus itself, and lives in a |V|-dimensional sparse space. That space escapes the dimension limits in Section 3. The last point is my inference, partly supported by LIMIT and BEIR.

### Cited Findings
**Binary independence model and RSJ/IDF.**
- BIM term weight: w_i^BIM = log[ P(t_i|rel)(1 − P(t_i|¬rel)) / ((1 − P(t_i|rel)) P(t_i|¬rel)) ]. The derivation rests on term independence given relevance (and, separately, given non-relevance) and on a query-terms-only assumption — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §2–3](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- RSJ estimator with 0.5 pseudo-counts: w_i^RSJ = log[(r_i + 0.5)(N − R − n_i + r_i + 0.5) / ((n_i − r_i + 0.5)(R − r_i + 0.5))]. With no relevance information (R = r_i = 0, equivalent to P(t_i|rel) = 0.5, and the "complement method" treating the whole collection as non-relevant), it becomes "a close approximation to classical idf", log((N − n_i + 0.5)/(n_i + 0.5)) — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §3.1–3.2](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)

**Eliteness, 2-Poisson and saturation (BM25 derivation).**
- Eliteness E_i ∈ {elite, ¬elite} is a hidden per-term "aboutness" variable. tf depends on relevance only through eliteness, and tf | elite ~ Poisson(λ_1), tf | ¬elite ~ Poisson(λ_0) with λ_1 > λ_0 (Harter's 2-Poisson model). The resulting weight w^elite(tf) satisfies w(0) = 0, increases monotonically in tf, and "asymptotically approaches a maximum value" lim_{tf→∞} w^elite(tf) = log[p_1(1 − p_0)/((1 − p_1)p_0)] = w^BIM. This is **saturation**: a term's contribution cannot exceed what observing eliteness itself would give — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §3.4.1–3.4.2](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- Special case: if eliteness coincides with relevance (p_1 = 1, p_0 = 0) the limit is infinite and the weight becomes linear in tf, the classic tf·idf. The saturating function "has frequently been shown to work better" — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §3.4.3](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- The exact 2-Poisson weight has unknown parameters, so BM25 *fits* the shape with tf/(k + tf), which satisfies the listed properties. It is a curve fit to a model, not a closed-form derivation — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §3.4.4](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- Length normalisation: the 2-Poisson model assumes fixed document length. Real length variation mixes "verbosity" (normalise fully) and "scope" (do not normalise), hence soft normalisation B = (1 − b) + b·dl/avdl and the final w_i^BM25 = tf/(k_1((1 − b) + b·dl/avdl) + tf) · w_i^RSJ. "The model provides no guidance" on k_1 and b. Empirically 0.5 < b < 0.8 and 1.2 < k_1 < 2 work in many collections, and BM25 is "quite robust with respect to their parameters" — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §3.4.5, §3.5, §5](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- **BM25F**: if eliteness is a term–*document* property shared across fields, evidence should be combined "first streams, then terms": tf~_i = Σ_s v_s tf_{si} and dl~ = Σ_s v_s sl_s, with saturation applied once to the combined evidence. The alternative of scoring each stream with BM25 and summing assumes an independent eliteness per stream, which the authors call "a little unreasonable" — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §3.6](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- Reproducibility: eight BM25 variants (including Lucene's lossy length encoding) showed "no significant effectiveness differences" on three newswire collections — [Kamphuis et al. 2020 (ECIR)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-45442-5_4)

**Language-modelling approach and its relation to TF-IDF.**
- Query likelihood with smoothing p_s(w|d) for seen words and α_d p(w|C) for unseen words decomposes, up to a document-independent term, into log p(q|d) = Σ_{i: c(q_i,d)>0} log[p_s(q_i|d)/(α_d p(q_i|C))] + n log α_d. The matched-term weight is "directly proportional to the document term frequency, but inversely proportional to the collection frequency", so the collection model "play[s] a role very similar to the well-known IDF". The n log α_d term acts as document length normalisation. Hence smoothing with a collection reference model "implies a retrieval formula that implements TF-IDF weighting heuristics and document length normalization" — [Zhai & Lafferty 2004 (TOIS)](https://doi.org/10.1145/984321.984322)
- Dirichlet prior: the matched-term weight is log(1 + c(q_i;d)/(μ p(q_i|C))), α_d is smaller for long documents (length penalty), and |d|/μ governs the trade-off. Retrieval performance is "highly sensitive" to smoothing parameters — [Zhai & Lafferty 2004](https://doi.org/10.1145/984321.984322)
- Lafferty & Zhai argue that the LM approach and the classical probabilistic model are two factorisations of one probabilistic model and "can be viewed as being equivalent". Later work disputes this, finding LMs differ "in every aspect" and that their effectiveness "cannot be explained by the PRP". Robertson & Zaragoza note the simple LM implicitly assumes a single relevant document per query instance — [Lafferty & Zhai 2003 (abstract and secondary discussion)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-017-0171-6_1); [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §4.3](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)

**Divergence from randomness (information-theoretic weights).**
- DFR builds nonparametric weights as w = Inf_1 · Inf_2. Inf_1 = −log_2 Prob_1 is the informative content (surprisal) of observing tf under a random model (binomial or Bose–Einstein), so terms whose distribution diverges most from randomness carry most information. Inf_2 = 1 − Prob_2 is the "first normalization" (an aftereffect/elite-set gain), and a "second normalization" handles document length — [Amati & van Rijsbergen 2002 (TOIS)](https://doi.org/10.1145/582415.582416). A DFR formulation "approximately the same as BM25" can be derived — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §4.6](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)

**Axiomatic IR.**
- Fang, Tao & Zhai define six constraints for a retrieval function f(d,q). TFC1: more occurrences of the query term means a higher score at equal length. TFC2: concave gain in tf, which implies a preference for covering more distinct query terms. TDC: favour occurrences of higher-IDF terms. LNC1: adding a non-query term lowers the score. LNC2: concatenating a document k times must not lower its score. TF-LNC: adding query-term occurrences raises the score despite added length. "None of these retrieval functions satisfies all the constraints unconditionally". Violations predict poor performance, but "satisfying all the constraints does not necessarily guarantee good performance" — [Fang, Tao & Zhai 2004 (SIGIR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1008992.1009004)
- BM25 specifically: its IDF part log((N − df + 0.5)/(df + 0.5)) is negative when df > N/2. It then violates TFCs, LNCs and TF-LNC, since "matching an additional occurrence of a query term could mean decreasing the score". With positive IDF the TFCs and LNCs hold, and with b < 1 TF-LNC holds unconditionally. Replacing the IDF with a non-negative one fixes it — [Fang, Tao & Zhai 2004](https://doi.org/10.1145/1008992.1009004)
- Lower-bounding TF normalisation: in BM25, LM and DFR the length-normalised TF is "not lower-bounded properly; as a result, very long documents tend to be overly penalized". Adding a lower bound (BM25+ style) fixes it at almost no cost, especially for verbose queries — [Lv & Zhai 2011 (CIKM)](https://doi.org/10.1145/2063576.2063584)
- Neural models and axioms: BERT rankers did *not* adhere to any of the tested axioms on diagnostic datasets, yet beat query likelihood by 40% on a large web corpus. The authors conclude new axioms are needed — [Câmara & Hauff 2020 (ECIR)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.04458)

**Why BM25 remains a strong baseline (evidence).**
- BEIR (18 datasets, zero-shot): "BM25 is a robust baseline". Re-ranking and late-interaction models were best on average at high cost, while dense and sparse models "often underperform" out of distribution — [Thakur et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08663)
- On the counter side, DPR beat Lucene-BM25 by 9–19 points top-20 accuracy on open-domain QA *in-domain*, after training on that task distribution — [Karpukhin et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)
- LIMIT: BM25 Recall@100 = 93.6 against at most 18.9 for single-vector embedders and 54.8 for a ColBERT model — [Weller et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21038)
- Weak baselines inflate neural gains: improvements "could very well be attributed to mundane issues like better hyperparameter optimization" — [Lin 2019 (SIGIR Forum)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3308774.3308781)

### Inferences
- **[J] Field boosts as BM25F, not as a sum of per-field BM25** (SKB "field boosts on title, identifiers, altLabels"; breadcrumb prefix). BM25F's eliteness argument says a term's evidence from the breadcrumb, title and body should be added *before* saturation. Otherwise a term that appears in both title and body is double-counted past its saturation point. The heading-path prefix is in effect a cheap high-weight stream folded into one field. A true BM25F (stream weights v_s) is the principled generalisation, with v_s tuned on the golden set like the fusion weights. My inference. Whether a given engine's multi-field query implements BM25F or per-field summation must be checked per engine (gap).
- **[B] Chunk-length policy and b.** Structural chunks merged to about 256–512 tokens narrow the dl/avdl spread, so BM25 becomes less sensitive to b, and the Lv–Zhai over-penalisation of very long units mostly disappears. Mixing page-level slide units with short FAQ units in one index brings length heterogeneity back, which argues for per-unit-type fields or BM25+ lower bounding. My inference.
- **[J] Negative IDF in small or skewed KB indexes.** In a small corpus where a domain word (the product name, "policy") occurs in more than half the units, classic Okapi IDF goes negative and violates TFC1. The toolkit should use a non-negative IDF variant (as Lucene's log(1 + (N − n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)) does; implementation detail not verified here) and must not "freeze" negative weights. My inference.
- **[J] "BM25 with frozen statistics" as a determinism rung** (DRC). BM25's only corpus-dependent quantities are N, n_i, avdl and dl. Freezing them with the snapshot makes scores a pure function of (query, snapshot), and ties break by an external ID ([Lin & Yang 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.05798)). My inference.
- **Why BM25 is hard to beat on agent KBs** (my inference from the theory). It has two parameters and no learned components, so there is no train/test distribution shift. Its IDF is computed on the deployed corpus, so domain jargon gets domain-appropriate weights for free. Its representation is |V|-dimensional and sparse, so the dimension and margin limits of Section 3 do not bind (identifiers, codes and CVE numbers are near-orthogonal axes). Its failure mode, vocabulary mismatch, is exactly what glossary expansion and dense retrieval repair.

### Gaps
- Robertson & Walker 1994 (the original 2-Poisson approximation paper), Ponte & Croft 1998 and Robertson 2004 (theoretical arguments for IDF) were not read directly. Their content is taken from the FnTIR and TOIS restatements.
- No formal proof was found that BM25 is optimal under any realistic generative model. Its form is a fitted approximation by its authors' own account.
- No axiomatic analysis of *dense* retrievers on enterprise-style identifier queries was found.

## 3. Theory of dense retrieval: dual-encoder capacity, LIMIT/sign-rank, late interaction, learned sparse

### Takeaway
Single-vector inner-product retrieval has provable capacity limits, but the binding quantity is **margin and numeric precision, not dimension alone**. With infinite precision, any top-k pattern is realisable in about 2k dimensions. With a margin γ, or B-bit coordinates, the needed dimension grows with log C(n,k) or with k·ln N. Long documents shrink margins, which pushes dimension up. Multi-vector MaxSim strictly extends what inner products can express with the same representation space and can simulate sparse inner products exactly. Sparse lexical vectors live in |V| dimensions and so are not bound by these limits. These results justify keeping a lexical channel and "match small". They also correct the report's shorthand that LIMIT "proved" single-vector embedders cannot represent top-k sets: the proof needs a margin or finite-precision assumption.

### Cited Findings
**Luan et al.: margin, length and dimension.**
- Definition 2.1 (normalised margin): for ⟨q,d_1⟩ ≥ ⟨q,d_2⟩, μ(q,d_1,d_2) = ⟨q, d_1 − d_2⟩ / (‖q‖·‖d_1 − d_2‖). Lemma 1: for a Gaussian or Rademacher random projection A ∈ R^{k×d} and μ = ε > 0, the pairwise ranking-error probability is β ≤ 4 exp(−(k/2)(ε²/2 − ε³/3)). Corollary 1: k > 12 ε^{−2} ln(4/β) suffices. Lemma 2 extends this by a union bound to recall-at-r_0 against a collection — [Luan et al. 2021 (TACL)](https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00369)
- For TF-IDF/BM25 vectors the minimum non-zero margin "decreases with the number of terms … as each additional term creates more ways in which two documents can receive nearly the same score". Empirically (TREC-CAR), normalised margins decrease with document length, implying "limitations in the capacity of fixed-length encodings to support precise retrieval of long documents". Theorem 1: splitting documents into m orthogonal segments and scoring by the max over segments can only increase the margin, which motivates the multi-vector ME-BERT and sparse–dense hybrids, both of which outperformed strong alternatives — [Luan et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00181)
- Caveat on reach: Lemma 1 is an *upper* bound on the dimension a *random projection* needs to mimic a sparse model. It is not a lower bound for learned encoders. The paper offers tightness only as "suggested" — [Luan et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00181). A matching worst-case lower bound for distance-preserving embeddings is Larsen & Nelson's m = Ω(ε^{−2} lg n) for any (even non-linear) map preserving all pairwise squared distances within 1 ± ε — [Larsen & Nelson 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02094)

**LIMIT (Weller et al.) — what is actually proved.**
- Setup: unit document vectors v_1..v_n and unit queries. A k-subset S is "realized with margin γ" if some unit u_S has min_{i∈S}⟨u_S,v_i⟩ ≥ max_{j∉S}⟨u_S,v_j⟩ + 2γ (feasible only for 0 < γ ≤ 1). **Theorem 1:** if every k-subset is realised with margin γ, then C(n,k) ≤ (1 + 1/γ)^d, so d ≥ log C(n,k) / log(1 + 1/γ). *Proof:* for S ≠ T choose i ∈ S∖T and j ∈ T∖S. Then ⟨u_S − u_T, v_i − v_j⟩ ≥ 4γ and ‖v_i − v_j‖ ≤ 2, so ‖u_S − u_T‖ ≥ 2γ. The γ-balls around the C(n,k) query points are disjoint inside a ball of radius 1 + γ, and a volume count gives the bound — [Weller et al. 2025 (v2)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21038)
- Rank notions (appendix): row-wise order-preserving rank equals row-wise thresholdable rank (Prop. 1), and rank±(2A − 1) − 1 ≤ rank_rop(A) = rank_rt(A) ≤ rank_gt(A) ≤ rank±(2A − 1) (Prop. 2). **Crucial correction in v2:** "the cyclotomic polynomial construction presented in Alon et al. (1985) implies that any qrel matrix has sign-rank at most 2k", which "requires infinite precision in general, and is thus not feasible in practice". The v1 no-margin sign-rank bound is "weaker" — [Weller et al. 2025 (v2, App. D)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21038); background on sign rank vs VC dimension: [Alon, Moran & Yehudayoff 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.07648)
- Empirics: for k = 2, free (directly optimised) embeddings have a critical-n that fits a cubic in d (r² = 0.999), extrapolating to about 500k documents at d = 512, 1.7M at 768, 4M at 1024 and 250M at 4096. On LIMIT (50k docs), Recall@100 was BM25 93.6, best single-vector embedder 18.9 (Promptriever-Llama3-8B, 4096-d) and GTE-ModernColBERT 54.8. Training on LIMIT's train split raised Recall@10 only to about 2.8 — [Weller et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21038)
- Precision version: with B bits per coordinate, making every k-subset realisable as a top-k result requires B·d = Ω(k ln N). The corpus-independent d = O(k) bound "is specific to infinite precision". Below a precision threshold B* = O(ln ln N), no dimension suffices. "In practical vector databases … where quantization is standard, the embedding dimension and possibly the precision must grow with the corpus size" — [Okajima & Yoshida 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11780)
- Index-size effect: dense performance decreases faster than sparse as the index grows, possibly reaching a tipping point where sparse wins. Lower dimension means more false positives — [Reimers & Gurevych 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14210)
- Dimension scaling is a power law for evaluation tasks aligned with training. For less-aligned tasks, performance is less predictable and sometimes *degrades* with larger dimension — [Killingback et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05062)
- High-dimensional geometry of inner products: for spherically symmetric data, lim_{d→∞} P(⟨q,X⟩ > ε) = 0. Points become nearly orthogonal to a random query — [Bruch 2024, Thm 2.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09350)
- Generative retrieval vs dense: DR is limited by "the global low-rank structure" and by local normalisation. GR's capacity can scale with parameters, "but despite its theoretical advantages, GR does not universally outperform DR in practice" — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22116)

**Late interaction (ColBERT / MaxSim) and multi-vector guarantees.**
- ColBERT encodes query and document independently and scores with a late MaxSim interaction, so document representations can be precomputed. It was competitive with BERT cross-encoders at two orders of magnitude lower latency — [Khattab & Zaharia 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)
- Capacity theorem: MaxSim "can exactly replicate the inner product between any two non-negative k-sparse vectors with possibly infinite dimension, requiring only O(k) representation space". Some similarities MaxSim can express cannot be expressed by inner products with the same space. Standard MaxSim cannot replicate arbitrary real inner products, but Signed MaxSim can. MaxSim acts as an aggregation of soft-ORs and can evaluate positive-CNF logical expressions. Empirically, Signed MaxSim raised nDCG@10 on negation-only queries from 0.008 to 0.788 — [Killingback, Ingale, Zamani & Musco 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05803)
- MUVERA's fixed-dimensional encodings give "high-quality ε-approximations" of multi-vector (Chamfer) similarity. This is the first single-vector proxy with guarantees, letting off-the-shelf MIPS serve multi-vector models (+10% recall, −90% latency vs prior implementations on BEIR) — [Dhulipala et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19504)

**Learned sparse.**
- SPLADE learns sparse lexical-plus-expansion weights with a log-saturation and an explicit sparsity regulariser. It keeps "exact matching of terms and the efficiency of inverted indexes", and sparsity is tunable against effectiveness — [Formal et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05720)
- Learned sparse weights are "wacky": they defeat the skipping and early-exit optimisations (WAND/MaxScore-style document-at-a-time) that make BM25 cheap, so score-at-a-time evaluation becomes competitive — [Mackenzie, Trotman & Lin 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.11540)

### Inferences
- **[C → corrected] SKB/ADL's LIMIT sentence** ("Google DeepMind proved that single-vector embedders are bounded in which top-k sets they can represent"). The correct statement is narrower. With a required margin γ (Theorem 1), or B-bit precision (Okajima & Yoshida), the number of realisable top-k sets is bounded by d. Without margin or precision limits, about 2k dimensions suffice for any qrel pattern. The practical conclusion still holds, because real systems are low-precision, quantised and trained by gradient descent. The empirical LIMIT failure (≤18.9 vs 93.6) is the decisive evidence, not a pure dimension impossibility. My inference from v2's own appendix.
- **[J] Never drop the lexical channel.** Identifier, code and acronym queries form a combinatorial family ("documents mentioning X and Y"). That is exactly the all-k-subsets regime of Theorem 1, which a sparse |V|-dimensional representation handles trivially and a fixed-d, quantised dense one cannot at scale. My inference.
- **[J] "Match small, read large" and structural chunking.** Luan et al.'s margin-decreases-with-length result plus Theorem 1 (max over orthogonal segments never lowers the margin) give a formal reason to embed short units (sentences, steps, FAQ questions) and return the parent section. My inference. Theorem 1 assumes orthogonal segments, which real sections only approximate.
- **[B] Quantisation in the vector store.** Okajima & Yoshida imply that int8/binary quantisation plus a fixed dimension degrades top-k realisability as the KB grows. Quantised ANN should be followed by exact re-scoring on full-precision vectors, and recall should be monitored as N grows. My inference.
- **[J] Late-interaction or multi-vector as the middle option.** MaxSim's ability to simulate sparse inner products in O(k) space, plus MUVERA's ε-guarantee, makes ColBERT-style indexes a principled alternative to hybrid fusion when one index is preferred. LIMIT's 54.8 shows it is still not a full substitute for BM25 on combinatorial queries. My inference.
- **[B] Learned sparse is not a free lunch.** It keeps inverted-index exactness but loses BM25's dynamic-pruning efficiency, so the report's "lexical indexes scale cheaply" claim applies to BM25-like weights, not automatically to SPLADE. My inference from Mackenzie et al.

### Gaps
- No tight lower bound on the dimension a *learned* dual encoder needs for realistic (non-adversarial) qrels; existing bounds are worst-case or for free embeddings.
- No theorem covering the dense + sparse *hybrid* capacity (what the union can represent) was found.
- The late-interaction capacity paper (2026) is recent and not yet peer-reviewed as far as I could see.

## 4. Rank fusion theory: RRF, CombSUM/CombMNZ, normalisation, Condorcet and data-fusion theory

### Takeaway
There is no optimality theorem for RRF. It is an empirically strong, scale-free, training-free heuristic, and its k = 60 was set on pilot data. The formal results are elsewhere. Convex-combination fusion is invariant, up to a reparametrised weight, across a family of linear score normalisations. RRF is not Lipschitz in the input scores and discards the score distribution. Exact rank aggregation (Kemeny) is NP-hard even for four lists, while footrule aggregation is polynomial and a 2-approximation. Lee's "unequal overlap" property explains why fusion helps: runs agree on relevant documents more than on non-relevant ones. Together these support the report's "tune the fusion, don't assume RRF" position and give a test for when hybrid helps.

### Cited Findings
- **RRF definition and evidence:** RRFscore(d) = Σ_{r∈R} 1/(k + r(d)) with "k = 60 … fixed during a pilot investigation". The intuition is that the importance of lower-ranked documents "does not vanish as it would were, say, an exponential function used", and that k "mitigates the impact of high rankings by outlier systems". RRF beat Condorcet-fuse in 7/7 experiments (sign test p ≈ 0.008), CombMNZ in 6/7, and the best individual run in 6–7/7, by 4–5% on average. The evidence is empirical and the paper gives no proof — [Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher 2009 (SIGIR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1571941.1572114)
- **CombSUM/CombMNZ and the unequal-overlap property:** CombMNZ(d) = |{r : d retrieved by r}| · Σ_r s_r(d), after per-run min–max normalisation. Lee measured overlap coefficients between run pairs (2|common|/(|A| + |B|)) separately for relevant and non-relevant documents. The degree of overlap among relevant documents was higher than among non-relevant ones, the "unequal overlap property": "different runs retrieve similar sets of relevant documents but retrieve different sets of nonrelevant documents". This is the stated rationale for why combination helps and why CombMNZ (which rewards agreement) beats CombSUM — [Lee 1997 (SIGIR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/278459.258587)
- **Convex combination and normalisation theory:** Bruch, Gai & Ingber study f = α φ(f_sem) + (1 − α) ω(f_lex). *Lemma 4.2:* for every query and α there exists α′ such that CC over min–max-normalised scores is rank-equivalent to CC over z-score-normalised scores, and vice versa. *Theorem 4.5:* for monotone transforms φ, ω, φ′, ω′, a single constant α′ gives rank-equivalence across a query set if φ′ expands relative to φ more rapidly than ω′ relative to ω at a *uniform* rate λ (their Definitions 4.3–4.4). The practical reading is that switching between bounded linear normalisations that do not distort the top-ranked score distribution yields a rank-equivalent fusion after re-tuning α, so "the learning of a CC fusion is generally agnostic to the choice of score normalization" — [Bruch et al. 2023 (TOIS)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3596512)
- **RRF's information loss:** RRF "disregards the distribution of scores", and it is not Lipschitz-continuous, since "the moment one lexical (or semantic) score becomes larger than another the function makes a hard transition". Contrary to earlier claims, RRF is "sensitive to its parameters": separate η_lex and η_sem helped, in opposite directions on MS MARCO (in-domain) and HotpotQA (out-of-domain). CC outperformed RRF in- and out-of-domain and is sample-efficient, needing only a small set of labelled examples to tune α — [Bruch et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11934)
- **Condorcet fusion:** a graph-theoretic analysis of the Condorcet (pairwise-majority) voting procedure yields a sorting-based algorithm, Condorcet-fuse. It performs well on TREC whether or not scores or training data are available and "significantly outperforms" Borda-fuse, the analogous positional-voting method — [Montague & Aslam 2002 (CIKM), abstract](https://doi.org/10.1145/584792.584881). Cormack et al. later found RRF better than Condorcet-fuse — [Cormack et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1145/1571941.1572114)
- **Rank-aggregation complexity:** Kemeny-optimal aggregation (maximising pairwise agreements) is NP-hard even when there are only four input rankings. Footrule aggregation is polynomial-time, by bipartite matching, and gives a 2-approximation to Kemeny. "Local Kemenization" satisfies the extended Condorcet criterion in O(kn log n) for k lists of n items — [Dwork, Kumar, Naor & Sivakumar 2001 (WWW)](https://doi.org/10.1145/371920.372165), read via secondary statements in [Hillebrand et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.11319)
- **Information-theoretic account of fusion:** Amigó et al. model system outputs and assessments in information-theoretic terms. Effectiveness becomes an information-theoretic similarity to the assessments, and fusion becomes "an information quantity measure". This explains "the empirically known phenomenon of effectiveness increase when combining retrieval system outputs in an unsupervised manner" — [Amigó, Giner, Mizzaro & Spina 2018 (ICTIR)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.04317)

### Inferences
- **[J] "Fusion must be tuned rather than assumed"** (SKB, Vespa NFCorpus: linear fusion 0.3423 > RRF 0.3233). Bruch et al.'s results (CC beats RRF and is sample-efficient, RRF is parameter-sensitive) are the formal and empirical backing. The golden set the report already requires (hundreds of queries) is far more than the "handful" CC needs. My inference.
- **[J] Choose RRF only when no labels exist or score scales drift.** RRF's scale invariance (it uses only ranks) is its real advantage when a dense model or BM25 statistics change between snapshots and no labelled queries exist. Once a golden set exists, CC with one α (and min–max or theoretical-min–max normalisation) is the principled default. My inference.
- **[J] A diagnostic for whether hybrid will help.** Lee's property can be measured directly on the KB. Compute R-overlap and N-overlap between BM25 and dense top-k on the golden set. If R-overlap ≫ N-overlap, fusion should help. If dense and BM25 retrieve *different* relevant units (low R-overlap), CombMNZ-style agreement bonuses help less and a union with reranking helps more. My inference. Lee's measurements were on TREC runs, not lexical-vs-dense pairs.
- **[B] Do not aim for "optimal" consensus rankings.** Kemeny NP-hardness means exact consensus is unattainable at scale. Footrule or sort-based Condorcet approximations are the tractable choices if more than two retrievers (BM25, dense, alias-expanded BM25, graph neighbours) are fused. My inference.
- **[J] Deterministic fusion.** RRF and CC are deterministic given deterministic input rankings. The non-determinism comes only from ANN inputs and tie-breaking (Section 5), so the fusion rule itself is not a determinism risk. Ties in fused scores still need an external-ID tiebreak. My inference.

### Gaps
- Montague & Aslam 2002 and Dwork et al. 2001 full texts could not be retrieved (TLS/host failures). Their claims rest on abstracts and secondary sources.
- No theorem was found giving conditions under which RRF provably beats its best input.
- Fox & Shaw 1994 (origin of CombSUM/CombMNZ) was not read directly. The definitions come from Lee 1997 and Cormack et al. 2009.

## 5. Approximate nearest-neighbour theory: curse of dimensionality, LSH guarantees and lower bounds, graph indexes, recall-vs-cost

### Takeaway
Worst-case ANN theory is clear. LSH gives sublinear (c,r)-ANN with exponent ρ = ln(1/p_1)/ln(1/p_2), and that exponent is tight for data-independent LSH (1/c in Hamming, 1/c² in Euclidean). Data-dependent hashing does better (1/(2c² − 1)), and SETH-based hardness rules out near-exact ANN in strongly sublinear time. The popular graph indexes (HNSW, NSG, DiskANN-fast) have **no worst-case guarantees**: there are instances needing linear query time, and a 2026 result shows no greedy flat or hierarchical graph method can be sublinear in the worst case for any approximation factor. Provable guarantees exist only under bounded intrinsic dimension, with navigable graphs, or with Steiner points. HNSW construction is randomised and order-dependent. These results justify the report's determinism ladder (exact kNN over a pre-filter before ANN) and treating ANN recall as a measured SLO, not a property.

### Cited Findings
**Curse of dimensionality.**
- Beyer et al. (as extended to general δ): if data points are i.i.d. and the query independent, and lim_{d→∞} Var[δ(q,X)]/E[δ(q,X)]² = 0, then for any ε > 0, lim P(δ(q,X) ≤ (1 + ε)δ(q,u*)) = 1, where u* is the nearest point. Nearly all points fall inside the (1 + ε)-enlarged NN ball. *Proof sketch:* min and max distances concentrate between α and β with β/α = 1 + ε — [Bruch 2024, Thm 2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09350); original: [Beyer et al. 1999 (ICDT)](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-49257-7_15)
- Inner product is not a metric: it is not non-negative, violates the triangle inequality, and does not even satisfy "coincidence" (a vector need not maximise its inner product with itself), so metric-space index guarantees do not transfer to MIPS without reductions — [Bruch 2024 §1.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09350)

**LSH upper bounds.**
- Definition (Indyk–Motwani): H is (r, cr, p_1, p_2)-sensitive if δ(u,v) ≤ r ⇒ P[h(u) = h(v)] ≥ p_1 and δ(u,v) > cr ⇒ P[h(u) = h(v)] ≤ p_2. With ρ = ln p_1 / ln p_2, L = n^ρ tables of ℓ = log_{1/p_2} n concatenated hashes solve the decision version (PLEB) with constant probability, in query time O(d n^ρ) and space O(n^{1+ρ}). The index stores each point L times, a super-linear space cost that "can easily become prohibitive". Bit-sampling in Hamming space is (r, cr, 1 − r/d, 1 − cr/d)-sensitive, giving ρ ≤ 1/c — [Bruch 2024, Def. 5.1, Thms 5.1–5.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09350); journal version of Indyk–Motwani: "space that is only polynomial in n and d, while achieving query times that are sub-linear in n and polynomial in d" — [Har-Peled, Indyk & Motwani 2012 (Theory of Computing)](https://doi.org/10.4086/toc.2012.v008a014)
- Angular distance: a practical cross-polytope LSH family attains the asymptotically optimal exponent and beats hyperplane LSH in practice. A fine-grained lower bound shows its time–quality trade-off is near-optimal for a natural class — [Andoni, Indyk, Laarhoven, Razenshteyn & Schmidt 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.02897)
- Data-dependent hashing: query O(d n^{ρ+o(1)}), space O(n^{1+ρ+o(1)} + dn), with ρ = 1/(2c² − 1) in Euclidean and 1/(2c − 1) in Hamming space, beating optimal data-independent LSH for all c > 1 — [Andoni & Razenshteyn 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.01062)

**Lower bounds.**
- LSH: in Hamming space any LSH family has ρ ≥ 1/c − o_d(1), matching Indyk–Motwani. This implies ρ ≥ 1/c² for Euclidean/sphere and 1/c for Jaccard, matching known upper bounds. The proof uses log-convexity of Boolean noise stability — [O'Donnell, Wu & Zhou 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.0250)
- Conditional hardness: under SETH, for every δ > 0 there is ε > 0 such that (1 + ε)-approximate bichromatic closest pair needs n^{2−δ} time. This implies near-linear query time for ANN with polynomial preprocessing at such small ε — [Rubinstein 2018 (STOC)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.00904)

**Graph-based indexes: guarantees and their absence.**
- Delaunay theory: if G contains the Delaunay graph, best-first greedy search finds the exact top-1 for any query (Thm 6.1), and the Delaunay graph is the *minimal* such graph (Thm 6.2). But in high dimensions Delaunay construction is exponential in d and "the graph is close to complete", so practical graphs are heuristic approximations — [Bruch 2024 §6.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09350)
- HNSW: a multi-layer proximity graph where each element's maximum layer "is selected randomly with an exponentially decaying probability distribution", plus a neighbour-selection heuristic. Its "logarithmic complexity scaling" is an empirical/heuristic claim — [Malkov & Yashunin 2016/2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09320)
- Worst case for practice: DiskANN's slow-preprocessing variant provably gives constant-factor ANN with polylog query time on data with bounded intrinsic dimension. For HNSW, NSG and DiskANN-fast there are instances where query time to reasonable accuracy is linear in n. DiskANN can take ≥ 0.1n steps before meeting any of the 5 true nearest neighbours — [Indyk & Xu 2023 (NeurIPS)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19126)
- General impossibility (2026): "no standard graph-based method (any that runs a variant of greedy search on a flat or hierarchical graph) can achieve sublinear query time for approximate nearest neighbor search in Euclidean space, even for an arbitrarily large approximation factor". With Steiner points, c-ANN is solvable in Õ(n^{1/(c−1)}) time — [Daliri et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38133)
- Positive results under assumptions: rigorous analysis of greedy NN-graph search in the low-dimensional regime d ≪ log n, including shortcut edges and candidate lists — [Prokhorenkova & Shekhovtsov 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00845). Greedy search on *approximate* NN graphs has guarantees for low-dimensional dense vectors — [Shrivastava, Song & Xu 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06210). On any navigable graph, distance-adaptive beam termination provably solves approximate NN and beats fixed-width beam search empirically on HNSW/Vamana graphs — [Al-Jazzazi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15636). MRNG guarantees near-logarithmic search, and NSG approximates it — [Fu et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00143). Probabilistic routing gives a probabilistic guarantee per neighbour-exploration step (1.6–2.5× throughput on HNSW/NSSG) — [Lu, Xiao & Ishikawa 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11354)
- Empirical trade-offs: across ANN-Benchmarks, "very different approaches to k-NN search yield comparable quality-performance trade-offs", and parameters must be swept per dataset — [Aumüller, Bernhardsson & Faithfull 2018/2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.05614)

**Determinism.**
- Score ties in Lucene are broken by internal document IDs assigned at (multi-threaded) index time, so rankings differ across index instances. Breaking ties by external IDs fixes it at a measurable efficiency cost — [Lin & Yang 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.05798)

### Inferences
- **[J] The DRC determinism ladder's "exact kNN over a structurally pre-filtered candidate set, then ANN".** Graph ANN has no worst-case recall guarantee (Indyk & Xu; Daliri et al.) and randomised, order-dependent construction (HNSW's random levels; FAISS insertion order, per DRC). Exact kNN over m pre-filtered candidates is a deterministic O(m·d) scan. Arithmetic (mine): m = 10⁵ and d = 768 is about 7.7×10⁷ multiply-adds per query, cheap on a CPU, so exact search is the right default below some m chosen by benchmark. My inference.
- **[J] The pgvector "~4 rows for a 10% filter" failure** (DRC). Graph ANN with post-filtering searches a graph whose navigability guarantees, where they exist, do not survive deleting 90% of nodes. Pre-filtering into exact search, or filter-aware indexes, is the theory-consistent fix. My inference.
- **[B] ANN recall is an empirical SLO.** Because no guarantee exists for HNSW-class indexes on adversarial or clustered data, the toolkit should record recall@k of ANN against exact kNN on the golden queries at every index build, and fail the build below a threshold. Al-Jazzazi et al.'s distance-adaptive termination is a principled knob for that trade-off. My inference.
- **[B] Curse-of-dimensionality concerns are about intrinsic, not ambient, dimension.** Beyer's concentration premise (i.i.d. coordinates) fails for learned embeddings, and the positive graph results all assume bounded intrinsic or doubling dimension. Measuring the KB's intrinsic dimension (e.g. local PCA) would indicate how much to trust ANN. I found no source quantifying intrinsic dimension of text-embedding corpora (gap).
- **[J] Determinism contract for lexical search too.** Lin & Yang show even exact BM25 is non-deterministic across index builds without an external-ID tiebreak. This supports DRC's "total ORDER BY with a primary-key tiebreaker" rule applied to search results. My inference.

### Gaps
- Datar et al. 2004 (p-stable LSH) was not read directly. The Euclidean LSH statements rest on Bruch 2024 and Andoni–Razenshteyn.
- Beyer et al. 1999's PDF was a scanned image. The theorem was read via Bruch's restatement.
- No theory was found for *filtered* graph ANN (attribute filters + vector search) in the worst case.

## 6. Evaluation theory: Cranfield, pooling bias, metric axioms (nDCG, ERR, RBP), measurement scales, significance, nuggets, LLM judges

### Takeaway
Cranfield-style evaluation is formally a *relative* comparison device. Rankings of systems are stable under assessor disagreement, but absolute recall is overestimated, and pools that are small relative to the collection bias judgments toward documents containing the query's title words, i.e. toward lexical systems. Metric axioms show no single metric satisfies all reasonable constraints. Standard nDCG has subtle asymptotics, and common metrics are not interval scales, which affects averaging and significance tests. Paired t, bootstrap and randomization tests agree, and Wilcoxon and sign tests do not. Nugget evaluation and α-nDCG formalise claim-level scoring. LLM judges agree with humans at the *system* level but are manipulable and circular when the same LLM is a system component. Prediction-powered inference turns a small human-labelled set plus LLM labels into valid confidence intervals.

### Cited Findings
**Cranfield and pooling.**
- System rankings stay highly correlated across different relevance-judgment sets, so "the comparative evaluation of retrieval performance is stable despite substantial differences in relevance judgments" — [Voorhees 2000 (IP&M)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0306-4573(00)00010-8) (abstract via search). A 2025 re-annotation of TREC DL'19 reproduces this for neural rankers. But "some models substantially degrade with our new relevance judgments, and some have already reached the effectiveness of humans as rankers", so test collections can "expire" — [Parry et al. 2025 (SIGIR)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20937)
- Zobel 1998: measured relative performance on TREC appeared reliable, but recall is overestimated because many relevant documents are never judged. Pooling bias did not appear significant for TREC at that scale. A pooling variant finds more relevant documents for the same effort — [Zobel 1998 (SIGIR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/290941.291014) (findings via search summaries)
- Pooling bias at scale: when pools are small relative to the collection, judgment sets "can be biased in that they favor relevant documents that contain topic title words". The phenomenon "is wholly dependent on the collection size", and AQUAINT (TREC 2005) exhibits it — [Buckley, Dimmick, Soboroff & Voorhees 2007 (IRJ)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10791-007-9032-x)

**Metric axioms and properties.**
- Amigó, Spina & Carrillo-de-Albornoz formalise 10 constraints: Priority, Deepness, Deepness Threshold, Closeness Threshold, Confidence, Query-Aspect Diversity, Redundancy, Monotonic Redundancy, Aspect-Relevance Saturation and Aspect Relevance. nDCG@k, ERR@k and RBP satisfy Priority/Deepness. AP and nDCG@k satisfy Closeness-Threshold but not Deepness-Threshold, while P@k, RR and ERR@k satisfy Deepness-Threshold but not Closeness-Threshold. RBP satisfies both, and "None of these metrics satisfy Conf". Among intent-aware metrics, ERR-IA satisfies Redundancy and Monotonic Redundancy, while nDCG-IA and RBP-IA fail Redundancy. "None of the existing diversity metrics satisfy all the" constraints, which motivated Rank-Biased Utility — [Amigó et al. 2018 (SIGIR)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.02334)
- Moffat lists seven numeric properties (boundedness, monotonicity, convergence, top-weightedness, localisation, completeness, realisability). Precision, RR, AP and nDCG "have different combinations" of them, and some properties are in tension — [Moffat 2013 (AIRS)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-45068-6_1) (abstract via search)
- nDCG asymptotics: with the standard log discount, NDCG "converges to 1 as the number of items to rank goes to infinity" for *every* ranking function. It nevertheless has "consistent distinguishability". Whether a discount has this property depends on its decay rate, with 1/r "a critical point". NDCG@k is analysed separately — [Wang et al. 2013 (COLT)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.6480)
- ERR is built on the cascade user model: at rank i the user is satisfied with probability c_i (a function of graded relevance) and otherwise continues. ERR is the expected reciprocal rank at which the user stops, so the continuation probability depends on the relevance of documents above, unlike RBP's constant patience — [Chapelle, Metzler, Zhang & Grinspan 2009 (CIKM)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1645953.1646033) (abstract and secondary description)
- Measurement theory: most IR measures are not interval-scaled, so averages and many significance tests are not "meaningful" in the representational sense. The recall base and run length also complicate cross-topic aggregation. After transforming P, R, AP, nDCG, RBP and RR to interval scales, about 25% of significance decisions changed — [Ferrante, Ferro & Fuhr 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02668)

**Statistical significance.**
- The randomisation, bootstrap and paired t tests showed "little practical difference". The Wilcoxon and sign tests have poor power and can give false detections, and the authors recommend discontinuing them for comparing means — [Smucker, Allan & Carterette 2007 (CIKM)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1321440.1321528) (abstract via search)
- A simulation over 500M p-values from TREC data, with known null hypotheses, measured actual Type I/II/III error rates across tests, measures and topic-set sizes — [Urbano, Lima & Hanjalic 2019 (SIGIR)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11096)

**Nugget-based evaluation.**
- TREC 2024 RAG's AutoNuggetizer uses LLMs to create nuggets and assign them to answers. Across 21 topics and 45 runs it showed "strong correlation" between fully automatic and (mostly) manual nugget scores — [Pradeep et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09607). The follow-up confirms strong *run-level* agreement, with "robust per-topic agreement" still unresolved — [Pradeep et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15068)
- α-nDCG is the ranking-metric form of nugget evaluation, with redundancy-discounted nugget gain; see Section 1 — [Clarke et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1145/1390334.1390446)

**Validity of LLM judges.**
- At Bing, LLM relevance labels had "accuracy as good as human labellers" and similar ability to pick the hardest queries and best runs. "Simple paraphrases" of prompts changed accuracy — [Thomas, Spielman, Craswell & Mitra 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10621)
- Faggioli et al. lay out a human–machine collaboration spectrum and give arguments for and against fully automatic judgments — [Faggioli et al. 2023 (ICTIR)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09161). Soboroff (NIST): "don't use LLMs to create relevance judgments for TREC-style evaluations" — [Soboroff 2025 (IRRJ)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15133)
- Clarke & Dietz submitted a system crafted to exploit an LLM-based metric and obtained inflated scores. Simulating all systems using the judge (UMBRELA) as their final re-ranker distorted Kendall's τ, an instance of circularity — [Clarke & Dietz 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17156). When prompt templates or gold nuggets leak, "near-perfect evaluation scores can be achieved" — [Dietz et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13227)
- LLMs label passages relevant more often than humans and are "highly influenced by the presence of query words in the passages", even irrelevant ones. Prompt-injection instructions also sway some models — [Alaofi, Thomas, Scholer & Sanderson 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17969)
- Prediction-powered inference gives "provably valid confidence intervals" for means and other estimands by combining a small gold-labelled set with many ML predictions, "without making any assumptions on the machine-learning algorithm". More accurate predictions mean smaller intervals — [Angelopoulos et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09633)

### Inferences
- **[J] Golden-set construction must pool from several retrievers.** Buckley et al.'s title-word bias means a golden set whose candidates were found by BM25 (or by an LLM prompted with the query) will favour lexical matches and over-credit BM25 and query-term-stuffed chunks, the same failure Alaofi et al. found in LLM judges. The KB golden set should pool BM25, dense, alias-expanded and browse-found units, and record which pool found each gold unit. My inference.
- **[J] PPI/ARES for judge calibration** (SKB). PPI's validity theorem is what makes "150–300 human labels + LLM labels" defensible. The intervals are valid regardless of judge quality, and judge quality only shrinks them. My inference connecting ARES's use of PPI to the theorem.
- **[J/B] Report system-level conclusions and keep judges out of the system.** LLM-judge agreement is established at run level, not per topic. Using the same LLM as reranker and judge creates measured circularity (Clarke & Dietz), so the toolkit's evaluation gate should use a different judge model from any reranker and blind it to nugget templates. My inference.
- **[J] Paired t or randomization test for A/B gates.** For "does enrichment X beat the free prefix" decisions, use per-query paired t, bootstrap or randomization tests, not Wilcoxon or sign tests. Consider interval-scale transforms when averaging nDCG across heterogeneous query types. My inference.
- **[B] Metric choice by agent behaviour.** An agent that reads the top 5 and stops behaves like a cascade or RBP user (DeepTh matters). A report-writing agent that wants coverage needs nugget or α-nDCG metrics (Redundancy, AspDiv). nDCG@10 alone satisfies neither redundancy constraint. My inference from Amigó's table.
- **[B] Golden sets expire.** Parry et al. support DRC's versioned golden set with "Stale" marking. Once systems reach the assessors' own effectiveness, re-annotation is needed. My inference.

## 7. Query expansion theory: Rocchio, relevance feedback, relevance models, PRF risk, and LLM expansion (HyDE, query2doc)

### Takeaway
Expansion is formally an estimate of the relevance model P(w|R) or of the optimal query vector. Rocchio is the vector-space optimum for separating relevant from non-relevant centroids. RSJ reweighting is the probabilistic version. Lavrenko & Croft estimate P(w|R) from the query alone via co-occurrence in corpus document models. Pseudo-relevance feedback is the greedy optimiser of a perfect-precision objective, and it carries measurable downside risk that robust optimisation can bound. LLM expansion (HyDE, query2doc) replaces corpus-grounded estimation with model knowledge, and it fails exactly where that knowledge is missing (unfamiliar or ambiguous queries). That is the theoretical case for a curated glossary/alias table as a low-variance, corpus-grounded relevance model.

### Cited Findings
- **Rocchio:** Q_m = a·Q_o + b·(1/|D_r|) Σ_{D_j∈D_r} D_j − c·(1/|D_nr|) Σ_{D_k∈D_nr} D_k, traditionally with a = 1, b = 0.8, c = 0.1, and "modern systems often set c = 0" — [Wikipedia: Rocchio algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocchio_algorithm). Optimal-query derivation (my sketch): for unit-normalised documents, the objective over unit q of (1/|R|)Σ_{d∈R}⟨q,d⟩ − (1/|NR|)Σ_{d∈NR}⟨q,d⟩ = ⟨q, μ_R − μ_NR⟩ is maximised by q* = (μ_R − μ_NR)/‖μ_R − μ_NR‖ (Cauchy–Schwarz). Rocchio's update interpolates the original query toward q*, and a > 0 anchors against drift.
- **Probabilistic feedback:** with judged relevant documents, terms are reweighted by RSJ (complement method for non-relevance) and candidate expansion terms ranked by an offer weight ∝ r_i·w_i^RSJ. The authors note expansion "may worsen the term independence assumption" — [Robertson & Zaragoza 2009 §3.2](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)
- **Relevance models:** assume an underlying relevance model R generates both the relevant documents and the query. Estimate P(w|R) ≈ P(w | q_1…q_k) = P(w, q_1…q_k)/P(q_1…q_k). Method 1 (i.i.d. sampling) gives P(w, q_1…q_k) = Σ_{M∈𝓜} P(M) P(w|M) Π_i P(q_i|M) over document language models M. Method 2 (conditional sampling) samples each query word from its own model and was "less sensitive to the choice of our universe of distributions" and closer in cross-entropy to the true relevance model. The paper claims the technique addresses "synonymy and polysemy" and gives "an effective formal method for estimating a relevance model with no training data" — [Lavrenko & Croft 2001 (SIGIR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/383952.383972)
- **PRF as greedy optimisation:** blind positive relevance feedback "arises naturally as a greedy heuristic" for the perfect-precision (n-call) objective — [Chen & Karger 2006](https://doi.org/10.1145/1148170.1148245)
- **Risk of expansion:** Collins-Thompson casts expansion as robust constrained optimisation over uncertainty sets of the feedback data and introduces risk–reward curves. Applied after an existing expansion method, it gave "fewer and less severe expansion failures without sacrificing average gains" — [Collins-Thompson 2009 (CIKM)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1645953.1646059) (abstract via search)
- **HyDE:** an instruction-following LLM writes a hypothetical document, which a contrastive encoder embeds, and nearest real documents are retrieved. The authors claim the "dense bottleneck" filters out false details. It beat Contriever and approached fine-tuned retrievers zero-shot — [Gao, Ma, Lin & Callan 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10496)
- **query2doc:** few-shot LLM pseudo-documents appended to the query boost BM25 by 3–15% on MS MARCO/TREC DL and help dense retrievers in- and out-of-domain — [Wang, Yang & Wei 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07678)
- **When LLM expansion fails:** when the LLM lacks knowledge of the query, expansions are incorrect. When the query is ambiguous, refinements are biased and narrow coverage. Both degrade sparse and dense retrieval — [Abe et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12694)

### Inferences
- **[J] Glossary/alias expansion as a curated relevance model** (SKB). A SKOS concept's prefLabel/altLabel/hiddenLabel set is a hand-verified estimate of the words a relevant unit uses for the query concept, i.e. a sparse P(w|R) with near-zero variance. It also covers the domain-jargon and acronym cases where Abe et al. show LLM expansion fails. This explains why thesaurus-guided expansion beat LLM-only expansion on hallucination (BMQExpander in SKB). My inference.
- **[J] Interpolate, don't replace.** Rocchio's a·Q_o term, relevance-model interpolation and Collins-Thompson's risk control all say the same thing: keep the original query with substantial weight and add expansions at lower weight. That suggests aliases go in as a separate, down-weighted field or sub-query, not as replacements. My inference.
- **[B] HyDE is a relevance model whose "documents" are LLM generations.** Lavrenko's estimator grounds P(w|R) in the KB's own document models, while HyDE grounds only at the final nearest-neighbour step. For enterprise vocabularies outside the LLM's knowledge, the generated document drifts, and HyDE should be gated on the query containing no unknown identifiers or aliases. My inference.
- **[J] PRF over the KB itself (RM3-style) is a cheap, deterministic, LLM-free expansion.** Given frozen BM25 statistics and deterministic top-k, it is deterministic, which fits DRC's determinism ladder better than LLM expansion. My inference. The Collins-Thompson risk curve is the right evaluation for it.

- No theoretical analysis of HyDE-style generative expansion (e.g. bounds on drift) was found.

## 8. Inverted-index compression bounds: Elias–Fano, entropy, and why lexical indexes scale cheaply

### Takeaway
A posting list is a monotone integer sequence. Its information-theoretic minimum is log₂ C(U,n) ≈ n·log₂(U/n) + 1.443n bits. Elias–Fano uses at most 2 + ⌈log₂(U/n)⌉ bits per posting, within half a bit of that bound, and supports constant-time-expected random access and NextGEQ. Real lists are clustered, so the best codes beat the random-sequence bound, reaching about 3–5.5 bits per docid on web collections. Lexical indexes therefore cost a few bits per posting, against tens of thousands of bits per dense vector. That gap is the formal reason the lexical channel is the cheapest to keep.

### Cited Findings
- **Elias–Fano space:** for n increasing integers in universe u, store the lower ℓ = max{0, ⌊log(u/n)⌋} bits of each explicitly and the upper bits in unary-coded buckets, for "at most 2 + ⌈log(u/n)⌉ bits per element". Because the information-theoretic lower bound for a monotone list is about n·log(u/n) + 1.44n, the representation is "quasi-succinct". The index offers expected constant-time operations and faster conjunctive, phrase and proximity queries in practice — [Vigna 2013 (WSDM)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4300); original: [Elias 1974 (JACM)](https://doi.org/10.1145/321812.321820)
- **Lower bound and gap to it:** log₂ C(U,n) = n log₂(eU/n) − Θ(n²/U) − O(log n), approximately n(log₂(U/n) + 1.443) bits for n = o(√U). Elias–Fano with ℓ = ⌈log₂(U/n)⌉ "wastes less than half a bit per element". Because real inverted lists contain "clusters of close integers", list compressors "often take less space" than this random-model bound. Docid assignment by URL order creates such clusters — [Pibiri & Venturini 2021 (ACM CSUR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3415148)
- **Measured costs (docids, lists > 4096 postings):** partitioned Elias–Fano (PEF) used 3.12, 4.99 and 5.45 bits/int on Gov2, ClueWeb09 and CCNews, decoding at 0.76–1.31 ns/int. BIC was smallest (2.94, 4.43, 5.24) but about 5–7 ns/int, and VByte about 8.8–9.3 bits/int. "Both methods [BIC, PEF] come very close to the entropy of gaps" — [Pibiri & Venturini 2021, Table 11](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10598)
- **Systems context:** the inverted-file family of representations underlies innovations in index storage, construction and query evaluation, surveyed as a tutorial with a core implementation and extensions — [Zobel & Moffat 2006 (ACM CSUR)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1132956.1132959)

### Inferences
- **[J] "Lexical indexes scale cheaply"** (SKB/ADL). By arithmetic (mine), a 768-dimensional float32 embedding is 24,576 bits per chunk (6,144 bits at int8). A 300-token chunk with about 150 distinct terms costs about 150 postings × roughly 4–6 bits ≈ 600–900 bits of docids (plus term frequencies, not counted here). The lexical index is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than an int8 dense index and decodes at about 1 ns per posting. This is my estimate combining Pibiri & Venturini's bits/int with assumed chunk statistics, and it should be benchmarked on the actual KB.
- **[J] Assign unit IDs in document/heading-tree order.** The compression results depend on docid clustering. Assigning chunk IDs contiguously within a document and section (the persisted document tree in SKB) makes each term's postings cluster, the analogue of URL ordering. It also makes snapshot diffs local. My inference.
- **[J] Exactness plus compression enable the determinism rungs.** Inverted-index term match is an exact set operation on compressed monotone sequences (NextGEQ intersections), so DRC's "inverted-index term match" rung is both exact and cheap at scale. ANN has neither property in the worst case (Section 5). My inference.
- **[B] Learned sparse indexes lose part of this advantage.** SPLADE-style expansion adds many postings per document and "wacky" weights that defeat skipping (Section 3), so the cheap-scaling argument applies most strongly to BM25-style term weights. My inference.

### Gaps
- No source measured posting-list compression on chunk-level enterprise KB indexes (short units, heavy identifier vocabulary). The bits/int figures are from web/news collections with lists longer than 4096 postings.
- Term-frequency and position compression costs (needed for BM25 and phrase queries) were not extracted. Only docid figures are cited.
- Ottaviano & Venturini 2014 (partitioned Elias–Fano) was identified but not read directly. PEF figures come from the Pibiri & Venturini survey.
