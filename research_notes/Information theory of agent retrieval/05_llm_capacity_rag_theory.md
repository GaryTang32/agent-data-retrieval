# Theory of LLM knowledge capacity, hallucination, in-context use of evidence and retrieval augmentation

Scope: formal results (theorem, assumptions, proof sketch or proof location) about why a language model needs an external knowledge store and what limits retrieval-augmented use of that store. Each entry is mapped to a method in the earlier reports:
- **[DET]** `reports/Deterministic retrieval data conversion.md`: LLMs are non-deterministic, so extract once, persist with provenance, query exact snapshots, have agents choose parameterized queries rather than write them, and gate quality.
- **[KB]** `reports/Searchable agent knowledge base construction.md`: external typed knowledge units; stale, duplicated or conflicting passages hurt more than weak retrieval; superseded content is filtered out, not ranked down; long context is a working set, not the store; short retrieval loops (two rounds capture most of the gain); abstain when evidence is missing; golden-set evaluation.
- **[LAKE]** `reports/Agent ready data lake toolkit.md`: typed records, versions, edges and permissions as the engineered context; typed graph or SQL tools instead of free-form generation; multi-stage retrieval.

Notation: N = number of training examples, B = number of bits, n = context length or number of retrieved passages, d = embedding dimension, p = precision in bits, ε = per-step error. "**Inference:**" marks a mapping that is my reasoning rather than a claim made by the cited authors. Entries are labelled "peer-reviewed" when the arXiv record or paper states a venue, and "preprint" otherwise.

**Summary map (detail and citations in the sections below):**

## Q1. Hallucination lower bounds: what they prove, what they assume, and why rare facts must come from retrieval

### Takeaway
Three independent formal lines show that a stand-alone LM must err: statistical (calibration plus Good–Turing missing mass), learning-theoretic (generation or detection in the limit) and computability (diagonalization). The statistical bounds are the only quantitative ones. They tie the hallucination rate on "arbitrary" facts to the fraction of facts seen exactly once in training. Every line names its own escape, which is information about the ground truth from outside the training sample: a fact database, negative or labelled examples, or abstention under a scoring rule that rewards it. That is exactly what [KB]'s external store, abstention and golden-set gates supply.

### Cited Findings

**1. Kalai & Vempala, "Calibrated Language Models Must Hallucinate" (STOC 2024; peer-reviewed).**
- *Setting and assumptions.* Training documents are i.i.d. from a world distribution over "factoids" Y = F ∪ H (facts and hallucinations). Training data are error-free, and each document holds at most one factoid. The generative LM g is *calibrated* in a binned sense: Mis_b(g,p) = ‖p^{V_b(g)} − g‖_TV, where p^{V_b(g)} coarsens the true distribution onto g's probability bins. Facts are *s-sparse*, |F| ≤ e^{−s}|H|. The world is *regular*: unobserved factoids are on average equally likely to be true, with a bounded-ratio "semi-regular" relaxation allowed. — [Kalai & Vempala](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14648)
- *Corollary 1.* For any algorithm, with probability ≥ 1 − δ over the training set: g(H) ≥ \hat{MF} − Mis_b(g,p) − 3e^{−s}/δ − sqrt(6 ln(6/δ)/n). Here g(H) is the hallucination rate and \hat{MF} is the "monofact" estimate, the fraction of facts appearing exactly once in training (Good–Turing). The introduction gives a simplified form: Hallucination rate ≥ MF − Miscalibration − 300|Facts|/|Possible hallucinations| − 7/√n, with probability ≥ 99%. — [Kalai & Vempala](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14648)
- *Proof sketch (Sec. 5.2, 6, 8).* (i) The analysis samples x_train first, then p from the posterior ν given x_train. (ii) Theorem 1: E_{p∼ν}[(p(U) − ‖p^Π − g‖_TV − g(H))_+] ≤ max_{y∈U} Pr_ν[y ∈ F] + |O|·max_{y∈U} E_ν[p(y)], where O is the set of observed facts and U = Y∖O. In words, calibration forces g to place roughly the unobserved mass p(U) on unobserved factoids, and regularity plus sparsity make almost all of them hallucinations. (iii) Good–Turing concentration gives |\hat{MF} − p(U)| = Õ(1/√n) (Appendix A). (iv) Markov's inequality turns the expectation into a high-probability statement. Section 7 gives a matching upper bound: one cannot guarantee a hallucination rate above the monofact estimate for nearly calibrated models. — [Kalai & Vempala](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14648)
- *Reach, as the authors state it.* The bound applies to "arbitrary" facts whose truth cannot be inferred from the training data. It does not apply to systematic facts such as arithmetic, or to facts that recur many times, such as most citations of published papers ("no statistical reason that pretraining will lead to hallucination" on those). The authors write that the analysis "justifies the mitigation of consulting a fact database at generation time, even if that fact database is constructed solely from training data (Borgeaud et al., 2022)". — [Kalai & Vempala](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14648)
- The monofact quantity is Turing's missing-mass estimator from Good (1953): the probability of unseen species is estimated by the fraction of the sample made up of species seen exactly once. — [Good 1953, Biometrika 40(3–4):237–264](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/40.3-4.237)

**2. Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate" (2025; preprint, OpenAI and Georgia Tech).**
- *Reduction to binary classification ("Is-It-Valid", IIV).* Plausible strings X = E ∪ V (errors and valid strings), with noiseless training p(E) = 0. Define the IIV distribution D as a 50/50 mix of p and uniform errors, and the classifier \hat f(x) = + iff \hat p(x) > 1/|E|. Corollary 1: err ≥ 2·err_iiv − |V|/|E| − δ, with δ = |\hat p(A) − p(A)| for A = {x : \hat p(x) > 1/|E|} (a calibration gap). Theorem 1 extends this to prompts: err ≥ 2·err_iiv − max_c|V_c|/min_c|E_c| − δ. The consequence: if valid and invalid strings cannot be told apart, a calibrated base model *must* generate errors. — [Kalai et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)
- *Theorem 2 (Arbitrary Facts, with an "IDK" response).* For each prompt c, one correct answer a_c is drawn uniformly from R_c; p(a_c|c) = α_c and p(IDK|c) = 1 − α_c. Let sr be the fraction of prompts seen exactly once, not counting abstentions. With probability ≥ 99%, err ≥ sr − 2/min_c|E_c| − (35 + 6 ln N)/√N − δ. The bound is nearly tight: an efficient calibrated algorithm achieves err ≤ sr − sr/(max_c|E_c|+1) + 13/√N. — [Kalai et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)
- *Theorem 3 (poor models).* In pure multiple choice with C choices, err ≥ 2(1 − 1/C)·opt(G), where opt(G) is the best error achievable in the model class. Corollary 2: any trigram model errs at a rate of at least 1/2 on a two-prompt example. — [Kalai et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)
- *Observation 1 (evaluation incentives).* If a grader is binary, giving 1 for correct and 0 for both wrong answers and abstentions, then for any belief ρ_c over graders the optimal response is never an abstention: A_c ∩ argmax_r E_{g∼ρ_c}[g(r)] = ∅. Proof: abstaining scores 0 with certainty, and any guess has expected score ≥ 0. The authors note that this "holds for arbitrary language models, including those with RAG … the binary grading system itself still rewards guessing whenever search fails to yield a confident answer." — [Kalai et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)
- *Proposed fix (explicit confidence targets).* The prompt states: "Answer only if you are > t confident, since mistakes are penalized t/(1 − t) points, while correct answers receive 1 point, and an answer of 'I don't know' receives 0 points." A model whose correctness probability is q then has expected score q − (1 − q)·t/(1 − t), which is positive iff q > t. One behaviour, answering exactly when q > t ("behavioral calibration"), is then optimal for every t at once. — [Kalai et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)
- *Reported numbers.* On SimpleQA, OpenAI's post reports gpt-5-thinking-mini at 52% abstention, 22% accuracy and 26% error, versus o4-mini at 1% abstention, 24% accuracy and 75% error. These figures are vendor-reported and came from a search snippet of the post; the page itself returned HTTP 403 to my fetch. — [OpenAI blog, Sep 2025](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)

**3. Computability-style inevitability (diagonalization).**
- Xu, Jain & Kankanhalli (2024; preprint). In a "formal world", an LLM h is a total computable function, and the ground truth f : S → S is computable. Hallucination means that for every training state i there is an s with h^{[i]}(s) ≠ f(s). Theorem 1: for every computably enumerable set of LLMs there is a computable f on which all of them, in all states, hallucinate. Theorem 2: they do so on infinitely many inputs. Theorem 3 applies this to any single computable LLM. Corollary 1: "All computable LLMs cannot prevent themselves from hallucinating." Proof: enumerate all (model, state) pairs with Cantor pairing and define f(s_k) to differ from \hat h_k(s_k), as in Table 1. — [Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11817)
- *The authors' own escape clause.* For "knowledge-enhanced LLMs" that retrieve from knowledge graphs or databases, "LLMs receive extra information about the ground truth function f other than via training samples. Therefore, Theorem 3 is inapplicable herein." Scalability is left open. — [Xu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11817)
- Banerjee, Agarwal & Singla (2024; preprint) argue from Gödel incompleteness and the undecidability of the Halting, Emptiness and Acceptance problems. Their claim is that every pipeline stage (training data, retrieval, intent classification, generation) has non-zero hallucination probability ("structural hallucination"). The paper is argued rather than proved with the precision of the two papers above. — [Banerjee et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05746)
- Suzuki, He & Tian (2025; preprint) give a counterweight. Their Theorem 8 is a strengthened diagonal result: there exists an acceptable-output map F_0 on which *every* LM hallucinates on infinitely many inputs, proved without the axiom of choice. They then prove that the *hallucination probability* HP_μ(h) = Pr_{S∼μ}(h(S) ∉ F_0(S)) can be made arbitrarily small given training data of sufficient quality and quantity. They argue that "innate" computability results "cannot explain practical issues of LLMs". — [Suzuki et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12187)
- Lower-confidence additions (all preprints with no stated venue): Xi et al. model RAG as an oracle machine and claim an "absolute escape" from inevitability ([Xi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.07334); 8 pages). Karpowicz proves an impossibility theorem for aggregating component "reports" truthfully; the arXiv record lists eight versions with "mathematics debugged" ([Karpowicz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06382)). Bowen Xu argues inevitability under an open-world assumption ([Xu B.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05116)).

**4. Learning-in-the-limit results on generation and detection.**
- Kleinberg & Mullainathan (2024). An adversary enumerates strings of an unknown language L from a countable candidate list. An agent can learn to *generate*, eventually producing only new valid strings, for every countable list. This contrasts with Gold-style impossibility of *identifying* L. — [Kleinberg & Mullainathan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06757)
- Kalavasis, Mehrotra & Velegkas (STOC 2025). Consistent generation *with breadth* (no hallucination and no mode collapse) is impossible for a large class of models, including next-token predictors, for most candidate collections. It becomes achievable for every countable collection "when negative examples (strings outside K) are available". — [Kalavasis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09642)
- Karbasi, Montasser, Sous et al. (2025; preprint). Theorem 2.1: a countable collection admits hallucination detection in the limit iff it is identifiable in the limit (Angluin's condition), so detection from positive examples alone is "fundamentally impossible for most language collections". Theorem 2.3: with expert-labelled negative examples, detection is possible for *every* countable collection. — [Karbasi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17004)
- Ganju et al. (2026; preprint): "timely" generation is impossible for eventually consistent generators, and possible if a vanishing hallucination rate is tolerated. — [Ganju et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11302)

**5. Empirical companions consistent with the frequency mechanism.**
- QA accuracy correlates, both correlationally and causally, with the number of pre-training documents about the question's entities. "Today's models must be scaled by many orders of magnitude" to do well on low-support questions, while "retrieval-augmentation can reduce the dependence on relevant pre-training information". — [Kandpal et al., ICML 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08411)
- On PopQA (14k questions), "scaling fails to appreciably improve memorization of factual knowledge in the long tail". Retrieval-augmented LMs beat models orders of magnitude larger, and adaptive retrieval ("only when necessary") saves cost. — [Mallen et al., ACL 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511)
- On Head-to-Tail (18K QA pairs across 16 LLMs), models are "far from being perfect … especially for facts of torso-to-tail entities". — [Sun et al., NAACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10168)
- Fine-tuning examples that introduce new knowledge are learned slowly, and once learned they "linearly increase the model's tendency to hallucinate". — [Gekhman et al., EMNLP 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05904). On synthetic recall tasks, "hallucinations emerge simultaneously with knowledge", and fine-tuning new knowledge "quickly corrupts its existing parametric memories". — [Zucchet et al., COLM 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.21676)
- Larger models are well calibrated on multiple-choice formats. P(IK) ("I know") is predictable, and it "increase[s] appropriately in the presence of relevant source materials in the context", but calibration of P(IK) on new tasks is weaker. — [Kadavath et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221)

### Inferences
- **Inference: long-tail enterprise facts must come from the store [KB].** Most enterprise facts (a contract's renewal date, a policy clause's effective date, a part number) are "arbitrary facts" in Kalai et al.'s sense, seen once or never in pre-training. For that class, Theorem 2 makes sr ≈ 1 and thus err ≈ 1 for a calibrated base model answering closed-book. Kalai & Vempala explicitly name a fact database as the mitigation. This is the strongest formal basis for [KB]'s "external typed knowledge units" and for [DET]'s rule that the database, not the model, is the source of truth.
- **Inference: abstention needs an explicit score in the golden set [KB, evaluation].** Observation 1 says that a golden-set metric of plain accuracy pushes an agent to guess even when retrieval came back empty or contradictory. The toolkit's evaluation gate should score with an explicit penalty t/(1 − t) and give credit for "evidence not found". It should report accuracy, error and abstention separately, as OpenAI's SimpleQA table does. This turns [KB]'s "abstain when evidence missing" from a style rule into the optimal policy under the stated metric.
- **Inference: the computability escape is conditional on the store being right.** Xu et al.'s "inapplicable" only means the diagonal argument no longer bites. If the store itself holds a stale or wrong value, the hallucination has moved into the data layer. That is why [KB]'s validity filtering and [DET]'s quality gates are not optional add-ons to RAG.
- **Inference: golden sets need labelled incorrect statements.** Karbasi et al. and Kalavasis et al. both show that negative (labelled-incorrect) examples are what make detection or breadth-with-consistency possible. A golden set should therefore contain known-false and known-superseded statements, not only correct Q/A pairs. [KB]'s "applicability probes" are the natural place for them.
- **Inference: probabilities, not worst cases, are the operating quantity.** Suzuki et al.'s positive result says that what the toolkit can move is hallucination *probability under the query distribution*. Coverage of the store on the queries actually asked is that lever.

### Gaps
- No theorem covers a RAG system end to end with a retriever error rate, for example a bound on err as a function of the singleton rate *and* retrieval recall. Kalai et al. only remark that Observation 1 still holds with RAG.
- The bounds assume error-free training data and one canonical phrasing per fact. The authors state that noisy data and multiple phrasings should only raise error. There is no quantitative version for enterprise corpora containing conflicting versions.
- I could not retrieve OpenAI's blog page directly (HTTP 403); the SimpleQA numbers come from a search-result summary of that page.

## Q2. Knowledge capacity: parametric memory is bounded, extraction is fragile, so an external store is required

### Takeaway
Capacity results agree on order of magnitude across methods: about 2 bits per parameter for extractable random-tuple knowledge (Allen-Zhu & Li) and about 3.6 bits per parameter for raw memorization (Morris et al.). An information-theoretic lower bound (Nichani, Lee & Bietti, Theorem 7) shows that a B-bit model must lose about log M nats on every fact beyond its top ~B/log M most frequent ones. Separately, stored knowledge may be unextractable (Physics 3.1) or unusable for inverse lookups and comparisons (Physics 3.2, reversal curse). These are formal and controlled-experimental reasons why a corpus-specific, long-tail, frequently updated fact base belongs in an external store with explicit indexes.

### Cited Findings

**1. Information-theoretic lower bound for associative-memory storage (Nichani, Lee & Bietti, 2024; ICLR 2025).**
- *Theorems 1–2 (constructions).* Linear associative memory: if d² ≳ N polylog N, there exists W with argmax_y u_y^T W e_x = f*(x) for all N keys. MLP associative memory: if md ≳ N polylog N, then V, W exist with the same property. Storage is therefore (nearly) linear in parameter count. Theorems 3–4 extend this to a one-layer transformer on a synthetic factual-recall task, which reaches 100% accuracy when either the attention or the MLP parameter count scales linearly in the number of facts, up to logarithmic factors. — [Nichani et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06538)
- *Theorem 7 (lower bound).* Outputs f*(x) are i.i.d. uniform on [M], the learner writes a B-bit model F, and keys are queried with frequencies p(x) sorted in decreasing order. Then E[L(\hat f)] ≥ log M · Σ_{x ≥ ⌈B/log M⌉} p(x). Zero loss therefore requires B ≥ N log M bits. Corollary 1: if p(x) ∝ x^{−α} with α > 1 (a power law), then E[L] ≳ B^{1−α}. — [Nichani et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06538)
- *Proof sketch (Appendix E.1).* f* → F → \hat f is a Markov chain, so by the data-processing inequality I(f*; \hat f) ≤ I(f*; F) ≤ H(F) ≤ B. Each key whose answer is not determined by F costs log M in expected cross-entropy. The learner minimizes loss by spending its B bits on the ~B/log M most frequent keys, which leaves the tail mass Σ_{x > B/log M} p(x) at full loss. — [Nichani et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06538)
- Related: Cabannes, Dohmatob & Bietti derive scaling laws in sample size and parameter count for outer-product associative memories, the model of transformer inner layers ([Cabannes et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02984)). Bietti et al. treat weight matrices as associative memories: global bigrams are learned fast, and the in-context induction-head mechanism develops more slowly ([Bietti et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00802)). Geva et al. show empirically that FFN layers act as key–value memories ([Geva et al., EMNLP 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14913)).

**2. Measured capacity: about 2 bits per parameter (Allen-Zhu & Li, Physics of LMs Part 3.3; ICLR 2025).**
- *Definition.* Knowledge consists of N synthetic biographies holding K attributes with random values. The bit-complexity lower bound (Theorem 3.2) gives the minimum bits a model must hold to reach its measured loss, roughly N log2(N_0/(e·loss_name)) + NK log2(D^C/(e·loss_value)) + KD log2(…), and it is "nearly as precise as the upper bound, within a 1 − o(1) factor". Capacity ratio = bits ÷ parameters. — [Allen-Zhu & Li 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405)
- *Results.* Result 1: after 1000 exposures per fact, GPT-2 models from 1M to 0.5B parameters reach a peak ratio of R ≥ 2 and never exceed 2.3 bits/param. Result 4: at 100 exposures the ratio is still ≥ 1, a loss of at most 2×. Result 8: int8 quantization costs a negligible amount, and int4 costs more than 2×. Result 9: MoE is nearly fully efficient. Result 10: when 7/8 of tokens are junk, capacity for the useful data degrades by up to 20× at 100 exposures. Result 12: prepending a domain token (for example "wikipedia.org") to useful data largely recovers it. The paper extrapolates that a 7B model can hold 14B bits, "surpassing the English Wikipedia and textbooks combined" by its estimate. — [Allen-Zhu & Li 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405)
- *Assumptions limiting reach.* The data are synthetic and uniformly random (maximally incompressible), and every fact is presented 100–1000 times. The paper does not measure facts seen once. — [Allen-Zhu & Li 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405)
- *Independent estimate.* Morris et al. separate "unintended memorization" from generalization using a Kolmogorov-complexity-style definition. They estimate that GPT-style models hold about 3.6 bits per parameter; models "memorize until their capacity fills", after which grokking begins. They trained models from 500K to 1.5B parameters. — [Morris et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24832). The 2 vs 3.6 difference reflects different definitions (extractable attribute knowledge vs total memorized bits of random data). Both are O(1) bits per parameter.
- *Fact-level scaling law.* Fact capacity is linear in model size. "Memorizing the whole Wikidata's facts requires training an LLM with 1000B non-embed parameters for 100 epochs". Later facts can overwrite earlier ones, which "significantly hinders low-frequency facts memorization". — [Lu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15720)
- *Parameter-count memorization bounds* (these count parameters, not bits). An attention layer with H heads and Θ(Hd²) parameters can memorize Ω(Hn) examples under linear-independence assumptions ([Mahdavi et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02010)). Transformers can memorize N labels with Õ(√N) parameters in next-token prediction, optimal up to logarithmic factors ([Kajitsuka & Sato, ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17677)). **Inference:** sublinear parameter counts are possible because each parameter can carry many bits. Bit-level bounds (Theorem 7 above) are the relevant constraint for real fixed-precision models.

**3. Prediction equals compression (Delétang et al., 2023; ICLR 2024).**
- Arithmetic coding turns any predictor p into a lossless code of length about −log2 p(x) bits, and conversely. LLMs are strong general compressors: Chinchilla 70B compresses ImageNet patches to 43.4% and LibriSpeech to 16.4% of raw size, beating PNG (58.5%) and FLAC (30.3%). The compression view "provides novel insights into scaling laws, tokenization, and in-context learning". — [Delétang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10668)
- **Inference:** the model's weights are a finite code for the training distribution, so the B-bit bound of item 1 applies to the code itself. Generating from it is sampling, not lossless decoding of individual records. Exact field values need a lossless store, which is [DET]'s "extract once, store exactly".

**4. Storage is not extraction or manipulation (Physics of LMs 3.1 and 3.2; reversal curse).**
- Part 3.1 (ICML 2024): knowledge is reliably extractable by QA only if it was "sufficiently augmented (e.g., through paraphrasing, sentence shuffling, translations) during pretraining". Otherwise it "may be memorized but not extractable, leading to 0% accuracy, regardless of subsequent instruction fine-tuning". Linear probing ties extractability to whether the attribute is linearly encoded on the entity name's hidden states. — [Allen-Zhu & Li 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14316)
- Part 3.2 (ICLR 2025): models "excel in knowledge retrieval but struggle even in the simplest classification or comparison tasks unless Chain of Thoughts (CoTs) are employed during both training and inference", and "inverse knowledge search is virtually 0%, regardless of the prompts". The paper argues these are inherent limits even when knowledge is perfectly stored. — [Allen-Zhu & Li 3.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14402)
- Reversal curse (ICLR 2024): training on "A is B" does not make the model assign "B is A" a higher likelihood than a random name. GPT-4 answers celebrity → parent questions 79% of the time but parent → celebrity questions 33% of the time. "If 'A is B' appears in-context, models can deduce the reverse relationship." — [Berglund et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12288)

**5. Retrieval substitutes for parameters (empirical).**
- RETRO with a 2-trillion-token database matches GPT-3 and Jurassic-1 on the Pile "despite using 25× fewer parameters" ([Borgeaud et al., ICML 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04426)). Atlas reaches 42% on Natural Questions with 64 examples, beating a 540B model by 3 points "despite having 50x fewer parameters", and its index "can easily be updated" ([Izacard et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.03299)).
- REALM motivates retrieval because parametric knowledge requires "ever-larger networks to cover more facts" ([Guu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)). RAG (NeurIPS 2020) adds provenance and updatability as reasons ([Lewis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)).
- kNN-LM interpolates p = λ·p_kNN + (1 − λ)·p_LM over a datastore and is "particularly helpful in predicting rare patterns, such as factual knowledge" ([Khandelwal et al., ICLR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00172)). An analysis attributes its gains to a different input representation, approximate kNN search and the softmax temperature of the kNN distribution ([Xu, Alon & Neubig](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02828)).
- Counterpoint for head facts: closed-book T5 "performs competitively with open-domain systems that explicitly retrieve" on standard QA ([Roberts et al., EMNLP 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08910)), and BERT holds relational knowledge competitive with some KB baselines ([Petroni et al., EMNLP 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01066)).

### Inferences
- **Inference: Theorem 7 is the core formal argument for [KB]/[LAKE]'s external store.** An enterprise corpus has a heavy-tailed query distribution, and its facts are near-uniform over large value spaces (IDs, dates, amounts), so log M is large. A model with B bits of budget for this corpus, shared with everything else it knows, must lose the tail mass beyond ~B/log M facts. Even the optimistic 2 bits/param figure assumes 100–1000 exposures, which internal documents never get.
- **Inference: fine-tuning facts into weights is the wrong write path [KB lifecycle].** Gekhman et al. and Zucchet et al. show that new facts learned through fine-tuning raise hallucination or corrupt old memories. Lu et al. show that later facts overwrite low-frequency ones. An append-and-invalidate store (Graphiti-style bi-temporal, as recommended in [KB]) has none of these interference effects.
- **Inference: build inverse lookups as indexes, not as model questions [KB alias tables; LAKE typed tools].** Inverse search is ~0% (Physics 3.2) and the reversal curse holds, yet in-context reversal works. The store should therefore expose bidirectional indexes and alias tables (value → entity, alias → canonical ID), and the agent should be given the forward fact in context. This justifies the alias-table-first design in [KB].
- **Inference: Result 12 has a retrieval-side analogue.** Domain tags help the model separate high-value data from junk. Source and authority metadata in each knowledge unit's envelope may play the same role at query time (see Q4, item 4, on priors). This is an analogy, not a proven result.

### Gaps
- No capacity result measures facts seen once or twice. That is the regime of most enterprise documents, and also where Kalai's singleton bound applies.
- The capacity work uses synthetic random tuples. The compressibility of real enterprise facts (correlated fields, templates) is unmeasured, so real "bits per fact" could be lower than log M.

## Q3. In-context learning theory and position effects: why contexts should stay small, reranked and loop-short

### Takeaway
Theory gives two complementary pictures. (a) In-context learning is implicit Bayesian inference or algorithm selection over the prompt. It works when the evidence for the right latent "concept" outweighs the distribution-shift error terms, so mixed or contradictory evidence weakens it. (b) Mechanistically, retrieval from context runs through a sparse set of copy and induction-style "retrieval heads" that use softmax attention. With bounded logits, softmax attention must disperse as Θ(1/n) as context grows, and causal masking creates a positional bias. Single layers provably cannot compose lookups over large domains. Empirics (lost in the middle, NoLiMa, context rot, distractor studies) match these predictions. Together they justify [KB]'s "long context is a working set, not the store", reranked small top-k contexts, and short loops.

### Cited Findings

**1. ICL as implicit Bayesian inference and algorithm selection.**
- *Xie, Raghunathan, Liang & Ma (ICLR 2022).* Pre-training is a mixture of HMMs indexed by a latent concept θ, and ICL means inferring the prompt concept θ*. Assumptions: delimiter hidden states; a prompt start distribution TV-close to the delimiter transitions, with the margin Δ between the top two labels; well-specification θ* ∈ Θ; regularity (transition probabilities bounded below). Condition 1 (distinguishability): for all θ ≠ θ*, Σ_{j=1}^k KL_j(θ*‖θ) > ε_start + ε_delim. Theorem 1: under the assumptions and Condition 1, as the number of examples n → ∞, argmax_y p(y|S_n, x_test) → argmax_y p_prompt(y|x_test), giving Bayes-optimal 0-1 risk. Theorems 2–3 bound the error when distinguishability fails; the bound improves with example length k. Proof idea: the posterior p(θ|S_n) ∝ exp(Σ log-likelihood ratios), so each example adds KL "signal" minus low-probability transition "error", and the posterior concentrates on θ* when the signal dominates. — [Xie et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02080)
- ICL "implicitly implements the Bayesian model averaging algorithm", which attention approximately parameterizes, with an O(1/T) regret bound for perfectly pre-trained ICL ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19420)). A PAC framework shows that for a mixture of latent tasks, ICL "is more about identifying the task than about learning it" ([Wies, Levine & Shashua](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07895)).
- *Transformers as in-context learners of algorithms.* Trained transformers learn linear functions in context with performance comparable to least squares, under some distribution shift ([Garg et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.01066)). A single linear self-attention layer is equivalent to one gradient-descent step on a regression loss, by explicit weight construction ([von Oswald et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07677)). Transformers can implement GD and closed-form ridge regression, and trained models track those predictors ([Akyürek et al., ICLR 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15661)). Transformers can implement least squares, ridge, Lasso, GLMs and GD on two-layer networks "with near-optimal predictive power", plus "in-context algorithm selection" ([Bai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04637)).

**2. Mechanism: induction heads and retrieval heads.**
- Induction heads implement [A][B] … [A] → [B]. They form at the same point in training as a jump in in-context learning, with causal evidence in small attention-only models and correlational evidence in larger ones. — [Olsson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11895)
- Retrieval heads are universal, sparse (<5% of heads), intrinsic (already present in short-context pre-training) and causal: pruning them "leads to failure in retrieving relevant information and results in hallucination". They "strongly influence chain-of-thought". — [Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15574)

**3. Formal limits that predict position and length effects.**
- *Softmax dispersion (Veličković et al., ICML 2025).* Lemma 2.1: if the logits satisfy m ≤ e_k ≤ M, then softmax_θ(e)_k = Θ(1/n) for every item as n → ∞. Proof (one line): exp(e_k/θ)/Σ_j exp(e_j/θ) lies in [e^{(m−M)/θ}/n, e^{(M−m)/θ}/n]. Theorem 2.2: in transformers over a finite vocabulary, logits are bounded in every layer, so all global heads must disperse at large enough sizes. Proposition 3.1: sharpness requires large weights. The proposed remedy is adaptive temperature. — [Veličković et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01104)
- *Attention fading (Nakanishi, 2025; preprint).* The maximum softmax element tends to 0 as input size grows, and the logit gap needed to keep focus "increases indefinitely as n grows". Scalable-Softmax replaces z_i with (s·log n)·z_i to cancel this. **Derivation (mine):** to give one item weight ≥ a against n − 1 items with logit 0, one needs z_max ≥ log(a(n−1)/(1−a)) = Θ(log n). — [Nakanishi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19399)
- *Representational collapse and over-squashing (Barbero et al., 2024).* Theorem 4.2 / B.3: two sequences that differ only by a repeated final token get final-token representations closer than any ε for large n, given positional information that decays with distance. Low-precision floating point exacerbates this. Theorem 5.1 bounds the sensitivity of the last token to earlier tokens, which the authors relate to over-squashing in GNNs. — [Barbero et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04267)
- *Positional bias from the causal mask (Wu, Wang, Jegelka et al., ICML 2025).* This is a graph-theoretic model of multi-layer attention. Causal masking "inherently biases attention toward earlier positions", while RoPE or decay masks add distance decay. Their combined effect trades long-term decay against "the cumulative importance of early sequence positions", and the model reproduces lost-in-the-middle and attention-sink patterns. — [Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01951)
- *Function composition lower bound (Peng, Narayanan & Papadimitriou, 2024).* Theorem 1: a single transformer layer with H heads, embedding dimension d and precision p cannot solve function composition over domains of size n (for example "grandparent of x") when H(d+1)p < n log n. With R = n log n − H(d+1)p > 0, the error probability is at least R/(3n log n). Proof: communication complexity. The query token's output depends on the other tokens only through H softmax aggregates of about (d+1)p bits each, but composition needs about n log n bits about g. Theorem 2: iterated composition needs Ω(√(n/(Hdp))) CoT steps. — [Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08164)
- Hahn (TACL 2020) shows that hard and soft self-attention cannot model periodic finite-state languages or hierarchical structure "unless the number of layers or heads increases with input length" ([Hahn](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06755)). Sanford, Hsu & Telgarsky prove that transformers solve "sparse averaging" with complexity logarithmic in input size, but "triple detection" needs complexity linear in input size, again by communication complexity ([Sanford et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02896)).
- *Softmax bottleneck (Yang et al., ICLR 2018).* A softmax over d-dimensional embeddings yields a log-probability matrix of rank at most about d + 1 over contexts × tokens. If the true matrix has higher rank, some contexts cannot be modelled. — [Yang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.03953)

**4. Empirical position, length and distractor effects consistent with the above.**
- Accuracy is highest when relevant information is at the start or end of the context, and it "significantly degrades" in the middle, even for long-context models. — [Liu et al., TACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172). LLMs show a U-shaped attention bias "regardless of their relevance", and calibrating it improves RAG ([Hsieh et al., ACL Findings 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16008)). Attention "sinks" form on initial tokens ([Xiao et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17453)).
- At 32K tokens, 11 of 13 models fall below 50% of their short-context baselines when the needle and question have minimal lexical overlap, and GPT-4o drops from 99.3% to 69.7%. The authors attribute this to attention difficulty "when literal matches are absent". — [Modarressi et al., ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167)
- Reasoning degrades "at much shorter input lengths than their technical maximum" when padding is added. — [Levy, Jacoby & Goldberg, ACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14848)
- Across 18 models, "performance grows increasingly unreliable as input length grows". "Even a single distractor reduces performance relative to the baseline", and low-similarity needle–question pairs degrade faster. — [Chroma "Context Rot", Hong, Troynikov & Huber, Jul 2025](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot) (vendor technical report)
- Irrelevant context in grade-school math problems "dramatically" lowers accuracy ([Shi et al., ICML 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00093)). High-scoring but non-relevant retrieved documents hurt RAG accuracy, while adding random documents improved accuracy by up to 35% in one setup ([Cuconasu et al., SIGIR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.14887)). The second finding is counter-intuitive, and I did not find theory explaining it.

### Inferences
- **Inference: why [KB] keeps contexts small and reranked.** Dispersion (Θ(1/n) per item unless the logit gap grows as log n) and causal-mask position bias predict that the *same* evidence becomes harder to use as n grows and as it moves to the middle. Reranking that places the best one or two units first and keeps k small acts directly on n and on position. These are the two variables the theorems identify.
- **Inference: near-duplicates and stale versions act like distractors.** In Xie et al.'s Bayesian picture, the posterior concentrates on the concept supported by the most evidence. Evidence-format distractors (a superseded clause, a regional variant) are high-likelihood under a *wrong* concept, which shrinks the KL margin in Condition 1. This is a formal reading of [KB]'s finding that "accuracy decays with competition, not with length", and of the recommendation to collapse duplicates and hard-filter superseded units.
- **Inference: joins belong in the data layer, not in attention [LAKE typed graph tools; DET semantic layer].** Peng et al.'s bound says one layer cannot compose relations over large domains. Multi-hop composition needs many CoT steps or many layers. Pre-computing joins (typed edges, SQL views, semantic layers) turns an n log n-bit in-context computation into a single lookup.
- **Inference: retrieval heads are a scarce, finite channel.** Pruning a few percent of heads destroys retrieval, so the in-context channel is narrow. This supports [KB]'s "match small, read large": search over compact units, then read the one selected document.

### Gaps
- No theorem quantifies accuracy as a function of the number of *semantically similar* distractors in a trained LLM. The softmax results use bounded logits and say nothing about which distractors raise competing logits.
- Position-bias theory is about attention-score structure. Its quantitative link to end-task accuracy curves is empirical only.

## Q4. Theory of retrieval augmentation: benefit vs detriment, noise, conflicts, generalization

### Takeaway
The few formal RAG theories agree on three points. (1) Retrieved text helps only to the extent that it is closer to the truth than the model's prior. When it contradicts a confident prior or is noisy, the effect can reverse (token-level benefit/detriment, Tok-RAG). (2) Adding more retrieved examples reduces variance but hits a bias ceiling, so there is a finite optimal number of passages (RAG-as-noisy-ICL). Excess risk depends on both retriever and predictor, with data-store coverage entering the approximation error (Basu et al.). (3) Resolving context–memory conflicts provably needs a *prior on how often the context is right*: no fixed rule and no symmetric divergence statistic suffices (Pham Van & Tran). Certified robustness against injected passages is achievable by isolating passages and aggregating their answers (RobustRAG). These results give formal backing to [KB]'s hard validity filters, authority metadata and small, deduplicated contexts.

### Cited Findings

**1. Token-level benefit and detriment (Xu, Pang, Shen et al., "A Theory for Token-Level Harmonization in RAG", ICLR 2025).**
- *Model.* RAG is a fusion of the LLM's latent-concept mixture with the distribution of the retrieved texts P_R, in the style of Xie et al. Writing p(x_i | R, x_{1:i−1}) ∝ ∫ p(x_i|R, x_{1:i−1}, z) exp(v(z)) p(z) dz, the paper derives v(z) ≈ n·E_{r∼P_R}[log p(r|z)/p(r|z*)] = −n·[KL(P_R‖p(·|z)) − KL(P_R‖p(·|z*))]. The first KL is "distribution completion" (benefit: how much out-of-distribution knowledge the passages add relative to concept z). The second is "distribution contradiction" (detriment: the model's resistance to the retrieved concept z*). n is the number of retrieved passages. — [Tok-RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00944)
- *Theorem 1.* For D = ‖p(x_i|R, ·) − p_R(x_i|·)‖_1: ‖Φ‖_1 − sqrt(2KL(P_R‖p(r|z*))) ≤ D ≤ ‖Φ‖_1 + sqrt(2KL(P_R‖p(r|z*))), via a Pinsker-type argument, with Φ ≈ α∫_{Z∖{z*}} p(x_i|R, ·, z)·exp(−(benefit − detriment)) p(z) dz. *Theorem 2:* benefit − detriment ∝ 1/D, with an error analysis in Appendix E. *Theorem 3:* with M = ‖p(x_i|R, ·) − p(x_i|·)‖_1 (RAG vs no RAG), detriment outweighs benefit iff 1/D < 1/M. This lets the method decide, per token and without training, whether to follow the passage or the model. The derivation relies on approximations (O(1) factors in Eq. 6 and the n → ∞ limit in Eq. 7). — [Tok-RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00944)

**2. Finite-sample risk of RAG as noisy in-context regression (Guo, Tao, Ming et al., 2025; preprint "under review").**
- *Setting.* In-context linear regression with a linear self-attention predictor ŷ_q = x_q^T W X^T y, using m ICL examples plus n retrieved examples whose inputs are offset from the query by r_i ∼ N(0, δ_i² I) and whose labels carry noise σ_rag. — [Guo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03100)
- *Theorem 1.* An exact bias–variance decomposition L = err_variance(W) + err_bias(W) + σ², proved with Isserlis' theorem on sixth-order Gaussian moments. *Proposition 1:* for the pre-trained W*, err_variance = O(1/n) as n → ∞ with m fixed, but err_bias → C_1 = O(‖β‖² d²). "Introducing the RAG is ineffective at reducing the bias-induced error. Even when m → ∞, increasing n will cause a loss plateau", which the authors call an intrinsic ceiling that ICL does not have. *Proposition 2:* the optimal number of retrieved examples is n* = O((d‖β‖² + σ² − dσ_rag²)/(d‖β‖²)). It does not scale with m, and its improvement over n = 0 is only O(1/m²). *Theorems 2–3:* when retrieval noise grows with retrieval distance (distance-proportional noise, or a mixture in which the chance of "small noise" decays with rank), added low-ranked examples raise variance further. — [Guo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03100)

**3. Excess-risk bounds with a learned retriever (Basu, Rawat & Zaheer, 2024; preprint).**
- The setting is classification with a data store I, a retriever p_θ(z|x) and a predictor h_ξ(x, z). Theorem 3.3 bounds excess risk by the sum of: (a) a generalization term O(ℓ_max·sqrt(log n / n)) plus covering-number integrals over the joint predictor/retriever classes; (b) a retriever approximation term inf_θ inf_τ ℓ_max‖r_θ + τ·gap‖_∞ + log|I|/τ²; (c) a predictor approximation term; and (d) a data-store coverage term c_I|I|^{−γ_I}·exp(ℓ_max). The generalization term depends only logarithmically on store size. Theorem 3.4 instantiates this with MLPs and shows that different predictor/retriever size combinations reach the same bound, and that larger stores reduce the approximation error. — [Basu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15399)

**4. Formal and quasi-formal models of knowledge conflict.**
- Longpre et al. (EMNLP 2021) formalize context–memory conflicts by substituting entities in passages. They measure models' "over-reliance on memorized information (the cause of hallucinations)" and show that training on substituted contexts reduces it. — [Longpre et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05052). A survey classifies conflicts as context–memory, inter-context and intra-memory ([Xu et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08319)).
- LLMs are "highly receptive to external evidence even when that conflicts with their parametric memory, given that the external evidence is coherent and convincing", but show "strong confirmation bias" when evidence is partly consistent with memory. — [Xie et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13300)
- On 1,200+ questions in six domains, six LLMs adopted incorrect retrieved content, "overriding their own correct prior knowledge over 60% of the time". Adoption falls as the perturbation becomes more unrealistic, and rises as the model's prior confidence falls. — [ClashEval, Wu, Wu & Zou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10198)
- Mechanistically, influential heads carry a "superposition of contextual information and parametric memory", which a dual-run intervention (JuICE) exploits. — [Li et al., ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10996)
- *Decision-theoretic impossibility of fixed conflict rules (Pham Van & Tran, Oct 2026; preprint, not yet peer-reviewed).*
  - Proposition 1: the score-maximizing rule commits to â = argmax_a q(a) if q(â) > 1/2 and abstains otherwise, which is Chow's reject rule.
  - Proposition 2: let π be the share of contexts that are right. Any fixed policy φ has a score S_φ(π) that is affine in π, while the best attainable S*(π) = max_φ S_φ(π) is convex. Every fixed rule is therefore strictly beaten wherever it is not exactly optimal. Proof: average over R_c ∈ {0,1} to get linearity; a maximum of affine functions is convex.
  - Proposition 3: any statistic T of the two next-token distributions that is independent of which source is right, given that a conflict occurred, carries no information about direction. Contrastive or divergence-weighted decoding "loses most where the divergence is largest" on wrong contexts.
  - Proposition 5: under label shift, an additive log-odds prior shift gives the exact Bayes posterior at a new π.
  - Conclusion: resolution needs "evidence about each source separately, and a prior on how often the context is right". — [Pham Van & Tran](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36169)

**5. Certified robustness to corrupted passages (RobustRAG; Xiang, Wu, Zhong et al., 2024; preprint).**
- "Isolate-then-aggregate": generate responses from disjoint passage groups, then aggregate securely with keyword-based or decoding-based aggregation. Theorems 1–2 show that the certification algorithm returns a valid τ such that response quality M(r, a) ≥ τ against any attacker injecting k′ passages, adaptive attackers included. — [Xiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15556)

**6. Limits on the retrieval step itself (boundary with the IR track).**
- For single-vector embeddings, "the number of top-k subsets of documents capable of being returned … is limited by the dimension of the embedding", bounded via sign-rank: rank_±(2A − 1) − 1 ≤ rank_rop(A) ≤ rank_±(2A − 1). The effect appears even with free, test-set-optimized embeddings. — [Weller et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21038)
- An information-entropy analysis argues that ensembling multiple RAG systems helps (preprint) — [Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13828)
- Practical precursors that need no architecture change include In-Context RALM, which simply prepends documents ([Ram et al., TACL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00083)).

### Inferences
- **Inference: why superseded passages must be filtered, not ranked down [KB freshness].** In Tok-RAG, the passages' pull on the fused distribution enters as v(z) ∝ n·(…). The retrieved concept's weight grows with the *number* of passages supporting it. k near-duplicate copies of a stale clause therefore multiply its pull, and down-ranking does not remove that pull while the passages stay in the context. Exclusion, by a hard validity filter plus duplicate collapse, is the only operation that sets their contribution to zero. This formalizes the HoH and stale-document-poisoning results summarized in [KB].
- **Inference: validity and authority metadata are the prior π [KB conflicts in the data layer].** Pham Van & Tran prove that no fixed "trust context" or "trust memory" rule is optimal across deployments, and that model-internal divergence cannot say which side is right. The missing input is a per-source reliability prior. Fields such as `valid_from/valid_to`, `superseded_by`, authority tier and verification date are exactly an estimate of P(context is right). The toolkit should expose them to the generator ("superseded on date X by unit Y") or use them to filter. This is the cleanest formal argument for [KB]'s choice of governance metadata over learned rankings. The paper is a recent preprint, so this should be treated as strong-but-unreviewed support.
- **Inference: a small k is optimal, not just cheap [KB; LAKE multi-stage retrieval].** Guo et al.'s optimal n* and bias ceiling, together with the dispersion results in Q3, predict a U-shaped accuracy curve in k when noise grows with rank. Reranking keeps the n* best passages, and the curve should be tuned on the golden set rather than fixed. The model is linear-regression ICL, so this transfers qualitatively, not numerically.
- **Inference: store coverage enters the risk bound directly [LAKE].** In Basu et al., the c_I|I|^{−γ_I} term means that store coverage reduces approximation error, while store size costs only log|I| in generalization. Adding relevant governed units is cheap in theory. Adding noisy ones is not free, because they hurt the retriever-gap term.
- **Inference: isolation is a write-path and read-path defence [KB poisoning].** RobustRAG shows that answering per passage and then aggregating bounds the damage any k′ poisoned units can do. A toolkit can offer "per-unit answer then vote" for high-stakes queries.

### Gaps
- No RAG theory found models *temporal validity* explicitly, for example a world state changing over time with stale passages drawn from an earlier concept. The Tok-RAG and conflict models handle it only as a generic wrong concept or wrong context.
- Most RAG theorems are for toy settings (linear regression ICL, classification with MLPs) or rest on approximations. None gives numeric guarantees for production LLMs.
- I found no peer-reviewed proof of an optimal top-k for LLM RAG. Guo et al. is under review.

## Q5. Determinism: why temperature 0 is not deterministic, and why extractions should be frozen

### Takeaway
Floating-point addition is not associative. Different reduction orders give different results within known error bounds (O(nε)·Σ|x_i| for naive summation, O(ε log n)·Σ|x_i| pairwise). An inference kernel is a deterministic function of (inputs, reduction schedule), but production servers choose the schedule by batch size, and batch size depends on other users' load. Greedy decoding is a discontinuous argmax, so a perturbation of one ulp can flip a token whenever the top-2 logit gap is tiny. Autoregression then amplifies the flip into a different completion. Batch-invariant kernels restore bitwise repeatability for a fixed (weights, kernels, hardware, precision) tuple at a throughput cost. Nothing restores it across model versions. This is the formal basis of [DET]'s "freeze extractions, query snapshots".

### Cited Findings
- *Floating-point model.* Rounding to nearest gives relative error ≤ machine epsilon ε per operation. Goldberg's example: "((2 × 10⁻³⁰ + 10³⁰) − 10³⁰) − 10⁻³⁰ is exactly equal to 10⁻³⁰, but on a machine with IEEE arithmetic the computed result will be −10⁻³⁰." — [Goldberg 1991, ACM Computing Surveys](https://docs.oracle.com/cd/E19957-01/806-3568/ncg_goldberg.html)
- *Summation error depends on the reduction tree.* Naive recursive summation has worst-case error O(εn)·Σ|x_i|. Pairwise summation has |E_n| ≤ (ε log₂n/(1 − ε log₂n))·Σ|x_i|, after Higham (1993). — [Wikipedia: Pairwise summation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pairwise_summation); [Higham 1993, SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 14(4)](https://doi.org/10.1137/0914050)
- *Batch invariance (Thinking Machines Lab, Horace He et al., Sep 2025).*
  - The common "concurrency + floating point" hypothesis is incomplete: "atomic adds are not necessary for the vast majority of kernels", and the LLM forward pass is run-to-run deterministic for a fixed batch.
  - The actual cause is that RMSNorm, matmul (split-K, stream-K, instruction choice) and attention (split-KV) change their reduction order with batch size, and "the load (and thus batch-size) nondeterministically varies". In one example, a row computed alone vs within a batch differed by up to 1669.25.
  - Fix: "the reduction order for each element must be fixed regardless of the batch-size of the kernel". Results: Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 at temperature 0 gave 80 unique completions in 1000 runs (divergence first at token 103); with batch-invariant kernels, all 1000 were identical. Cost: 26 s (default vLLM) vs 55 s (unoptimized deterministic) vs 42 s (improved attention kernel). — [Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)
- vLLM ships batch invariance as a beta feature (`VLLM_BATCH_INVARIANT=1`) on listed GPUs, validated on listed models. It "may impact performance", and tensor-parallel all-reduces not a multiple of 16 bytes are unsupported. — [vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/batch_invariance.html)
- PyTorch: "Completely reproducible results are not guaranteed across PyTorch releases, individual commits, or different platforms", and CPU and GPU runs may differ even with identical seeds. — [PyTorch reproducibility notes](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.15/notes/randomness.html)
- Under bf16 greedy decoding, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B shows up to 9% accuracy variation and 9,000 tokens of response-length difference across GPU count, GPU type and evaluation batch size. The root cause is "the non-associative nature of floating-point arithmetic under limited numerical precision". LayerCast (16-bit weights, FP32 compute) mitigates this. — [Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09501)
- Across five LLMs configured for determinism, eight tasks and 10 runs: "accuracy variations up to 15%", a best-to-worst gap up to 70%, and "none of the LLMs consistently delivers repeatable accuracy across all tasks, much less identical output strings". — [Atil et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667)
- In ChatGPT code generation, the share of tasks with zero equal test outputs across requests was 75.76% (CodeContests), 51.00% (APPS) and 47.56% (HumanEval) in the paper's main setting. — [Ouyang et al., ACM TOSEM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02828). Greedy decoding generally beats sampling, and evaluation should not ignore non-determinism. — [Song et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10457)

### Inferences
- **Formal sketch (mine; it assembles the cited facts).** Let z_t(σ) be the logits at step t under reduction schedule σ. Write z_t(σ) = z_t* + η_t(σ), where ‖η_t‖ ≤ c·ε·κ_t by the summation bounds (κ_t is a condition-number-like factor of the network at that input). The greedy token flips between two schedules whenever the top-2 gap Δ_t < 2‖η_t‖_∞. Assuming independence across steps, P(identical T-token output) ≤ Π_t (1 − P(Δ_t < 2‖η_t‖)). This decays geometrically in T if near-ties occur at a positive rate, which long reasoning chains make likely, as in Yuan et al.'s 9,000-token divergences. After the first flip there is no contraction, because the next input differs, so outputs diverge. Batch invariance makes η_t(σ) = η_t(σ′) for all batch sizes. It removes the cause only for a fixed software/hardware/precision tuple.
- **Inference: why [DET] should freeze extractions rather than rely on deterministic inference.** (i) Hosted APIs do not expose kernel or batch control. (ii) Even full batch invariance does not survive a model or weights update, which changes z* itself. (iii) Kalai's lower bounds (Q1) mean a perfectly repeatable extractor still errs at a non-zero rate, so outputs need validation regardless. Persisting each extraction with (model id, prompt hash, schema version, kernel/precision config, timestamp) and querying the snapshot is the only design that gives identical repeat answers. Batch-invariant serving is worth enabling for self-hosted *re-extraction diffs*, so that observed churn reflects model or prompt changes, not numerics.
- **Inference:** greedy (T = 0) decoding should still be the default for extraction (Song et al.), but "temperature 0" should be documented as "low-variance", not "deterministic".

### Gaps
- No paper found gives a measured distribution of top-2 logit gaps Δ_t for extraction-style outputs, which would let one predict flip rates per field.
- No study measures how batch-invariant serving changes field-level churn in structured extraction. This matches the gap noted in [DET].

## Q6. Limits of chain-of-thought, tool use and multi-step agents: expressivity vs compounding error

### Takeaway
Chain-of-thought provably increases a transformer's computational power, and the increase is proportional to the number of steps. Without CoT, bounded-depth transformers sit in TC⁰ (log precision) or AC⁰ (constant precision). With t(n) steps they can simulate any t(n)-time Turing machine. Each step, however, is a chance to err: per-step error ε compounds to success ≈ (1 − ε)^T, imitation-style learners can incur O(T²ε) cost, and models condition on their own earlier errors. Verification breaks the compounding only if the verifier is external and sound. Self-critique can collapse, and detection needs negative examples. This supports [KB]/[DET]'s short loops, tool-executed composition (SQL or graph queries) and deterministic verification gates.

### Cited Findings
- *CoT expressivity.*
  - Merrill & Sabharwal (ICLR 2024): log-precision transformers with no intermediate steps are in uniform TC⁰, so they cannot recognize all regular languages unless TC⁰ = NC¹. Theorem 1: |x| + 1 decoding steps recognize any regular language. Theorem 2 / Corollary 2.1: TIME(t(n)) ⊆ CoT(t(n)). Theorem 4: CoT(t(n)) ⊆ SPACE(t(n) + log n). Logarithmically many steps "push the limits of standard transformers only slightly". — [Merrill & Sabharwal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07923)
  - Li, Liu, Zhou & Ma (ICLR 2024): constant-depth, constant-precision transformers without CoT are in AC⁰. With T CoT steps and O(log n) embedding size, they solve anything computable by boolean circuits of size T. — [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12875)
  - Feng et al. (NeurIPS 2023, oral): bounded-depth transformers cannot directly output answers to basic arithmetic or linear-equation tasks unless size grows super-polynomially (a circuit-complexity argument). Constant-size autoregressive transformers solve them, and dynamic programming problems, by generating CoT. — [Feng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15408)
  - Iterated function composition needs Ω(√(n/(Hdp))) CoT steps for a single layer ([Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08164)). Concatenating intermediate supervision makes otherwise unlearnable composite seq2seq problems learnable ([Wies, Levine & Shashua, ICLR 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02892)).
- *Compounding error.*
  - Ross, Gordon & Bagnell (AISTATS 2011): if a policy has expected per-step loss ε under the expert's state distribution, supervised training gives J(π) ≤ J(π*) + T²ε, and this is Θ(T²ε) for small ε. Training on the learner's own induced distribution (DAgger) gives J(π) ≤ J(π*) + uTε + O(1), which is linear in T. — [Ross et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686). An imitation-learning analysis of exposure bias in text generation shows that it "leads to an accumulation of errors" that perplexity fails to capture ([Arora et al., ACL Findings 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01171)).
  - Dziri et al. (NeurIPS 2023), Proposition 4.1: if f_n combines n independent applications of g, and the estimator of g errs independently with probability ε, then P(f_n ≠ \hat f_n) > 1 − c_n − (1 − ε)^n(1 − c_n), which tends exponentially to ≥ 1 − c. Proposition 4.2 (iterated application, recovery probability at most c): s_n ≤ (1 − ε − c)s_{n−1} + c, so lim sup P(correct) ≤ c/(c + ε). The authors advise "chaining only a few compositional steps" and adding planning or refinement modules. — [Dziri et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18654)
  - Sinha et al. (ICLR 2026), Proposition 1: with independent constant step accuracy p and no self-correction, the horizon at success rate s is H_s(p) = ln(s)/ln(p), which grows hyperbolically as p → 1. Empirically, per-step accuracy *degrades* with step count because of "self-conditioning": models become likelier to err when the context contains their own earlier errors, and thinking mitigates this. — [Sinha et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09677)
  - Bachmann & Nagarajan (ICML 2024): the compounding-error critique assumes teacher forcing learned an accurate next-token predictor. On some planning tasks it does not learn one in the first place. — [Bachmann & Nagarajan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)
- *Verification.*
  - Self-improvement is governed by a "generation-verification gap", and a variant of it scales monotonically with pre-training FLOPs ([Song et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02674)).
  - GPT-4 on Game of 24, graph coloring and STRIPS planning shows "significant performance collapse with self-critique and significant performance gains with sound external verification" ([Stechly et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08115)).
  - Coverage from repeated sampling scales log-linearly over four orders of magnitude. With automatic verifiers this becomes real gains: SWE-bench Lite rose from 15.9% (1 sample) to 56% (250 samples) ([Brown et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787)).
  - Hallucination detection needs labelled negatives (Karbasi et al., Q1).

### Inferences
- **Inference: short loops and verification gates [KB "two rounds capture most"; DET quality gates].** With per-step accuracy p and an external verifier that catches a fraction r of errors and forces a retry, effective per-step accuracy is ≈ 1 − ε(1 − r), so H_s ≈ ln s / ln(1 − ε(1 − r)). This is my extension of Sinha et al.'s Proposition 1, assuming independent errors and successful retries. Verification therefore multiplies the usable horizon by about 1/(1 − r). Self-verification has an r that is small or even negative (Stechly et al.). Deterministic checks (schema validation, exact-match against the store, SQL constraint checks) have r close to 1 for the error classes they cover. That is why [DET] puts deterministic checks first.
- **Inference: let tools do the composition [DET "agents choose queries, not write them"; LAKE typed tools].** CoT power is proportional to step count, and each step adds error. Moving composition (joins, aggregations, version resolution) into a deterministic executor replaces T error-prone generated steps with a single *selection* step (choose a parameterized query), which shrinks the exponent in (1 − ε)^T. Combined with Peng et al.'s composition bound (Q3), this explains why parameterized tools let small models match large ones in [DET]'s evidence.
- **Inference: keep failed tool output out of the next context.** Self-conditioning suggests that agents should not carry their own failed intermediate outputs forward verbatim. Loops should summarize or reset state between rounds.

### Gaps
- No theorem covers error accumulation for *retrieval* loops in particular, for example how query reformulation errors compound across rounds. The two-rounds finding in [KB] is empirical.
- The CoT expressivity results are about what is *representable* with idealized precision and constructions. They do not show that trained models learn these algorithms.
