# Information science (LIS) theory for agent knowledge bases and research agents

Scope: information foraging, information-seeking models, relevance theory, knowledge organization, bibliometric laws, knowledge management and information quality. Other tracks cover information theory, IR theory, decision and statistical theory, and LLM capacity. Each entry below states the model, labels its epistemic status, cites its derivation or validation, and maps it to a method in the earlier reports (`reports/Searchable agent knowledge base construction.md`, `reports/Deterministic retrieval data conversion.md`, `reports/Agent ready data lake toolkit.md`).

**Status labels used throughout**
- **[THEOREM]**: a mathematical result that follows from stated assumptions. The proof is given or cited.
- **[EMPIRICAL LAW]**: a regularity fitted to data. It can fail, and its parameters vary.
- **[DESCRIPTIVE MODEL]**: a qualitative account derived from observation, such as interviews or log studies. It is not predictive in a formal sense.
- **[NORMATIVE / STANDARD]**: a standard or design canon. It prescribes rather than describes.
- **[MY INFERENCE]**: a mapping to the toolkit, or a derivation step, that is mine and not the source's.

## 1. Information foraging theory: scent, patches, the marginal value theorem and when an agent should stop reading

### Takeaway
Information foraging theory (IFT) is the one LIS/HCI framework with real theorems behind it. Charnov's marginal value theorem (MVT) and the diet (prey-choice) model are provable optimality conditions on a rate of gain. Information scent has a validated computational form: a PMI-weighted activation fed into a softmax choice rule. Under the MVT, cheaper between-source travel (a good index, progressive disclosure, fast search) implies that an agent should read *less* of each source and visit *more* sources. The diet model implies that low-profitability source classes should be excluded outright, not merely down-ranked, once better sources are plentiful. Recent LLM work (InForage, SCOUT, PatchScout) uses IFT vocabulary, but none of these papers implements the MVT itself.

### Cited Findings

**1a. The original framework [DESCRIPTIVE + FORMAL MODEL]**
- Pirolli & Card's IFT covers "strategies and technologies for information seeking, gathering, and consumption", adapted to the flux of information in the environment. It rests on a rational (adaptation) analysis plus a production-system process model, ACT-IF. Information scent is the imperfect perception of a source's value, cost or access path, obtained from proximal cues such as link labels, citations or icons — [Pirolli & Card 1999, Psychological Review 106(4):643–675](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643) (abstract and definition via search summary; I did not read the full text).
- The "conventional" patch and diet models derive from **Holling's disc equation**. The rate of currency intake R is the ratio of net currency gained (U) to total search time (Ts) plus exploitation (handling) time (Th), i.e. **R = U/(Ts + Th)**. Each Scatter/Gather cluster is treated as a patch with a cumulative gain function g_i(t) — [Pirolli & Card 1995, CHI](https://www.chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/papers/ppp_bdy.htm).
- In the same paper, the MVT is stated as follows: the long-term rate is maximized when "the marginal value (instantaneous rate) of the currency gain at the time of leaving each patch" equals the long-term average rate. Residence times decrease as the overall rate of gain improves. Re-ordering cluster titles by relevance turns a linear within-patch gain curve into a diminishing-returns curve, which raises the overall rate and *shortens* optimal patch time — [Pirolli & Card 1995](https://www.chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/papers/ppp_bdy.htm).
- In the diet model, profitability is expected net gain divided by handling time. Items are ranked by profitability, and a lower-ranked item type is included only if its profitability is at least the average rate obtained from the higher-ranked types. The **zero-one rule** says each type is taken all-or-none, and inclusion of a type does not depend on that type's own encounter rate — [Pirolli & Card 1995](https://www.chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/papers/ppp_bdy.htm).
- Empirical setting in that paper: 4 Xerox PARC researchers retrieved TREC-relevant documents for one hour from the Tipster collection (>1M documents) using Scatter/Gather. A dynamic model found scatter-gathering optimal early in the task and select-and-display optimal later or at higher cluster precision — [Pirolli & Card 1995](https://www.chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/papers/ppp_bdy.htm). The sample is very small; treat it as a demonstration, not a validation.

**1b. Charnov's marginal value theorem [THEOREM]**
- Source: Charnov, "Optimal foraging: the marginal value theorem", *Theoretical Population Biology* 9:129–136 (1976). A predator exploiting discrete patches should leave a patch when its marginal capture rate falls to the habitat's average capture rate — [Charnov 1976](https://doi.org/10.1016/0040-5809(76)90040-X); bibliographic details and the original rule as summarized by search results. A textbook derivation: maximize W = B(C)/C, so dW/dC = 0 gives B′(C*) = B(C*)/C*, and the second-order condition reduces to B″ < 0 at the optimum — [Sargent, MVT lecture notes, U. Kentucky](https://darwin.uky.edu/~sargent/Bio608/MVT.htm).
- **Formal statement (the multi-patch form, as in Charnov).** Patch types i are encountered at rate λ_i per unit travel time. Each has a gain function g_i(t) that is increasing and concave with g_i(0) = 0. Residence times are t_i. The long-run rate is
  R(t_1,…,t_k) = Σ_i λ_i g_i(t_i) / (1 + Σ_i λ_i t_i).
  **Proof.** Write N = Σλ_i g_i and D = 1 + Σλ_i t_i. Then ∂R/∂t_j = λ_j [g_j′(t_j)·D − N] / D². At an interior optimum this is zero, so g_j′(t_j*) = N/D = R* for every j. *Leave every patch when its instantaneous gain rate falls to the best achievable average rate.* Concavity makes the stationary point a maximum. [Derivation: mine, standard. It reproduces the condition stated by Pirolli & Card 1995 and by Sargent.]
- **Comparative statics: the key prediction for indexes.** With one patch type and mean travel time t_B = 1/λ, R(t) = g(t)/(t_B + t), and the optimum solves F(t, t_B) = g′(t)(t_B + t) − g(t) = 0. By the implicit function theorem, dt*/dt_B = −F_{t_B}/F_t = −g′(t*)/[g″(t*)(t_B + t*)], which is **> 0** because g″ < 0. *Lower travel cost means shorter optimal residence.* A better within-patch ordering (a more concave g reached earlier) also shortens residence, as Pirolli & Card 1995 note. [Derivation: mine.]
- **Worked example (my computation).** Take g(t) = G(1 − e^{−t/τ}), where τ is the "depletion scale" of a document. The optimum satisfies e^{−t*/τ}(t_B + t* + τ) = τ (closed form via Lambert W_{−1}). Numerically:

  | t_B/τ (search cost relative to depletion scale) | t*/τ | share of the document's extractable value taken | R* (in G/τ) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 0.1 (excellent index, cheap search) | 0.42 | 34% | 0.66 |
  | 1 | 1.15 | 68% | 0.32 |
  | 10 (each new source is expensive) | 2.61 | 93% | 0.07 |

**1c. Information scent: formal form and validation [FORMAL MODEL + EMPIRICAL VALIDATION]**
- In SNIF-ACT, link scent is computed by ACT-R spreading activation. A_i = B_i + Σ_{j∈L} W_j S_ji, where S_ji (association between link cue j and goal chunk i) "is a very near approximation of … Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)". Scent is IS(G, L) = Σ_{i∈G} A_i. Link choice uses a softmax/Luce rule, P(n) = e^{IS(n)/τ} / Σ_{j∈C} e^{IS(j)/τ}, so a link's choice probability depends on competing links and on the size of the conflict set — [Fu & Pirolli 2007, HCI 22(4):355–412](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/25938.pdf).
- Validation: SNIF-ACT 2.0 adds a Bayesian satisficing mechanism that evaluates links sequentially and decides when to leave a page. It fit link-selection counts with **R² = .91 (Yahoo) and .69 (ParcWeb)**, or .64 and .91 after excluding rarely chosen links. It predicted going back to the previous page with **R² = .73 and .80**. In the model, the probability of backing up increases when a page's scent drops below the mean scent of previous pages. Site-leaving followed a scent drop, but on only 12 events that difference was not significant (t(11) = 0.61, p = .56). Scent predicted link choice better than screen position, although the two correlated at r = .64 — [Fu & Pirolli 2007](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/25938.pdf).
- People approximate MVT-like rules with heuristics. In discretionary task interleaving, participants allocated more time to the more productive task. "Giving-up times" (time since the last success) were *longer* in the less productive task. Behaviour reflected sensitivity to the continuous rate of return, consistent with Green's rule (1984), plus a tendency to switch after completing a subgoal. A model combining the two predicted all the reliable effects across three experiments — [Payne, Duggan & Neth 2007, JEP: General 136(3)](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/444452/Payne,_Duggan_&_Neth,_07.pdf).

**1d. Formal search-economics extensions and stopping [FORMAL MODELS + SIMULATION]**
- Azzopardi & Zuccon built ad-hoc retrieval models under IFT, Search Economic Theory and the Interactive Probability Ranking Principle in one framework. Under certain assumptions the three reach similar behavioural hypotheses — [Azzopardi & Zuccon 2015, ICTIR](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/110908).
- SERP-level information scent predicts when searchers stop. Adding a results-page-level stopping rule improved simulated gain for most snippet-level strategies and matched real behaviour more closely, so models that assume every snippet is examined are less accurate — [Maxwell & Azzopardi 2018, ECIR](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/62856).
- Among query-level stopping rules, "frustration point" and "disgust" rules, which stop after a tolerance of non-relevant items, performed best overall and best matched real searchers — [Maxwell et al. 2015, CIKM](https://core.ac.uk/download/42366798.pdf).

**1e. Recent applications to LLM agents and AI search**
- **InForage** (NeurIPS 2025 spotlight) is an RL framework "inspired by Information Foraging Theory". Its reward is R = R_eff·(R_outcome + α·R_gain), with R_gain = max_t coverage(∪_{τ≤t} K_τ, D*) (the peak coverage of retrieved "patches" against an expected knowledge set) and R_eff = β^{max(0, T−2)}. It uses α = 0.2 and β = 0.95. Scent is mapped to reasoning traces and sub-queries, and patches to retrieved `<info>` blocks. **The MVT is not used.** Average EM was 41.0 against 35.6 for Search-R1-GRPO across Self/NQ/HotpotQA/2Wiki (author-reported) — [Qian & Liu 2025, arXiv 2505.09316](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09316).
- **SCOUT** recasts million-token long-text understanding as "active information foraging". It answers from a compact, provenance-grounded "epistemic state" and uses "state-level gap diagnosis" to alternate coarse-to-fine exploration and state updates "toward query sufficiency". It reports parity with proprietary models at up to **8× fewer tokens** (author-reported) — [Zhang et al. 2026, arXiv 2605.04496](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04496).
- **PatchScout** formulates thematic web collection as long-horizon foraging over "query-induced semantic patches". The central decision is when to keep exploiting locally and when to switch regions under a fixed budget. It reports higher yield and domain coverage than traversal and search-agent baselines, and discovered 102 previously unidentified entities in a social-science use case (author-reported) — [West & Dragut 2026, arXiv 2611.00169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00169).
- Chatbots are a "non-patchy" environment, so the IFT cost-value proposition must be re-validated there. The paper is a thought experiment with preliminary hypotheses — [Srinivasa Ragavan & Alipour 2024, arXiv 2406.04452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04452).
- "Information Farming" (CHIIR 2026 perspective) argues that GenAI moves users from foraging in others' patches toward cultivating their own "information plots" through prompts and workflows. This is presented as a hypothesis, not as a measured result — [Azzopardi & Roegiest 2026, arXiv 2601.12544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.12544).
- Collection-centric foraging (Marco) uses user-composed schemas over a document collection. In a usability study (n = 16), tasks were completed 16% faster without loss of accuracy — [Fok et al. 2024, arXiv 2405.01501](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01501).
- Over-searching is measurable. Search improves accuracy on answerable queries but harms abstention on unanswerable ones. It is worse for reasoning models and deep-research systems, worse under noisy retrieval, and it compounds across turns. Negative evidence improves abstention. The paper proposes **Tokens Per Correctness (TPC)** as a cost-performance metric — [Xie et al. 2026, arXiv 2601.05503](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05503).
- Deep search agents have "misaligned decision boundaries", the threshold at which accumulated information suffices. The result is both over-search and under-search. Causal-intervention preference optimization (DAS) calibrates this boundary (author-reported) — [Zhang et al. 2026, arXiv 2602.03304](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03304).
- Evidence on progressive disclosure, already in the earlier report and checked here at abstract level: one-level progressive disclosure helps when the corpus is too large to navigate by reading. A second routing level "never helps and sometimes breaks accuracy". "Progressive disclosure buys context, not intelligence" — [He et al. 2026, arXiv 2607.17598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17598). In a separate ablation, two retrieval iterations captured 95% of the gains of five — [Shaikh 2026, arXiv 2606.21553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21553).

### Inferences
- [MY INFERENCE] **Index files and progressive disclosure are a reduction in between-patch time t_B, plus scent on the "links".** The MVT comparative statics (dt*/dt_B > 0) predict that an agent with a good index should read less of each file and open more files. The table in 1b quantifies this: with cheap travel, about a third of a document's extractable value is taken before moving on, against 93% when each new source is expensive. This argues for chunk-level `read(range)` tools and against "read whole file" defaults whenever search is cheap. It also explains the ∞Bench result: when a strong harness already makes t_B small through grep, an extra disclosure layer adds travel cost without adding scent.
- [MY INFERENCE] **A deeper hierarchy adds travel cost.** Each routing level is an extra patch transition with its own t_B and its own scent-estimation error. Under the softmax choice rule, misrouting probability compounds multiplicatively across levels, so with noisy scent a two-level tree loses more than it saves. This is consistent with "one level is enough" (2607.17598). It is a hypothesis about mechanism, not something the paper tested.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Writing index entries for scent.** Because S_ji ≈ PMI(cue, goal), the index line for a unit should contain the terms with the highest PMI against the questions the unit answers. These are the domain's preferred and alternate labels and the requester's own phrasing (KCS "requestor's context"), not generic words. Glossary altLabels injected into index descriptions raise scent directly. The Furnas "vocabulary problem" (§4) is the reason a single label gives low scent for most users.
- [MY INFERENCE] **A principled stopping rule for the agent loop.** Estimate the marginal gain of the next read, for example as new atomic claims or coverage of the decomposed sub-questions per 1k tokens, and stop when it falls below the session's running average rate, an MVT-style giving-up rule. Alternatively use a Maxwell-style frustration rule: stop after k consecutive non-contributing reads. TPC (2601.05503) is the natural currency. InForage's β^{T−2} discount is a fixed per-step tax. It is *not* rate-matching, so it cannot adapt t* to the depletion profile of each source.
- [MY INFERENCE] **The diet model justifies hard exclusion of low-tier sources when high-tier sources are plentiful.** Proof sketch (standard): with types ranked by π_i = g_i/h_i, adding type k+1 raises R iff π_{k+1} > R_k. This holds because (A + λg)/(B + λh) > A/B ⟺ g/h > A/B, and λ_{k+1} cancels. So whether to include forum posts, tier-4 external content or very long low-scent PDFs in candidate generation does not depend on how often they appear. It depends only on whether their gain per token beats the rate the better tiers already deliver. Superseded content has g < 0 and is never included, which formally supports "filter out, not rank down" from the earlier report.
- [MY INFERENCE] SCOUT's "epistemic state toward query sufficiency" and InForage's coverage reward both treat stopping as a sufficiency test, which is the relevance-theoretic view in §3. IFT says the stop should depend on the *rate*, not only on sufficiency. Both signals are needed: stop when sufficient, or when the marginal rate falls below the average rate.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that implements a literal MVT patch-leaving rule (marginal rate equal to average rate) inside an LLM agent and compares it with fixed step budgets. This is an open, testable proposal.
- I did not read the full text of Pirolli & Card (1999). Its ACT-IF and Scatter/Gather fits are cited from the abstract and the 1995 CHI paper.
- The SNIF-ACT site-leaving result rests on 12 events and is not significant. The strong evidence is for link choice and backing up.

## 2. Information-seeking and searching models: berrypicking, Ellis, Kuhlthau, Marchionini, ASK, Wilson, Taylor

### Takeaway
These are **descriptive models** built from interviews, longitudinal studies and observation, not theorems. They agree on four points. Needs are not fully specifiable at the start (Belkin, Taylor). Queries evolve, and answers are gathered piecemeal (Bates). Seeking is a repertoire of distinct moves (Ellis). Uncertainty peaks mid-search before a focus forms (Kuhlthau). Each point maps onto a specific agent design element: a clarify step, an iterative loop with a working set, a tool repertoire, and gap-driven stopping. The strongest quantitative support for one of these mappings is from IR: one good clarifying question gave more than 170% P@1 improvement in an oracle setting.

### Cited Findings
- **Bates (1989), berrypicking [DESCRIPTIVE]**: "the query itself (as well as the search terms used) is continually shifting", and the need "is satisfied not by a single final retrieved set" but bit by bit. Six techniques: footnote chasing (backward chaining), citation searching (forward chaining), journal run, area scanning (browsing shelf neighbours, "jump the rails" of the classification), subject searching in A&I services, and author searching. Design advice: build each technique in "as a package that the searcher can call upon when desired", support browsing and linking, and provide a temporary store for flagged items — [Bates 1989](https://pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/bates/berrypicking.html).
- **Ellis (1989) [DESCRIPTIVE]**: from academic social scientists, six behavioural "characteristics" (not stages): starting, chaining, browsing, differentiating (selecting sources by orientation and audience), monitoring, extracting. They are proposed as a basis for IR system design and possibly hypertext — [Ellis 1989, J. Doc. 45(3):171–212](https://doi.org/10.1108/eb026843). Wilson lists the extended set "Starting, Chaining, Browsing, Differentiating, Monitoring, Extracting, Verifying, Ending" and notes that Starting must begin and Ending must close a process — [Wilson 1999](http://informationr.net/tdw/publ/papers/1999JDoc.html).
- **Kuhlthau (1991) ISP [DESCRIPTIVE, longitudinally validated]**: six stages: Initiation, Selection, Exploration, Formulation, Collection, Presentation. Exploration is where "confusion, uncertainty, and doubt often increase", with a confidence "dip". Formulation is the "pivotal turning point" at which a focus forms. At Presentation, a summary search finds "relevance decreases and redundancy increases". The principle of uncertainty reads: "Uncertainty is a cognitive state that commonly causes affective symptoms of anxiety and lack of confidence", and increased uncertainty marks a "zone of intervention". The model was developed from a 1985 study of 25 high-school students, a longitudinal case study of 6 students through college, large-scale studies of library users (1989–1990), and later workplace studies (early-career workers 1999; lawyers 2001) — [Kuhlthau, ISP page](https://wp.comminfo.rutgers.edu/ckuhlthau/information-search-process/); [Kuhlthau 1991, JASIS 42(5):361–371](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-4571(199106)42:5%3C361::AID-ASI6%3E3.0.CO;2-%23) (the "bibliographic paradigm … based on certainty and order" versus users' uncertainty, via the abstract).
- **Belkin, Oddy & Brooks (1982), ASK [THEORETICAL / DESIGN STUDY]**: information needs "cannot in principle be precisely specified". It is "more suitable to attempt to describe a user's anomalous state of knowledge than to ask the user to specify her/his need as a request". The assumption that the expression of a need and document text are functionally equivalent fails unless the user can state the need as "a coherent or defined information structure" (p. 64) — [Belkin, Oddy & Brooks 1982, J. Doc. 38(2):61–71](https://doi.org/10.1108/eb026722) (via abstract and quoted secondary summaries; full text not accessible to me).
- **Taylor (1968), levels of need [DESCRIPTIVE]**: Q1 visceral (actual but unexpressed), Q2 conscious (within-brain description), Q3 formalized (formal statement), Q4 compromised (the question as presented to the system). The intermediary's job in question negotiation is to move back from Q4 toward Q2/Q1 — as described in [Ruthven 2019](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/65478) and summarized secondary material; original: [Taylor 1968, College & Research Libraries 29(3):178–194](https://doi.org/10.5860/crl_29_03_178).
- **Empirical test of Taylor**: in several hundred posts from four Internet discussion groups, conscious-need descriptions were more emotional, used more sensory references and had different temporal dimensions than formalized needs. "Information needs at different levels can be distinguished by their linguistic patterns", with an explicit implication for automated need classification — [Ruthven 2019, IP&M 56(1):77–90](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/65478).
- **Wilson (1999), nested model [META-MODEL]**: information behaviour contains information seeking, which contains information searching (interaction with IR systems). Information seeking is framed as goal-directed problem solving through problem identification, definition, resolution and solution statement, with unresolved uncertainty sending the person back to earlier stages. Dervin's sense-making contributes situation–gap–bridge–outcome — [Wilson 1999, J. Doc. 55(3)](http://informationr.net/tdw/publ/papers/1999JDoc.html).
- **Marchionini (2006), exploratory search [DESCRIPTIVE TAXONOMY]**: lookup (fact retrieval, known-item), learn and investigate (comparison, synthesis, discovery). Learning searches typically combine browsing with analytical strategies, and tools that go beyond predictable fact retrieval are "critical to exploratory search success" — [Marchionini 2006, CACM 49(4):41–46](https://ils.unc.edu/courses/2018_fall/inls509_001/Reading/Marchionini06.pdf) (via abstract and summary).
- **Quantitative support for clarification [IR EXPERIMENT]**: in Qulac (10K+ question-answer pairs over 198 TREC topics, 762 facets), "asking only one good question leads to over 170% retrieval performance improvement in terms of P@1" in an oracle setting — [Aliannejadi et al. 2019, SIGIR](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06554).
- **Agent behaviour mirrors berrypicking**: on τ-Knowledge, stronger agents re-search as the conversation evolves, while weaker ones retrieve once at the start (vendor-reported, cited in the earlier report). A short loop captures most of the gain: 2 iterations reach 95% of the gain of 5 — [Shaikh 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21553).

### Inferences
- [MY INFERENCE] **The clarify step is Taylor's question negotiation.** The user's prompt is Q4 (compromised). A clarify step should try to recover Q3/Q2, meaning the task context, the decision to be made and the constraints, rather than paraphrase Q4. Ruthven's linguistic markers suggest a cheap classifier for when to clarify: emotive, vague or sensory language signals a pre-formalized need. Qulac's 170% oracle gain is an upper bound, and real clarification gains will be smaller.
- [MY INFERENCE] **ASK supports gap-driven retrieval.** Instead of embedding the query, the agent should represent *what is missing*: unresolved sub-questions and unverified premises. That is what SCOUT's "state-level gap diagnosis" and the earlier report's "decompose into clues, then search" first move do. ASK predicts that query-document similarity will systematically under-serve ill-formed needs, which is an argument for decomposition plus lexical channels.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Bates and Ellis give a tool checklist.** footnote chasing → `related(unit)` and following `source_refs`; citation searching → backlinks; journal run / area scanning → `ls` over taxonomy folders (browse neighbours); subject search → `search` / `define`; author search → filter by `owner`; differentiating → authority-tier filter; monitoring → freshness subscriptions and `review_due`; verifying → the verbatim-quote validator (Airbnb); ending → an explicit stop rule (§1). Bates' "temporary store" is the agent's working set, or scratchpad of flagged evidence. Bates' advice to package each technique as a callable unit matches the "five consolidated tools" finding.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Kuhlthau's "redundancy increases" is an operational stopping signal.** When newly retrieved units mostly duplicate claims already in the working set, the search is at Presentation and should stop. This is the sufficiency counterpart of the MVT rate rule. Her "exploration dip" predicts that early in a multi-hop task the agent's confidence (and any calibrated abstention) should *fall* before it rises. Agents trained to answer early are mis-calibrated in exactly that zone.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Marchionini's lookup/learn/investigate classes can drive routing**: lookup → typed tables or `define`, which is deterministic and exact; learn → taxonomy pages and progressive disclosure; investigate → the iterative hybrid loop with stop rules. The earlier report's split between the "typed database" and the "searchable KB" is the lookup versus learn/investigate split.

### Gaps
- These models were derived from humans (students, social scientists, library users). There is no direct evidence that LLM agents pass through Kuhlthau-like stages, and Srinivasa Ragavan & Alipour (2024) explicitly say the theories need re-validation in chatbot settings.
- I could not access the full texts of Belkin et al. (1982), Ellis (1989) or Kuhlthau (1991). Quotations come from abstracts, the author's own ISP page, and secondary summaries.

## 3. Relevance theory: Saracevic's manifestations, Cooper's logical relevance and utility, Wilson's situational relevance, Borlund, Mizzaro, and "sufficient context"

### Takeaway
Information science treats relevance as **multi-stratified** (system, topical, cognitive, situational, motivational/affective) and **dynamic** (it changes over time with the user's state). Cooper (1971) gave a *formal* definition: logical relevance as membership in a minimal premise set that entails the answer. That definition is essentially today's "sufficient context" notion, and it shows that relevance in multi-hop retrieval is a property of *sets*, not of individual passages. Cooper's "utility" and Wilson's "situational relevance" justify evaluating agent retrieval by task outcome and evidence sufficiency rather than by topical match. LLM judges can approximate human topical labels at scale, with documented prompt sensitivity.

### Cited Findings
- **Saracevic's stratified manifestations [CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK]**: system relevance (query to retrieved objects), topical (query topic to text topic), cognitive or pertinence (the user's state of knowledge and cognitive need to texts), situational (the situation, task or problem to texts), and motivational/affective (intent, goal and motivation to texts). The strata "dynamically interact and are interdependent" — table reproduced in [Rakow 2023, arXiv 2307.14355](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14355); original: [Saracevic 2007, JASIST 58(13):1915–1933, Part II](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.20682), which treats the nature of relevance through meanings, theories and models.
- **Cooper (1971), logical relevance [FORMAL DEFINITION]**: relevant(s, r) iff ∃M (s ∈ M ∧ M ⊨ r ∧ M − {s} ⊭ r). A sentence is relevant if it belongs to some *minimal* premise set entailing a component statement of the need. A document D = {s_1…s_n} is relevant iff some s_i is relevant. The definition gives "a mathematically precise criterion" for yes/no question systems in formalized languages — [Rakow 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14355); original: [Cooper 1971, Information Storage and Retrieval 7(1):19–37](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0271(71)90024-6).
- **Cooper's utility**: logical relevance is separated from utility, "a catch-all concept involving not only topic-relatedness but also quality, novelty, importance, credibility, and many other things" — as quoted in a search summary of Cooper's work ([Cooper 1971](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0271(71)90024-6)). The exact wording should be checked against the original.
- **Wilson (1973), situational relevance**: Wilson refines Cooper's definition with the user's "situation", "stock of information" and "goals" — [Rakow 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14355); original: [Wilson 1973, ISR 9(8):457–471](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0271(73)90096-X).
- **Mizzaro (1997), a four-dimensional framework [CONCEPTUAL]**: relevance is a relation between D1 (document, surrogate, or the information the user receives) and D2 (problem, information need, request, query), along D3 (topic, task, context) and D4 (time, "from the arising problem until its solution") — [Rakow 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14355); original: [Mizzaro 1997, JASIS 48(9):810–832](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-4571(199709)48:9%3C810::AID-ASI6%3E3.0.CO;2-U) (a review of about 160 papers over three periods).
- **Borlund (2003)**: the multidimensionality of relevance explains why "no consensus" is claimed. Situational relevance is "the most realistic type of user relevance", and its dynamic nature is a requirement for interactive IR evaluation — [Borlund 2003, JASIST 54(10):913–925](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.10286).
- **Sufficient context (the LLM-era operationalization)**: Joren et al. define context as sufficient when it has enough information to answer the query, and build an autorater to classify it. Large models (Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5) answer well with sufficient context but "often output incorrect answers instead of abstaining" when it is insufficient. Smaller models hallucinate or abstain even with sufficient context. Context can be *useful but insufficient*. Selective generation using sufficiency signals improved the fraction correct among answered queries by 2–10% — [Joren et al. 2024, arXiv 2411.06037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06037).
- **LLM relevance judges [EMPIRICAL]**: at Bing, LLM labels matched first-party searcher preferences "as good as human labellers", and better than third-party workers at a fraction of the cost. Prompt changes and even simple paraphrases change accuracy — [Thomas et al. 2023, arXiv 2309.10621](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10621). The counter-view: a human–machine collaboration spectrum, a pilot correlation test, and arguments for and against fully automated judgments, ending in a compromise position — [Faggioli et al. 2023, ICTIR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09161).
- In the Airbnb SOP case study, the agent beat production RAG on **evidence sufficiency (83.3% vs 59.8%)** while matching it on guidance — [Bako et al. 2026, arXiv 2610.14428](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14428).

### Inferences
- [MY INFERENCE] **"Sufficient context" is Cooper's logical relevance lifted to the context window.** A retrieved set C is sufficient for answer a iff ∃M ⊆ C with M ⊨ a. Two consequences follow, both provable from the definition. (1) **Relevance is not decomposable over passages.** A bridging passage can be logically relevant (a member of a minimal entailing set) while it is topically unrelated to the query string, and a topically perfect passage can be logically irrelevant if it is redundant (not in any *minimal* M). Pointwise topical ranking therefore cannot guarantee sufficiency on multi-hop questions, which is why iterative, gap-driven retrieval helps there. (2) **Minimality gives a citation criterion.** The cited evidence for an answer should be a minimal entailing set. Supporting claims beyond it are padding, and if any member is removed the claim must become unsupported. This is a concrete check for claim-level evaluation.
- [MY INFERENCE] **The evaluation stack mirrors Saracevic's strata**: system relevance → recall@k and nDCG on the golden set; topical → LLM-judge topicality labels (Thomas et al. show these are usable, Faggioli et al. show they need validation); cognitive/pertinence → novelty against the agent's working set (Wilson's "stock of information": a fact already known adds zero); situational → task success and evidence sufficiency (Airbnb's metric, Joren's autorater, τ-Knowledge pass^k); motivational → whether the answer served the user's actual goal (Taylor's Q1), which can only be measured with user feedback. The earlier report's "golden sets, claim-level diagnostics, calibrated judges and production gap signals" covers strata 1–4. Stratum 5 is a gap.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Mizzaro's time dimension is a reason for applicability metadata.** A unit relevant at t1 may be irrelevant at t2 because the problem changed (D4) or because the document was superseded (D1). The report's validity windows encode the D1 change. The agent's evolving working set encodes the D2/D4 change.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Cooper's utility includes credibility and novelty**, so ranking should combine relevance with authority-tier and freshness priors, but credibility should not be folded into a single learned score. Superseded or low-authority content can be perfectly relevant topically and negative in utility.

### Gaps
- I did not access the full text of Saracevic (2007) or Cooper (1971). The formal Cooper definition is quoted from Rakow (2023), and the Saracevic table comes via Rakow (who cites Saracevic 1996 for the stratified model).
- I found no study that measures inter-annotator agreement on *sufficiency* (as distinct from topical relevance) for agent contexts in enterprise corpora.

## 4. Knowledge organization: faceted classification, thesauri and SKOS, controlled vocabularies versus folksonomies, FRBR/LRM, DIKW critiques, information-as-thing, and Otlet

### Takeaway
Knowledge organization (KO) supplies three things the earlier reports already use without naming. **Facets** (Ranganathan's PMEST) are the hard-filter metadata. **Thesaurus semantics** (ISO 25964/SKOS) are the alias tables. **Otlet's "monographic principle"**, a century-old version of atomic knowledge units, is the unit compiler. The strongest empirical KO evidence favours controlled vocabularies plus aliasing: removing subject headings would lose about 35% of keyword hits, and two people choose the same term less than 20% of the time. FRBR/LRM gives a rigorous four-level model (work, expression, manifestation, item) for versions, supersession and duplicates. WorldCat data show that version complexity is concentrated in about 6% of works. Critiques of DIKW warn against treating "data → information → knowledge" as an upward, inferential pipeline.

### Cited Findings

**4a. Faceted classification [NORMATIVE / DESIGN THEORY]**
- Ranganathan's five fundamental categories, **PMEST**, are Personality (most concrete, partly identified by a residual method), Matter (later split into property, method and material), Energy (actions and processes), Space and Time. Citation order runs from concrete to abstract, and facets are combined "analytico-synthetically" rather than enumerated. The system has 55 canons, 22 principles, 13 postulates and 10 devices. The canons for arrays and chains include **Exhaustiveness, Exclusiveness, Helpful Sequence, Decreasing Extension and Modulation**, and the canons of characteristics require characteristics that differentiate, are relevant and are ascertainable. Definitions: "An isolate is the smallest unit of knowledge in CC; whereas a facet is a group of isolates." Colon Classification itself declined: about 24% of Indian libraries used it in the 1960s and few do now. Its complex notation is blamed, and "success depends more on institutional support than on theoretical quality" — [Satija 2017, ISKO Encyclopedia: Colon Classification](https://isko.org/cyclo/colon_classification).
- Facet analysis sorts terms into mutually exclusive facets, each based on a single characteristic of division. **Critique**: the approach is rationalist and a priori, assumes concepts keep fixed meanings across contexts, specifies its literature methods only vaguely, and has had weak influence on IR. Faceted web design is widespread, often without awareness of this tradition. The CRG's 1955 manifesto proposed facets "as the basis for all information retrieval" — [Hjørland 2013, ISKO Encyclopedia: Facet analysis](https://isko.org/cyclo/facet_analysis).
- **Usability evidence**: with 32 art-history students and 35,000 images, 90% preferred a hierarchical faceted-metadata interface to a baseline search interface — [Yee, Swearingen, Li & Hearst 2003, CHI](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse454/13wi/papers/yee-chi03.pdf). The sample is small and specialist.

**4b. Thesaurus standards and SKOS [STANDARDS]**
- ISO 25964-1 (2011) covers the construction of monolingual and multilingual thesauri for retrieval: concepts, terms, and equivalence, hierarchical and associative relationships. Part 2 (2013) covers mapping to other vocabularies (classification schemes, taxonomies). In the mapping to SKOS, closeMatch corresponds to ISO equivalence, exactMatch to exact equivalence, relatedMatch to associative, and broadMatch/narrowMatch to hierarchical. ISO's **compound equivalence has no SKOS provision**, and SKOS-XL is used where the basic SKOS model lacks an ISO construct — [NISO, ISO 25964 page](https://niso.org/schemas/iso25964) and project-leader slides summarized there.
- SKOS (W3C Recommendation, 18 Aug 2009): prefLabel, altLabel and hiddenLabel are **pairwise disjoint**, and a resource has **at most one prefLabel per language tag**. `skos:broader` is **not** transitive, while `broaderTransitive` is. `skos:related` is symmetric, not transitive, and disjoint with `broaderTransitive`. `exactMatch` is transitive and a sub-property of `closeMatch`, which is not transitive. `exactMatch` is disjoint with `broadMatch` and `relatedMatch` — [W3C SKOS Reference](https://www.w3.org/TR/skos-reference/).

**4c. Controlled vocabularies versus keywords and folksonomies [EMPIRICAL]**
- In 186 catalog keyword searches, the mean share of hits that would be lost without subject headings was **35.9%** (median 30.2%; pooled **35.4% = 36,319 of 102,580 hits**). For 31.7% of searches, half or more of the hits came only from subject fields, and some searches lost 80–100% — [Gross & Taylor 2005, College & Research Libraries 66(3):212–230](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/crl/crl-15726.pdf).
- **The vocabulary problem**: the probability that two people choose the same term for an object was **< 0.20** across five domains. A single designer-chosen term fails 80–90% of the time in common situations, and "unlimited aliasing" gives several-fold improvements — [Furnas, Landauer, Gomez & Dumais 1987, CACM 30(11):964–971](https://doi.org/10.1145/32206.32212) (via abstract).
- **Folksonomies**: on Delicious, after roughly the first 100 bookmarks "each tag's frequency is a nearly fixed proportion" of all tags for a URL. This stabilization is modelled by a Pólya (Eggenberger–Pólya) urn, whose colour fractions converge to a *random* limit. Tagging suffers from polysemy, synonymy and basic-level variation. Of 212 popular URLs, 67% peaked within 10 days and 17% on day 1 — [Golder & Huberman 2006, arXiv cs/0508082](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0508082).

**4d. FRBR / IFLA LRM [CONCEPTUAL MODEL / STANDARD]**
- FRBR Group 1 entities. **Work**: an abstract intellectual creation, and modification requiring significant independent effort creates a new work. **Expression**: the realization of a work in a specific form (text, translation, score). **Manifestation**: the physical embodiment of an expression, and a change of medium, typeface or publisher makes a new manifestation. **Item**: a single exemplar. User tasks are find, identify, select and obtain. Relationships include reproduction, succession and adaptation — [UKOLN FRBR factfile](https://www.ukoln.ac.uk/bib-man/factfile/cataloguing-rules/frbr/index.html).
- IFLA LRM (Riva, Le Bœuf & Žumer, adopted 18 Aug 2017) consolidates FRBR, FRAD and FRSAD for linked data. Its entities: Res (E1) ⊃ Work (E2), Expression (E3), Manifestation (E4), Item (E5), Agent (E6: Person E7, Collective Agent E8), Nomen (E9), Place (E10), Time-span (E11) — [IFLA repository record](https://repository.ifla.org/handle/123456789/42?mode=full); [CIDOC-CRM LRM page](https://cidoc-crm.org/lrmoo/Resources/ifla-library-reference-model).
- **Empirical distribution of version complexity**: in a 1,000-record sample projected to 32M WorldCat works, **78% of works had a single manifestation**, 99% had seven or fewer, and about 1% (≈30,000) had more. "Complex" works (revised, augmented, translated, collected) were about **6%**. Revised, augmented and collected works were 4% of works but more than 12% of manifestations, and were over-represented among the most widely held. FRBR's benefits "could largely come from focusing on a small number of complex works". Grouping manifestations into works lets metadata be inherited: works carrying an LC class number rise from 48% of records to 53% of works, and the chance that a work has a Dewey number rises from 22% (1 manifestation) to 37% and 53% (2 and 3 manifestations) — [Bennett, Lavoie & O'Neill 2003, OCLC](https://www.oclc.org/content/dam/research/publications/library/2003/lavoie_frbr.pdf).

**4e. Information concepts: DIKW critiques, Buckland, Otlet [THEORETICAL]**
- **Rowley (2007)**: textbooks typically define information in terms of data, knowledge in terms of information, and wisdom in terms of knowledge, but "there is less consensus in the description of the processes that transform elements lower in the hierarchy into those above them", and wisdom is rarely discussed — [Rowley 2007, J. Inf. Sci. 33(2):163–180](https://doi.org/10.1177/0165551506070706).
- **Frické (2009)**: DIKW is "unsound and methodologically undesirable". It rests on operationalism and inductivism and faces a dilemma. If only valid inference is allowed, generalizations such as "Most rattlesnakes are dangerous" cannot be information. If inductive inference is allowed, it abandons its "core faith" that data and information are true. Formally, "Data typically is expressed by Existential-Conjunctive logic, information requires the full First Order Logic", and "[s]upposing that [information] can be inferred from [data] is the central mistake of the DIKW pyramid". DIKW also omits "why" questions, which need causal information beyond the data — [Frické 2009, J. Inf. Sci. 35(2):131–142](https://zenodo.org/record/904809) (full text read).
- **Buckland (1991)**: three senses: information-as-process, -as-knowledge and -as-thing (data, text, documents, objects, events regarded as informative). "Whatever information storage and retrieval systems store and retrieve is necessarily 'information-as-thing'" — [Buckland 1991, JASIS 42(5):351–360](https://people.ischool.berkeley.edu/~buckland/thing.html).
- **Otlet's monographic principle**: chunks of text on standardized cards or sheets, organized by the UDC. The aim was to "detach what the book amalgamates, to reduce all that is complex to its elements and to devote a page to each" (Otlet 1918, p. 149). Standardized units made "collaborative, continuous expansibility" and easy excerpting and recombination possible. Rayward sees these as functionally similar to hypertext nodes and links, while noting that Otlet's positivist assumptions differ — [Rayward 1994, JASIS 45(4):235–250](https://monoskop.org/images/f/f8/Rayward_W_Boyd_1998_Visions_of_Xanadu_Paul_Otlet_1968-1944_and_Hypertext.pdf).

### Inferences
- [MY INFERENCE] **The envelope's applicability fields are a PMEST facet scheme**: Personality → `product` / entity / subject of the rule; Matter → property or material, e.g. the account type or data class; Energy → the process or `procedure`; Space → `jurisdiction`; Time → `effective_from/effective_to`. Ranganathan's **canons of exclusiveness and exhaustiveness** are the conditions under which *hard filters are safe*. If facet values overlap (not exclusive), a filter can drop applicable units. If they are not exhaustive, units with "other/unknown" values disappear. The KB linter should check both, for example by requiring an explicit `ALL` or `unspecified` value instead of null. Hjørland's critique implies facet sets should come from query logs and failure analysis, not be fixed a priori.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Gross & Taylor plus Furnas are the strongest LIS evidence for the glossary/alias table.** If roughly a third of keyword hits come only from controlled-vocabulary fields, a KB that drops the alias channel and relies only on body text or embeddings should expect large recall losses on jargon-heavy queries. "Unlimited aliasing" is the SKOS altLabel/hiddenLabel list. Golder & Huberman's urn result says harvested labels (user queries, ticket phrasing) converge to stable proportions after about 100 observations per item, so frequent requester phrasings can be promoted to altLabels once their share stabilizes. The urn's *random* limit means early phrasings get locked in, and controlled prefLabels guard against that path dependence.
- [MY INFERENCE] **SKOS integrity conditions should be lint rules**: one prefLabel per language; pref/alt/hidden disjoint; no `related` between concepts linked by `broaderTransitive`; never treat `skos:broader` as transitive in query expansion unless the scheme opts in; propagate `exactMatch` transitively but not `closeMatch`. The ISO compound-equivalence gap ("USE A + B") needs an extension field in the concept unit.
- [MY INFERENCE] **FRBR/LRM is the right model for "versions are chains, duplicates are collapsed"**: Work = the policy or SOP as an abstract obligation ("Refund policy"); Expression = a specific revision or translation (v3.2-en), where supersession is an Expression → Expression `succeeds` relation inside one Work; Manifestation = a rendering (PDF, HTML page, wiki export); Item = a connector-specific copy (SharePoint file ID, Confluence page ID). Exact and near duplicates are multiple Items or Manifestations of one Expression and should be *collapsed*. Different Expressions must *not* be collapsed. They form a version chain with one current member per (jurisdiction, audience). The OCLC numbers predict that most enterprise documents will have one Expression and that version-chain machinery matters for a few percent of units, which happen to be the high-traffic, high-risk ones. They also justify inheriting metadata across a Work's members, such as owner, taxonomy facet or glossary links.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Frické's argument challenges the "compile upward" narrative.** Rule cards are universal or conditional statements (full FOL), and they cannot be validly *inferred* from the records and tickets of typed tables. Those are existential-conjunctive data. So rule units must be extracted from authoritative normative text, with spans, not induced from operational data. LLM-induced "rules from examples" are inductive and can be false even when every input is true. This supports the earlier report's split between deterministic tables (data) and the searchable KB (information that needs interpretation), and its caution about LLM-invented taxonomies. DIKW's missing "why" also predicts that pure fact stores serve why-questions poorly, which is a reason to keep narrative pages and causal fields such as KCS "cause".
- [MY INFERENCE] **Buckland's information-as-thing is the epistemic basis of the evidence store.** The KB stores *things* (spans, documents), and knowledge exists only in the reader, here the agent. This is why verbatim `source_refs` and quote validation are essential. A synthesized page is a new "thing" whose informativeness depends on its evidence links.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Otlet's monographic principle is the knowledge-unit compiler, and it has a 1918 statement.** Its stated benefits, recombination, excerpting and collaborative expansion, are the benefits claimed for typed units. Its failure mode, UDC notation complexity and institutional dependence, parallels Colon Classification's decline: KOS success depends on maintenance and support more than on theoretical elegance (Satija). That argues for a simple, linted format over an elaborate ontology.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study comparing SKOS-style alias expansion with embedding-only retrieval on an enterprise agent KB. The quantitative evidence is from library catalogs (Gross & Taylor) and from the medical thesaurus expansion already cited in the earlier report.
- ISO 25964 is paywalled. The relationship mapping comes from NISO's summary and the project leader's slides, not from the normative text.
- I did not read the IFLA LRM PDF itself. The entity list comes from the German entity table and the repository record.

## 5. Bibliometric and statistical laws: Zipf, Heaps, Bradford, Lotka, and Price's cumulative advantage

### Takeaway
These are **empirical laws** with partial **derivations**. Zipf (rank-frequency) and Lotka (size-frequency) are equivalent power laws, and Bradford's geometric zones follow from a Zipf-type yield distribution. Heaps' vocabulary growth follows from Zipf under independent sampling. Price's cumulative advantage (a Pólya-urn model, later Barabási–Albert preferential attachment) gives a generative mechanism, though a Zipf fit does not by itself diagnose any mechanism. For agent KBs the consequences are quantitative. About half of all entity mentions fall outside the top 1,000 entities. A majority of distinct terms occur once. Vocabulary grows without bound. Covering the long tail of sources costs geometrically more per relevant item. AI-search citations are concentrated (Gini about 0.5–0.65 per engine in one 2026 audit) but have a long tail of single-cited domains.

### Cited Findings
- **Zipf's law [EMPIRICAL LAW]**: f(r) ∝ r^{−α} with α ≈ 1. Zipf–Mandelbrot: f(r) ∝ (r + β)^{−α} with β ≈ 2.7. "Almost all of the variance in frequencies is fit very well", but language is "only near-Zipfian", with structured residuals. Estimating rank and frequency from the same sample creates correlated errors, so a split-sample (binomial split) method should be used. Zipf curves arise even from random typing, so a fit does not identify the generating mechanism — [Piantadosi 2014, Psychon. Bull. Rev.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4176592/).
- **Heaps' (Herdan–Heaps) law [EMPIRICAL LAW]**: V(n) = K n^β, with K ≈ 10–100 and β ≈ 0.4–0.6 for English. Herdan found it in 1960 and Heaps described it in 1978 (*Information Retrieval: Computational and Theoretical Aspects*, §7.5). It is asymptotically equivalent to Zipf under mild assumptions, and a formal derivation is in van Leijenhorst & van der Weide (2005) — [Wikipedia: Heaps' law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaps%27_law).
- **Zipf ⇒ Heaps [THEOREM-LEVEL DERIVATION + EMPIRICAL CHECK]**: "Heaps' law can be considered as a derivative phenomenon if the system obeys the Zipf's law". The approximate solution is asymptotic for infinite systems. In finite systems the Heaps exponent depends on system size and increases slowly with n. The refined finite-size relation beat the asymptotic one on **34 of 35** datasets (books, 24 journals' keywords, H1N1 cases, PNAS citations) — [Lü, Zhang & Zhou 2010, PLoS ONE](https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.3861).
- **Bradford's law of scattering [EMPIRICAL LAW]**: if journals are ranked by yield on a subject and divided into a nucleus and zones of *equal article yield*, "the numbers of periodicals in the nucleus and succeeding zones will be as 1:n:n²" (Bradford 1948). Bradford found 3 zones with n ≈ 5. Vickery extended this to any number of equal-yield zones. Leimkuhler and Brookes gave logarithmic equations for cumulative yield against rank — [Bates 2002, "Speculations on browsing, directed searching, and linking in relation to the Bradford Distribution", CoLIS4](https://www.pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/bates/articles/SearchingBradford.pdf).
- **Bates' search mapping [DESCRIPTIVE / SPECULATIVE]**: Region 1 (core) suits browsing (journal run). Region 2 is where indexing and database searching pay. In Region 3 relevant items are a "needle in a haystack", and following or creating links (citations, web links) is best. "The core region … [is] the ideal location for browsing, the middle region … for directed searching, and the outer region … for pursuing links" — [Bates 2002](https://www.pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/bates/articles/SearchingBradford.pdf).
- **Caveat on "subject"**: Bradford analyses are ambiguous about what scatters, whether words in texts, concepts in texts, or items useful to a task. How "subject" is operationalized changes the results — [Hjørland & Nicolaisen 2005, CoLIS](https://doi.org/10.1007/11495222_9).
- **Lotka's law [EMPIRICAL LAW]**: x^n · y = c, where y is the number of authors with x papers. Across **48** author-productivity datasets there was "overwhelming conformity" to the general form, but only **7** fitted the inverse-square case n = 2. Pao calls for representative sampling and gives a method for estimating n and c — [Pao 1986, JASIS 37(1):26–33](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.4630370105).
- **Unification**: Lotka's law is equivalent to the Mandelbrot, Zipf, Leimkuhler and Bradford functions (derived for single-source items, with multiple-source systems treated separately) — [Egghe 2005, *Power Laws in the Information Production Process: Lotkaian Informetrics*](https://shop.elsevier.com/books/power-laws-in-the-information-production-process/egghe/978-0-12-088753-8) (via publisher and chapter excerpts).
- **Price's cumulative advantage [GENERATIVE MODEL]**: "success breeds success". It is a modified Pólya urn in which failure "is not punished by increased chance of failure", in contrast to the negative binomial. The resulting distribution is governed by the Beta function, with one free parameter and a limiting inverse-square case. It is proposed as the underlying theory of Bradford, Lotka, Pareto and Zipf and of citation-frequency results, and it yields an obsolescence factor — [Price 1976, JASIS 27(5):292–306](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/price/pricetheory1976.pdf).
- **Preferential attachment**: scale-free degree distributions follow from (i) continuous growth and (ii) preferential attachment of new vertices to well-connected ones — [Barabási & Albert 1999, Science](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9910332).
- **The long tail in LLM knowledge [EMPIRICAL]**: QA accuracy correlates, both correlationally and causally, with the number of pre-training documents mentioning the question's entities. Models would need to scale "by many orders of magnitude" for tail questions, and retrieval reduces this dependence — [Kandpal et al. 2022, arXiv 2211.08411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08411). On PopQA (14k questions), LMs struggle with less popular facts, "scaling fails to appreciably improve memorization … in the long tail", and retrieval-augmented small models outperform much larger unassisted ones. Adaptive retrieval only for tail queries cuts cost — [Mallen et al. 2022, arXiv 2212.10511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511).
- **Rare queries are harder**: search-log analysis shows engines perform worse on rare queries, and sessions show characteristic rare-to-common transitions — [Downey, Dumais & Horvitz 2007, SIGIR poster](https://erichorvitz.com/heads_and_tails_SIGIR.pdf).
- **Source concentration in AI search [EMPIRICAL, recent]**:
  - In 55,936 queries across six LLM search engines and two traditional engines, LLM engines cite *more diverse* domains, with **lower Gini indices than Google and Bing**, and 37% of domains appear only in LLM engines. Wikipedia is about 32% of all domain occurrences and **41% of Gemini's citations**. The strongest predictor of selection is Tranco popularity (LLM engines favour less popular domains), followed by outlinks, HTML semantic structure and readability. LLM engines do not beat traditional ones on credibility, political neutrality or safety — [Zhang et al. 2025, arXiv 2512.09483](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.09483).
  - In 712 queries, 4 engines, 2,848 responses and 26,266 URLs from 7,675 domains, top-25-domain citation share and Gini were: ChatGPT 29.89% / 0.648, Gemini 27.92% / 0.595, Perplexity 31.58% / 0.563, Copilot 15.56% / 0.493 (overall Gini 0.68). **59.1% of domains were cited once and 16.5% twice**, giving "a somewhat narrow set of repeatedly cited domains in the head … [and] a large number of minimally cited domains". About 16% of cited sources were classified as AI-generated — [Allaham & Diakopoulos 2026, arXiv 2605.23684](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23684v1).
  - In vendor tracking of "thousands of prompts" across ChatGPT, Gemini, AI Mode, AI Overviews and Perplexity (week of 1 Feb 2026), the **top 1% of domains held 64% of citations** (top 5% 78%, top 10% 84%), and 96.8% of cited domains showed no week-over-week change (vendor-reported, method not disclosed) — [BrightEdge 2026](https://www.brightedge.com/resources/weekly-ai-search-insights/ai-search-citations-week-to-week-changes).

### Inferences (with derivations)
- [MY DERIVATION] **Zipf ⇒ Heaps, sketch.** Draw N tokens i.i.d. from p_r = r^{−α}/ζ(α), with α > 1. Then E[V(N)] = Σ_r [1 − (1 − p_r)^N] ≈ Σ_r [1 − e^{−N p_r}]. Types with N p_r ≳ 1, i.e. r ≲ r_c = (N/ζ(α))^{1/α}, are almost surely seen and contribute ≈ r_c. The tail contributes ≈ Σ_{r>r_c} N p_r ≈ N r_c^{1−α}/(ζ(α)(α−1)) ∝ r_c. So E[V(N)] = Θ(N^{1/α}), and the **Heaps exponent is β = 1/α**. This matches Lü et al.'s asymptotic solution. **Tension**: α ≈ 1 for words would give β ≈ 1, but measured β is 0.4–0.6. Lü et al. attribute part of the gap to finite-size effects, and steeper tails in large corpora could also contribute (not verified here).
- [MY DERIVATION] **Most distinct terms and entities are rare.** A Zipf rank-frequency exponent α corresponds to a Lotka size-frequency exponent 1 + 1/α (Egghe's equivalence). With α = 1, n(f) ∝ f^{−2}, and the fraction of types seen exactly once is 1/ζ(2) = **6/π² ≈ 0.61** in the infinite limit. This is the same constant as Lotka's law with n = 2, where c = 6/π² gives about 61% of authors with one paper. So most glossary terms, aliases and entities in a corpus appear once or a few times. Kandpal and Mallen show these are exactly where parametric knowledge and, plausibly, embedding quality are weakest.
- [MY DERIVATION] **Rare facts dominate in aggregate.** For α = 1 over N = 10⁶ entities, the share of mentions beyond rank r is 1 − H_r/H_N: 0.64 beyond the top 100, **0.48 beyond the top 1,000**, and 0.32 beyond the top 10,000. Even a perfect "head" cache of 1,000 entities misses about half of entity-bearing queries. This is the formal reason a KB must have lexical, exact and alias channels and must not rely on LLM priors. It also matches the earlier report's finding that dense retrieval fails on jargon and acronym queries.
- [MY DERIVATION] **Bradford follows from a Zipf yield distribution.** If the source at rank r yields y(r) = C/r relevant items, cumulative yield is R(r) = C·H_r ≈ C(ln r + γ), which is the Leimkuhler/Brookes logarithmic form. Equal-yield zones require ln(r_j/r_{j−1}) = constant, i.e. r_j = n·r_{j−1}. Zone j then contains r_{j−1}(n − 1) ∝ n^{j−1} sources, which is 1:n:n². The cost corollary: to reach fraction j/k of relevant items one needs S_j = s_0(n^j − 1)/(n − 1) sources. With n = 5, the three zones need 1, 6 and 31 source-units cumulatively, so the **last third of recall costs 25× the sources per item of the core**.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Bates' three regions map to the KB's three access paths.** Core → the small always-loaded index plus browse (`ls`, folder pages), cheap and high-precision. Middle → the hybrid lexical+dense index (directed search). Tail → `related()` links, backlinks, citation and source-ref chasing, and graph neighbours. This gives a principled reason for the earlier report's combination of "small index + hybrid search + related links" rather than one of them alone. It also predicts *when* graph links pay: for tail items, as with multi-hop and rare-entity questions, which matches the earlier finding that graphs earn their keep on multi-hop and corpus-level questions.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Heaps' law means index and vocabulary maintenance never finish.** dV/dN = βK N^{β−1}, so each new document still adds new terms at a slowly declining rate, and the glossary/alias table must have a continuous "new-term candidate" job (Heaps-rate monitoring). A sudden jump above the fitted Heaps curve after an ingest batch is a cheap anomaly signal for new product lines, a vendor acronym flood or OCR garbage.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Cumulative advantage in KB usage signals.** Fields such as `cited_count` and `involved_count`, and any ranking boost based on them, form a Price/Pólya urn, so usage-weighted ranking will produce Zipfian usage and lock in early winners. Golder & Huberman's random limit shows this. KCS-style reuse metrics should be normalized by exposure, and newly verified units need exploration slots. Otherwise a newer, correct unit cannot overtake an old, often-cited one, which is the stale-page failure in the earlier report.
- [MY INFERENCE] **AI-search citation concentration bears on generative-engine visibility, not on KB internals.** Per-engine Gini of 0.5–0.65 and a 59% single-citation tail mean answer engines show a Bradford-like head plus a long tail. Readability and semantic HTML structure predict selection (Zhang et al.), so the same structural properties the toolkit gives its markdown units (headings, short claims) probably also help external citation. The vendor figure (top 1% → 64%) and the academic figures differ by method and are not comparable.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Lotka for curation load**: if KB authorship follows Lotka with n ≈ 2, about 60% of contributors write one unit and a few owners write most. Owner-based review queues (`owner`, `review_due`) will concentrate on a few experts, creating a bus-factor and review-bottleneck risk that should be monitored.

### Gaps
- I could not access Manning et al.'s IR-book Heaps page (HTTP 503), so I did not use the commonly quoted RCV1 fit.
- No study I found fits Zipf, Heaps or Bradford parameters for *enterprise* corpora of SOPs and policies. The numerical corollaries above assume α ≈ 1 and should be re-fitted per corpus (with Piantadosi's split-sample method).
- Pao's finding (7/48 inverse-square) warns that the n = 2 constant (61%) is illustrative. The exponent must be estimated from data.

## 6. Knowledge management science: SECI, tacit knowledge, communities of practice, KCS foundations and double-loop learning

### Takeaway
Nonaka's SECI model is the standard vocabulary for converting tacit SOP knowledge, but it is a **descriptive and prescriptive framework with weak empirical support** (Gourlay: none of the four modes is supported by evidence that cannot be explained more simply). Brown & Duguid's ethnography-based distinction between canonical and noncanonical practice is the more useful warning. Written SOPs (canonical) systematically omit how work is actually done. KCS operationalizes capture "in the workflow" through a Solve/Evolve double loop, which corresponds to Argyris & Schön's single- and double-loop learning. That correspondence is my analogy, not KCS's stated foundation.

### Cited Findings
- **Nonaka (1994) [DESCRIPTIVE/PRESCRIPTIVE THEORY]**: organizational knowledge is created "through a continuous dialogue between tacit and explicit knowledge". There are four interaction patterns: socialization (tacit→tacit), externalization (tacit→explicit), combination (explicit→explicit) and internalization (explicit→tacit). Individuals create knowledge, and organizations articulate and amplify it — [Nonaka 1994, Organization Science 5(1):14–37](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Nonaka_1994.pdf) (abstract; SECI labels via secondary summary).
- **Gourlay (2006) critique [CRITICAL REVIEW]**: two of the modes appear plausible, "but none are supported by evidence that cannot be explained more simply". The framework omits inherently tacit knowledge and uses a radically subjective definition of knowledge. The paper proposes instead that non-reflective behaviour creates tacit knowledge and reflective behaviour creates explicit knowledge (after Dewey) — [Gourlay 2006, J. Management Studies 43(7):1415–1436](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/339).
- **Brown & Duguid (1991) [ETHNOGRAPHIC THEORY]**: ethnographies show that actual work differs substantially from manuals, training, org charts and job descriptions. Formal descriptions obscure both actual practice and the learning and innovation in informal communities-of-practice. The evidence is Orr's studies of photocopier technicians, whose canonical guidance was stepwise repair guides while their noncanonical practice (shared narratives) bridged the gap to changing machines — [Brown & Duguid 1991, Organization Science 2(1):40–57](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ororsc:v:2:y:1991:i:1:p:40-57) (abstract summarized; Orr details via secondary sources).
- **KCS v6 [PRACTICE STANDARD]**: KCS "creates knowledge in the context of demand or use" and captures it "as a natural by-product of getting the work done". "The collective experience will always be more accurate and complete than any individual's knowledge" — [KCS v6 Practices Guide, intro](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/020/010). The Solve Loop (Capture, Structure, Reuse, Improve) handles individual requests and the Evolve Loop analyzes patterns across them, and "each enables the other". "Capture in the workflow" is where tacit knowledge becomes explicit. "Search early, search often" prevents duplicates. "Reuse is review". "Flag it or fix it". Capture the "requestor's context" in the requester's own words — [KCS v6 Practices Guide, Double Loop](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/030/030).
- **Argyris & Schön (1978) [THEORY OF ACTION]**: single-loop learning changes action strategies while governing variables stay fixed (the thermostat). Double-loop learning revises the governing variables themselves. Learning is "the detection and correction of errors", and few organizations double-loop well — [infed summary of Argyris & Schön](https://infed.org/dir/chris-argyris-theories-of-action-double-loop-learning-and-organizational-learning/).

### Inferences
- [MY INFERENCE] **LLM extraction of SOP documents into procedure graphs is SECI "combination" (explicit→explicit), not externalization.** It cannot recover what Brown & Duguid show the documents omit. The tacit layer lives in tickets, chat threads, escalations and resolution notes, where KCS "capture in the workflow" occurs. So the toolkit's tacit-knowledge path should mine resolved cases into *candidate* articles and procedure deltas, and flag divergences between the canonical step graph and the observed resolution sequences ("noncanonical practice detected"). Divergences should go to an owner rather than be auto-merged.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Gourlay's critique sets a limit**: some knowledge is inherently tacit (non-reflective skill) and will not become text. The agent design needs explicit handoff and escalation steps (already in the procedure step types) rather than an assumption that the KB is complete.
- [MY INFERENCE] **KCS Solve/Evolve ≈ single/double loop.** Solve corrects individual articles (single loop). Evolve changes the governing variables: content standard, taxonomy, unit schema and routing (double loop). For an agent KB, single loop is "flag it or fix it" on a unit after a failed answer. Double loop is changing the *schema or the facets* when clusters of insufficient-context queries show a systematic gap, for example adding a jurisdiction facet. The analogy is mine; the KCS page I read does not cite Argyris.
- [MY INFERENCE] **The "requestor's context" field is the KM-side source of scent and altLabels** (§1, §4). It is the empirical answer to Furnas's vocabulary problem.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study of LLM-assisted *externalization* (tacit→explicit) that measures what fraction of expert know-how ends up in generated SOPs compared with expert interviews.
- The KCS pages I read do not state academic theoretical foundations. The organizational-learning lineage above is my analogy.

## 7. Information quality frameworks: Wang & Strong, ISO/IEC 25012, Pipino–Lee–Wang metrics

### Takeaway
The data-quality literature separates **intrinsic and inherent** quality (accuracy, believability, consistency), which can be stored as unit metadata, from **contextual** quality (relevancy, timeliness, completeness, appropriate amount), which depends on the task and so must be computed at query time. Pipino et al. give simple functional forms, including a timeliness function of currency and volatility, that turn the earlier report's `review_due` and verification intervals into a formula.

### Cited Findings
- **Wang & Strong (1996) [EMPIRICAL FRAMEWORK, consumer-derived]**: 15 dimensions in 4 categories. **Intrinsic**: accuracy, objectivity, believability, reputation. **Contextual**: value-added, relevancy, timeliness, completeness, appropriate amount of data. **Representational**: interpretability, ease of understanding, representational consistency, concise representation. **Accessibility**: accessibility, access security. Method: 25 industry data consumers and 112 MBA students produced 179 attributes; 355 usable responses from 1,500 alumni rated 118 attributes; factor analysis gave 20 dimensions; two sorting studies reduced these to 15 (placement 70% → 81%). "Intrinsic DQ denotes that data have quality in their own right", while contextual DQ "must be considered within the context of the task at hand" — [Wang & Strong 1996, JMIS 12(4):5–33](https://web.mit.edu/tdqm/www/tdqmpub/beyondaccuracy_files/beyondaccuracy.html).
- **ISO/IEC 25012:2008 [STANDARD]**: 15 characteristics from two points of view. **Inherent** quality concerns the data itself (domain values, relationships, metadata), for example accuracy (syntactic and semantic), completeness and consistency. **System-dependent** quality concerns whether quality is reached and preserved within a computer system — [iso25000.com: ISO/IEC 25012](https://iso25000.com/en/iso-25000-standards/iso-25012). Other named characteristics include credibility, currentness, accessibility, availability, confidentiality, compliance and traceability — [arc42 quality model: ISO/IEC 25012](https://quality.arc42.org/standards/iso-iec-25012).
- **Pipino, Lee & Wang (2002) [ASSESSMENT METHOD]**: subjective (questionnaire) and objective (computed) assessments. Aggregation is conservative: a dimension gets a value no higher than its weakest normalized (0–1) indicator, i.e. a min operator. Timeliness is computed as max(0, 1 − …) of currency relative to volatility — [Pipino, Lee & Wang 2002, CACM 45(4):211–218](https://doi.org/10.1145/505248.506010) (partially verified via search excerpts; see Gaps).

### Inferences
- [MY INFERENCE] **Which quality fields belong in the unit envelope**: intrinsic and inherent dimensions (accuracy/verification status, believability → `authority_tier`, reputation → source tier, consistency → conflict flags) and accessibility (ACL, sensitivity) are static and belong in the envelope, which the earlier blueprint already does. Contextual dimensions (relevancy, completeness for *this* question, appropriate amount) cannot be stored. They are the relevance and sufficiency judgments of §3 and must be computed per query by the evaluator or judge. This is a principled boundary for the schema.
- [MY INFERENCE] **A timeliness formula for review scheduling.** Using the Pipino form T = max(0, 1 − currency/volatility)^s, where currency is the time since last verification, volatility is the expected shelf life and s ≥ 0 controls strictness, a unit should be queued for review when T < θ, i.e. at currency = volatility·(1 − θ^{1/s}). Volatility can be set per unit type or from the CRAG-style dynamism classes and Guru-style intervals cited in the earlier report. The formula makes those intervals derivable instead of arbitrary.
- [MY INFERENCE] **Min-aggregation matches "fail closed".** A unit's quality should be bounded by its weakest critical indicator (for example, an ACL sync older than the SLA makes the unit ineligible even if its content is perfect), rather than averaged.
- [MY INFERENCE] The ISO inherent/system-dependent split corresponds to the toolkit's split between content linting (inherent: accuracy against spans, completeness of required fields, consistency across a version chain) and platform service levels (system-dependent: availability, recoverability, ACL freshness, traceability through lineage fields).

### Gaps
- I could not read the full text of Pipino et al. (2002). The exponent form `^s` and the exact simple-ratio and weighted-average definitions are from my knowledge of the paper, and the excerpts I saw confirm only the min-aggregation and the max(0, 1 − …) timeliness shape.
- I could not access the normative ISO/IEC 25012 text, so the assignment of all 15 characteristics to inherent, system-dependent or both is not verified here.
- I found no study validating Wang & Strong's dimensions for LLM-consumed (as opposed to human-consumed) knowledge units. Whether "concise representation" or "interpretability" matter in the same way for models is untested.
