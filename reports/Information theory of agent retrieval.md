# Proofs Back the Evidence Store, Not the Ranker

Yes: almost every method in the earlier reports can be tied to a formal result from information theory, IR theory, decision and statistical theory, or information science. The support, however, is uneven. **The data-layer recommendations rest on published theorems.** Keeping raw evidence with span-level provenance is the only representation sufficient for every future question, by the data processing inequality and the theory of sufficient statistics. Long-tail enterprise facts must live in an external store, because a calibrated model's closed-book error on such facts is at least the fraction of facts seen once in training ([Kalai & Vempala](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14648)) and a $B$-bit model must lose every fact beyond roughly $B/\log M$ ([Nichani et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06538)). Normalized schemas are exactly the information-maximal designs ([Arenas & Libkin](https://marceloarenas.cl/publications/jacm05.pdf)). SHA-256 content IDs and Merkle-root snapshot IDs are safe by the birthday bound. Fellegi–Sunter thresholds minimize the human-review band. Split conformal and conformal risk control give finite-sample review thresholds. Prediction-powered inference (PPI) makes a few hundred human labels a valid calibration for LLM judges, and provenance semirings let one stored polynomial answer every provenance question. **The retrieval-side methods rest mostly on bounds and empirical laws.** Johnson–Lindenstrauss, margin and LIMIT-style results explain why a lexical channel cannot be dropped. BM25 is a fitted approximation to a 2-Poisson model. Structure-led chunking and the two-to-three-round search loop are empirical findings whose shape theory explains but whose numbers it does not derive. Zipf, Heaps, Bradford and Lotka price the long tail. **Several defaults have no theorem at all:** reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) with $k=60$, the 256–512-token chunk size, maximal marginal relevance (MMR), one-level progressive disclosure, and SECI-style knowledge conversion. **The theory also corrects the reports in nine places.** LIMIT proves a dimension bound only under a margin or finite precision. The Probability Ranking Principle (PRP) is optimal only for additive metrics, so the final context-assembly step is a set problem. RRF has no optimality theorem, and tuned linear fusion beats it. Repeated LLM calls converge confidently to the wrong value when the model is systematically wrong, so cross-call disagreement is a routing signal, not a vote. Conformal guarantees are marginal and void after any extractor change. Fellegi–Sunter optimality is pairwise, so clusters need a deterministic global step. HNSW-class indexes have no worst-case recall guarantee. Binary-graded evaluations make abstention never optimal. And a Bloom filter used as a dedup check silently drops new documents. Most links between a theorem and a toolkit method are our inferences, not claims made by the theorems' authors, and each one is labelled below.

*Evidence base.* This report synthesizes five research tracks: information-theory foundations, IR theory, information science, decision and statistical theory, and LLM capacity and RAG theory. It also reuses the four earlier reports: *Deterministic retrieval data conversion* (here DRC), *Searchable agent knowledge base construction* (SKB), *How AI research agents retrieve information* (RA) and *Agent ready data lake toolkit* (ADL). The five manifests contain 490 entries, which reduce to **471 distinct sources (434 papers and 37 web sources)** after de-duplicating by arXiv ID, DOI and normalized URL. The combined source catalog for the project is in `papers/README.md` and `papers/manifest.csv`. PDFs are kept locally only, because many licenses forbid redistribution, and are rebuilt by passing the research-notes folders (including `research_notes/Information theory of agent retrieval`) to `scripts/download_papers.py`. Several theorems were read through textbooks, surveys or restatements rather than the original papers. Fellegi–Sunter (1969), Robertson (1977), Kolahi–Libkin (2006) and Dwork et al. (2001) are among them, and this is noted where it matters. Many 2025–2026 sources are unreviewed arXiv preprints. Status is as of November 2026.

## Three kinds of claim, kept apart

Every formal statement below carries one of three labels. **[P] (published)** means a source states the result and proves it, and the citation points to that proof. **[S] (our sketch)** means a derivation written in the research notes. It is either a standard textbook argument written out, a corollary we proved, or arithmetic applied to a published bound. It has not been peer-reviewed. **[I] (inferred mapping)** means the link from a theorem to a toolkit method is ours. The theorem's authors did not make it, and the mapping is only as good as the fit between the theorem's assumptions and the toolkit's setting. Most mappings in this report are [I]. That matters because a theorem never certifies a method directly. It certifies a model of the method, and the "what breaks" paragraphs say where the model and the method part ways.

The cross-walk table also gives each result a **type**. A *theorem* is a mathematical result that follows from stated assumptions. A *bound* is a theorem that limits performance rather than prescribing a method. An *empirical law* is a fitted regularity, such as Zipf's law or the vocabulary problem, that holds widely and can fail. A *descriptive model* is a qualitative account built from observation, such as berrypicking or Kuhlthau's search process. A *specification* is a documented system guarantee, such as SQL ordering semantics. The verdict column says whether the result **justifies** a method, **bounds** it (says when or how far it works), or **corrects** the earlier report's wording or default.

## Raw evidence is the only representation sufficient for every future question

**The method.** DRC parses each source into a provenance-bearing intermediate representation and grounds every extracted value to a source span. SKB keeps an immutable evidence store under the canonical document tree. DRC's third invariant is that every answer row's evidence span re-slices from the content-hashed source to the stored quote.

**Theorem (data processing inequality) [P].** If $X \to Y \to Z$ is a Markov chain, then $I(X;Z) \le I(X;Y)$, with equality iff $X \to Z \to Y$ ([Polyanskiy & Wu, Thm 3.7](https://people.lids.mit.edu/yp/homepage/data/itbook-export.pdf); [Cover & Thomas, Thm 2.8.1](https://doi.org/10.1002/047174882X)). *Proof sketch.* Expand $I(X;Y,Z)$ by the chain rule in both orders. Markovity makes $I(X;Z\mid Y)=0$, so $I(X;Y) = I(X;Z) + I(X;Y\mid Z) \ge I(X;Z)$. *Mapping [I].* Model ingestion as $Y \to S \to P \to C \to Z$. Here $Y$ is any question the agent may later need answered, $S$ the source bytes, $P$ the parse (for example a DoclingDocument), $C$ the chunks or typed records, and $Z$ a summary, wiki page or embedding. Each stage is a function, possibly randomized like an LLM, of the previous stage alone. Then $I(Y;Z) \le I(Y;C) \le I(Y;P) \le I(Y;S)$ for every $Y$ at once. No chunker, extractor or summarizer can add information about the source. Anything in $Z$ that is not in $S$ is either noise (hallucination) or knowledge from the model's parameters, and that needs separate provenance.

**Theorem (sufficiency) [P] and corollary [S].** $T=f(X)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ iff $I(\theta;X)=I(\theta;T)$ ([Polyanskiy & Wu, Thm 3.9](https://people.lids.mit.edu/yp/homepage/data/itbook-export.pdf)). *Corollary (our elementary proof).* If $T=f(S)$ is sufficient for every question in a class that contains $Y=S$ itself, then $I(S;T)=H(S)$. So $H(S\mid T)=0$ and $f$ is injective on the support. **Only a lossless representation is universally sufficient.** A typed record is sufficient for the questions its schema anticipates and for nothing else that can be guaranteed. The pair (record, span pointer into a retained $S$) is lossless with respect to $S$, which is exactly what DRC's "re-sliceable evidence" invariant buys [I]. It also explains why DRC must keep a routed fallback to raw text and promote recurring fallback questions into the schema. The schema is a sufficient statistic for a finite question class, and new questions fall outside it.

**Theorem (Fano's inequality) [P].** If $X \to Y \to \hat X$ and $P_e = P[X \neq \hat X]$, then $I(X;Y) \ge (1-P_e)\log(1/P_{\max}) - h(P_e)$, where $P_{\max} = \max_x P_X(x)$ ([Polyanskiy & Wu, Thm 3.12](https://people.lids.mit.edu/yp/homepage/data/itbook-export.pdf)). *Proof sketch.* Apply the DPI for divergence to the error indicator $\mathbf 1\{X\neq\hat X\}$. *Arithmetic [S].* To identify one of $N=10^7$ equiprobable units with error at most 0.1, the query plus filters must carry about $0.9 \times 23.25 - 0.47 \approx$ **20.5 bits** about the unit ID. A pre-filter over eight equiprobable document types supplies at most 3 of them. *Mapping [I].* Combined with the DPI, Fano gives an error floor for "answer from records only" designs: if $H(Y\mid Z) > H(Y\mid S)$, no prompt or model reading only $Z$ can remove the gap. It also quantifies why exact identifiers (policy IDs, CVE numbers) and typed facets help: they are cheap, high-certainty bits.

**Theorem (strong DPI) [P].** For a noisy channel the contraction coefficient $\eta = \sup I(U;Z)/I(U;X)$ over $U \to X \to Z$ can be strictly below 1, and per-channel coefficients compose into end-to-end bounds along chains and Bayesian networks ([Polyanskiy & Wu 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.06025)). *Mapping [I].* For $k$ stochastic re-summarizations, $I(Y;Z_k) \le (\prod_i \eta_i)\, I(Y;S)$. Information about the source decays geometrically with summary depth. This is a formal reason to distrust deep RAPTOR-style trees and wiki pages written from other summaries, and to build navigation trees from the document's own headings, which lose nothing, as SKB recommends. *What breaks.* $\eta=1$ for deterministic injective maps, and nobody has measured $\eta$ for an LLM summarizer, so the geometric decay is a bound, not an observation.

**The caveat that keeps derived views alive [P].** The DPI is about Shannon information under an optimal decoder. Xu et al. define *V-information* for an observer restricted to a function class and prove that, "in violation of the data processing inequality, V-information can be created through computation" ([Xu et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10689)). A bounded reader such as an LLM with finite context can extract more *usable* information from a processed view than from raw text. That is what the measurements show. An information-bottleneck context filter raised Natural Questions exact match to **21.5 with 12.7 words**, against 14.7 with 517.6 words of raw top-5 passages and 16.2 with no retrieval ([Zhu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01549)). The two results together give the design rule [I]. Store $S$ immutably, treat parses, chunks, records and summaries as recomputable views, and judge each view by the usable information it adds for the deployed agent, measured as golden-set accuracy. Shannon information is the wrong yardstick for a view, because no view can ever exceed the raw store on it. Measuring "how much information a chunk keeps" with neural mutual-information estimators is also statistically hopeless: any distribution-free high-confidence lower bound from $N$ samples is $O(\ln N)$ ([McAllester & Stratos](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04251)).

### Provenance semirings answer every lineage question from one stored polynomial

**Theorem (Green, Karvounarakis & Tannen 2007) [P].** Annotate tuples with elements of a commutative semiring $K$. A map $h:K\to K'$ commutes with every positive relational algebra query, $q(h(R)) = h(q(R))$, iff $h$ is a semiring homomorphism (Prop. 3.5). Every RA⁺ query factors as $q(R) = \mathrm{Eval}_v \circ q(\bar R)$ over provenance polynomials $\mathbb N[X]$ (Thm 4.3), with an analogue for datalog ([GKT 2007](https://web.cs.ucdavis.edu/~green/papers/pods07.pdf)). *Proof sketch.* RA⁺ operators use only $+$ (union, projection) and $\cdot$ (join), and homomorphisms preserve both, so commutation follows by structural induction on the query. For the converse, single-operator queries force $h$ to preserve $+$ and $\cdot$. *Mapping [I].* DRC's "where, why and how" provenance becomes a compute-once artifact. Store one $\mathbb N[X]$ polynomial per SQL or graph answer over extraction-fact IDs, each $x$ being one extracted field with its span. Later questions are then answered by applying a homomorphism, without re-running the query. Mapping to why-provenance gives "which spans support this answer". The Boolean semiring with $x_S=0$ gives "is it still supported if we distrust source $S$". The counting semiring gives "how many derivations". The tropical (Viterbi) semiring gives "minimum trust along the best derivation". Why-provenance alone cannot tell alternative derivations apart, while $\mathbb N[X]$ can ([GKT 2007](https://web.cs.ucdavis.edu/~green/papers/pods07.pdf); [Buneman, Khanna & Tan](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-44503-X_20)). *What breaks.* Aggregates need the semimodule extension ([Amsterdamer, Deutch & Tannen](https://doi.org/10.1145/1989284.1989302)). Negation and difference are outside the positive algebra. No semiring theory covers derivations produced by LLM semantic operators. A demonstration with how-provenance over span-linked RDF found extraction and query errors "even when the final answer is correct" in 400 multi-hop questions ([GraphRAGProv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37237)).

## Freezing extractions is forced by arithmetic, not by taste

**The method.** DRC runs the LLM once at ingestion "as a non-deterministic compiler", memoizes outputs on (document IR, schema, prompt, model, parameters, code version), publishes immutable snapshots, and requires every query to carry a snapshot ID and a total `ORDER BY` with a primary-key tiebreaker.

**Floating-point non-associativity [P].** Rounding to nearest gives relative error at most machine epsilon $\varepsilon$ per operation, and addition is not associative. Goldberg's example: $((2\times10^{-30}+10^{30})-10^{30})-10^{-30}$ equals $10^{-30}$ exactly, but IEEE arithmetic returns $-10^{-30}$ ([Goldberg 1991](https://docs.oracle.com/cd/E19957-01/806-3568/ncg_goldberg.html)). Summation error depends on the reduction tree: naive recursive summation has worst-case error $O(n\varepsilon)\sum|x_i|$, while pairwise summation has $|E_n| \le \frac{\varepsilon\log_2 n}{1-\varepsilon\log_2 n}\sum|x_i|$ ([Higham 1993](https://doi.org/10.1137/0914050)). **The measured mechanism.** The LLM forward pass is run-to-run deterministic for a fixed batch. RMSNorm, matmul and attention kernels, however, change their reduction order with batch size, and batch size depends on other users' load. One prompt at temperature 0 gave **80 distinct completions in 1,000 runs**. Batch-invariant kernels made all 1,000 identical, at 42 s against 26 s for default vLLM ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). "Deterministic" settings still varied accuracy by up to 15% across runs ([Atil et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667)). Under bf16 greedy decoding, accuracy varied by up to 9% and response length by 9,000 tokens across GPU count, type and batch size ([Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09501)).

**Why a tiny perturbation becomes a different answer [S].** Write the step-$t$ logits under reduction schedule $\sigma$ as $z_t(\sigma) = z_t^* + \eta_t(\sigma)$, with $\|\eta_t\|$ of order $\varepsilon$ times a conditioning factor, by the summation bounds. Greedy decoding flips a token whenever the top-two gap $\Delta_t < 2\|\eta_t\|_\infty$. If near-ties occur independently across steps, $P(\text{identical } T\text{-token output}) \le \prod_t \big(1 - P(\Delta_t < 2\|\eta_t\|_\infty)\big)$, which decays geometrically in $T$. After the first flip the next input differs, nothing contracts the divergence, and the outputs part ways. *Mapping [I].* Hosted APIs expose no kernel or batch control. Batch invariance does not survive a weight update, which changes $z^*$ itself. And the hallucination lower bounds below mean a perfectly repeatable extractor still errs at a non-zero rate. Persisting each extraction with its fingerprint and querying the snapshot is therefore the only design that delivers DRC's "identical repeat answers" invariant. **Correction:** "temperature 0" should be documented as *low variance*, not deterministic. Batch-invariant serving is still worth enabling for self-hosted shadow re-extraction, so that the diffs ADL wants to publish measure model and prompt changes rather than numerics.

**Snapshot isolation is not serializability [P].** Snapshot isolation (SI), defined by start and commit timestamps plus first-committer-wins, "is non-serializable" because of write skew. It is strictly stronger than READ COMMITTED and incomparable with REPEATABLE READ ([Berenson et al. 1995](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0701157)). Even a read-only transaction under SI can see a state that no serial order produces ([Fekete, O'Neil & O'Neil 2004](https://doi.org/10.1145/1031570.1031573)). Recognizing serializable histories is NP-complete ([Papadimitriou 1979](https://doi.org/10.1145/322154.322158)). Iceberg-style tables, by specification, make "all table changes occur in a linear history of atomic table updates", and readers "always use a consistent snapshot" ([Apache Iceberg](https://iceberg.apache.org/docs/latest/reliability/)). *Mapping [I].* A read-only query over one committed snapshot in a linear history sees a single serial state, so the SI anomalies, which need concurrent reads and writes over a mutable store, cannot arise. The read-only anomaly is a warning about the *operational* store. Agents that read a live OLTP database under SI while ingestion writes can observe impossible states, so they should read pinned lake snapshots or run at SERIALIZABLE via serializable SI ([Cahill, Röhm & Fekete](https://doi.org/10.1145/1376616.1376690); [Ports & Grittner](https://arxiv.org/abs/1208.4179)).

**Total ordering is a specification, not a theorem.** PostgreSQL documents that rows come back "in an unspecified order" without an explicit sort, and that `LIMIT` without an `ORDER BY` imposing a unique order returns "an unpredictable subset" ([PostgreSQL](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/queries-limit.html)). Lucene breaks score ties by internal document IDs assigned at multi-threaded index time, so rankings differ across index instances until ties are broken by external IDs ([Lin & Yang](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.05798)). *Mapping [I].* The primary-key tiebreaker belongs on search results too, not only on SQL. A formal SQL semantics exists as a reference for what "the same query" means ([Guagliardo & Libkin](https://doi.org/10.14778/3151113.3151116)). *What breaks.* Volatile functions (`now()`, `random()`) and parallel floating-point aggregation. Higham's bounds explain why a parallel `SUM` can differ across plans, but no source we read proves conditions under which parallel query plans are bit-reproducible. Byte-identical checksums therefore need a fixed aggregation order or exact (decimal or integer) arithmetic for money-like fields.

## Long-tail facts must come from a store, by two independent lower bounds

**The method.** SKB and ADL keep enterprise knowledge in external typed units and records. DRC makes the database, not the model, the source of truth. SKB adds alias tables and an append-and-invalidate lifecycle rather than fine-tuning facts into weights.

**Theorem (Kalai & Vempala, STOC 2024) [P].** Training documents are i.i.d. from a distribution over "factoids", error-free, with at most one factoid each. The model is calibrated in a binned sense with miscalibration $\mathrm{Mis}_b$. Facts are $s$-sparse among plausible strings, and the world is "regular" (unobserved factoids are on average equally likely to be true). Then with probability at least $1-\delta$ over the training set, the hallucination rate satisfies $g(H) \ge \widehat{MF} - \mathrm{Mis}_b - 3e^{-s}/\delta - \sqrt{6\ln(6/\delta)/n}$, where $\widehat{MF}$ is the fraction of facts that appear exactly once in training ([Kalai & Vempala](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.14648)). *Proof sketch.* Calibration forces the model to put roughly the unobserved probability mass $p(U)$ on unobserved factoids. Regularity and sparsity make almost all of those hallucinations. Good–Turing concentration gives $|\widehat{MF} - p(U)| = \tilde O(1/\sqrt n)$ ([Good 1953](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/40.3-4.237)), and Markov's inequality turns the expectation into a high-probability statement. A matching upper bound shows the bound cannot be pushed higher for nearly calibrated models. The authors state its reach themselves: it covers "arbitrary" facts, not systematic ones like arithmetic or facts repeated many times, and it "justifies the mitigation of consulting a fact database at generation time". A 2025 preprint extends this to prompts with an "I don't know" option: $\mathrm{err} \ge sr - 2/\min_c|E_c| - (35+6\ln N)/\sqrt N - \delta$, where $sr$ is the fraction of prompts seen once ([Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)).

**Theorem (Nichani, Lee & Bietti, ICLR 2025) [P].** Let answers $f^*(x)$ be i.i.d. uniform on $[M]$, keys queried with frequencies $p(x)$ sorted decreasingly, and the learner write a $B$-bit model. Then $\mathbb E[L] \ge \log M \cdot \sum_{x \ge \lceil B/\log M\rceil} p(x)$. Zero loss requires $B \ge N\log M$, and under a power law $p(x)\propto x^{-\alpha}$ the loss is $\gtrsim B^{1-\alpha}$ ([Nichani et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06538)). *Proof sketch.* $f^* \to F \to \hat f$ is a Markov chain, so the DPI gives $I(f^*;\hat f) \le H(F) \le B$. Each key whose answer $F$ does not determine costs $\log M$ in cross-entropy, so the best a learner can do is spend its bits on the $\sim B/\log M$ most frequent keys and lose the tail. **Measured capacity agrees in order of magnitude.** GPT-2-family models peak at about **2 bits per parameter** of extractable knowledge after 1,000 exposures per fact, never above 2.3. At 100 exposures the ratio is still at least 1. When 7/8 of training tokens are junk, capacity for the useful data falls by up to 20× ([Allen-Zhu & Li, Physics 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405)). An independent definition gives about 3.6 bits per parameter of raw memorization ([Morris et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24832)).

**Storage is not extraction [P, empirical].** Knowledge stored without paraphrase augmentation can be memorized yet give 0% QA accuracy ([Allen-Zhu & Li 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14316)). Inverse knowledge search is "virtually 0%, regardless of the prompts" ([Allen-Zhu & Li 3.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14402)). GPT-4 answered celebrity-to-parent questions 79% of the time and parent-to-celebrity 33% of the time, but "if 'A is B' appears in-context, models can deduce the reverse" ([Berglund et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12288)). Retrieval enters the risk bound directly: in a classification model with a learned retriever, a data-store coverage term $c_I|I|^{-\gamma_I}$ shrinks as the store grows, while store size costs only $\log|I|$ in generalization ([Basu, Rawat & Zaheer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15399)). The computability line agrees on the escape. Every computable LLM hallucinates on some computable ground truth by diagonalization, but the authors call the theorem "inapplicable" when the model receives external information about the ground truth ([Xu, Jain & Kankanhalli](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11817)).

*Mapping [I].* Most enterprise facts (a renewal date, a clause's effective date, a part number) are arbitrary facts seen once or never in pre-training, so $sr\approx1$ and closed-book error is near 1 for a calibrated model. Their answer spaces are large (IDs, dates, amounts), so $\log M$ is large and Theorem 7's tail term bites early. This is the strongest formal basis in the whole project for the external typed store. Two corollaries follow. First, fine-tuning facts into weights is the wrong write path. New facts learned by fine-tuning "linearly increase the model's tendency to hallucinate" ([Gekhman et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05904)), and later facts overwrite low-frequency ones ([Lu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15720)). Second, inverse lookups (value → entity, alias → canonical ID) should be indexes, with the forward fact put in context, which is SKB's alias-table-first design. *What breaks.* The bounds assume error-free training data and one phrasing per fact. Head facts are a different regime: closed-book T5 "performs competitively" with retrieval systems on standard QA ([Roberts et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08910)). Most importantly, the escape is conditional on the store being right. A stale or wrong value in the store moves the hallucination into the data layer, which is why quality gates and validity filters are not optional add-ons. No theorem yet bounds end-to-end RAG error as a function of the singleton rate *and* retrieval recall.

## Normal forms and MDL decide which structure earns a place

**The method.** DRC freezes a closed, versioned schema contract, "lets profiling and lookups decide structure", and puts deterministic checks first. SKB and ADL ask for taxonomy and glossary layers. ADL calls schema induction "research-grade". SKB's envelope separates identity, applicability and governance fields from type-specific bodies.

**Theorem (Arenas & Libkin, JACM 2005) [P].** For an instance $I$, a position $p$ and a domain $[1,k]$, let $E_k(I,p)$ be uniform over the values that can replace $p$ without violating the constraints $\Sigma$. A schema with functional dependencies (resp. FDs plus multivalued dependencies) is in BCNF (resp. 4NF) iff $H(E_k(I,p)) > 0$ for every $k>1$, instance and position (Thm 3.1). With the refined relative measure $\mathrm{INF}_I(p\mid\Sigma) \in [0,1]$, a specification is "well designed" (every position has $\mathrm{INF}=1$) iff it is in 4NF under FDs and MVDs, or BCNF under FDs alone (Thm 4.22). A BCNF decomposition step never decreases information (Thm 6.1) ([Arenas & Libkin](https://marceloarenas.cl/publications/jacm05.pdf)). *Proof sketch of Thm 3.1.* If every determinant is a key, replacing $p$ by a fresh value $k+1$ cannot violate an FD, so entropy is positive. If some non-key $X\to A$ holds, a two-tuple instance forces $t_1[A]$, so its entropy is 0. **The price of keeping dependencies local [P].** On the same scale, every dependency-preserving normal form has worst-case redundancy at least 1/2, and 3NF achieves exactly 1/2 ([Kolahi & Libkin](https://doi.org/10.1145/1670243.1670248); verified at abstract level only). *Mapping [I].* The canonical record store behind DRC's schema contract should be in BCNF/4NF. Multi-valued facts, such as a rule card's many applicability tags, belong in separate relations, not repeated columns. SKB's breadcrumb prefix and repeated document metadata on each chunk are positions with zero information under the dependency chunk → document title. They are justified for retrieval (they supply Fano bits cheaply) but belong in regenerable views. In the canonical facts table they would create update anomalies, such as stale titles or effective dates on some chunks. SKB already regenerates views from units, so this is a confirmation with a reason attached.

**Dependencies are entropy constraints [P].** Under the uniform distribution over a relation's tuples, $X\to Y$ holds iff $H(Y\mid X)=0$, and the MVD $X \twoheadrightarrow Y \mid Z$ holds iff $I(Y;Z\mid X)=0$ ([Kenig & Suciu](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.09987), reviewing Lee 1987 and [Dalkilic & Robertson](https://doi.org/10.1145/335168.336059)). Kenig & Suciu prove that exact implications among FDs and MVDs relax to approximate ones, $h(\tau) \le \lambda\, h(\Sigma)$, with a factor at most quadratic in the number of variables and factor 1 when the consequent is an FD. They also give a repair bound. *Repair bound, with our one-line proof [S].* For a key $X$ with $XY=\Omega$, $H(XY)=\log|R|$ and $H(X)\le\log|\Pi_X R|$, so if $h(Y\mid X)=c$ then $|R|/|\Pi_X R| \le 2^c$, and at most $(2^c-1)|\Pi_X R|$ tuples need repair. *Mapping [I].* DRC's L1 checks can become graded. Measure $h(Y\mid X)$ for every declared key and FD on each snapshot of LLM-extracted tables. A non-zero value bounds the number of conflicting records, and by relaxation it bounds violations of implied dependencies. *What breaks.* Plug-in entropy is biased upward and "indicates" dependencies between independent columns at high dimension. Mandros et al. give a bias-corrected "reliable fraction of information" with an optimistic estimator for exact top-$k$ discovery ([Mandros, Boley & Vreeken](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09391)), and approximate acyclic-schema mining scales to a million rows ([Kenig et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.12933)). Small extraction batches need the corrected estimator or a held-out check. No work applies the Arenas–Libkin measure to JSON or document stores. The XML normal form is the nearest analogue.

**Minimum description length [P, principle].** MDL picks the hypothesis minimizing $L(H) + L(D\mid H)$, with $L(D\mid H) = -\log P(D\mid H)$ ([Grünwald](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0406077)). Its ideal limit, Kolmogorov complexity, is incomputable. A counting argument shows incompressible strings exist: there are $2^n$ strings of length $n$ but only $2^n-1$ shorter programs ([Li & Vitányi](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-11298-1)). Practical MDL comes with optimality relative to a chosen encoding. Li & Abe's algorithm "provably obtains the optimal tree cut model" of a thesaurus under MDL ([Li & Abe](https://arxiv.org/abs/cmp-lg/9507011)). Krimp's code tables return itemset collections "up to seven orders of magnitude" smaller than frequent-itemset mining ([Vreeken, van Leeuwen & Siebes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-010-0202-x)). KGist learns graph rules that best compress a knowledge graph, and rule exceptions located "up to 93% of missing entities" ([Belth et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10412)). *Mapping [I].* MDL gives ADL's "research-grade" schema induction an objective: keep a type, field, rule template or taxonomy level iff it shortens the total description of the corpus. Li & Abe's tree cut chooses which level of a category tree to expose as facets. KGist-style exceptions become type-conditional completeness gates, for example "entities of type Policy normally have an owner and an effective date". The score is model-free, so it complements golden-set gains rather than replacing them. **A two-part warning [P].** With arithmetic coding, a model's log-loss is its code length. Chinchilla 70B compresses enwik9 to 8.3% of raw size, but to **14,008%** once its parameters are counted ([Delétang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10668)). An LLM-written "compressed wiki" of a small corpus is therefore not a compression of it. Its value is usability (V-information), not information. *What breaks.* Every MDL result is relative to its encoding, which is fine for ranking candidate schemas but never certifies "the true schema". No study applies MDL schema selection to LLM-extracted records.

**Rules cannot be induced from data [logical argument].** Frické shows that "data typically is expressed by Existential-Conjunctive logic, information requires the full First Order Logic", and that supposing information "can be inferred from" data "is the central mistake of the DIKW pyramid" ([Frické 2009](https://zenodo.org/record/904809)). *Mapping [I].* Rule cards are universal or conditional statements. They cannot be validly inferred from the records and tickets in typed tables, which are existential-conjunctive. Rule units must be extracted, with spans, from authoritative normative text. LLM-induced "rules from examples" are inductive and can be false even when every input is true. This supports SKB's split between deterministic tables and the searchable knowledge base, and its caution about LLM-invented taxonomies.

**Facets are safe hard filters only under two canons [normative, I].** Ranganathan's canons require arrays of facet values to be *exclusive* and *exhaustive* ([Satija, ISKO](https://isko.org/cyclo/colon_classification)). These are exactly the conditions under which SKB's hard pre-filters on `jurisdiction`, `audience`, `product` and validity are safe [I]. Overlapping values make a filter drop applicable units, and non-exhaustive values make "other" or null units disappear. The KB linter should require an explicit `ALL` or `unspecified` value rather than null. Hjørland's critique that facet analysis is a priori ([Hjørland, ISKO](https://isko.org/cyclo/facet_analysis)) implies the facet set itself should be derived from query logs and failure analysis.

## Summaries are query-agnostic codes, so they navigate rather than answer

**The methods.** SKB recommends simple segmentation along the document's own structure, merged to about 256–512 tokens, a free heading-path prefix, one short summary per document, summary trees treated as navigation, "match small, read large", and no semantic, LLM or late chunking by default. ADL reports chunking evidence that "favors simple structure plus context".

**Theorem (rate–distortion) [P].** For a memoryless source with separable distortion, $R(D) = \inf_{P_{\hat S\mid S}:\ \mathbb E\,d(S,\hat S)\le D} I(S;\hat S)$. $R(D)$ is convex and non-increasing, and zero beyond $D_{\max}$. The converse says any code with $M$ messages and expected distortion at most $D$ has $\log M \ge \inf I(X;Y)$ over admissible channels. For a Gaussian source under squared error, $R(D) = \frac12\log^+(\sigma^2/D)$, and the Gaussian is the hardest source of a given variance ([Polyanskiy & Wu, Thms 24.3, 24.7, 25.1, 26.2–26.3](https://people.lids.mit.edu/yp/homepage/data/itbook-export.pdf)). Arda & Yener define a summarizer's rate as summary length over text length and prove a converse: any summarizer with rate $R$ and distortion $D$ has $R \ge R_S(D)$ ([Arda & Yener](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13100)). *Mapping [I].* Convexity means each extra summary token buys less distortion reduction than the last, which supports SKB's one short prefix over long synthesized pages. The converse is a hard floor: a fixed-length summary cannot answer every question at low distortion.

**Query-aware compression dominates [P plus empirical].** Nagle et al. define the distortion-rate function of prompt compression for a fixed black-box LLM as a linear program. Query-aware compressors (which see the question) contain query-agnostic ones as a special case, so $D^*_{\text{aware}}(R) \le D^*_{\text{agnostic}}(R)$ trivially. Their contribution is that the gap is large in practice, that current methods sit far from the optimum, and that only their variable-rate, query-aware method beat the optimal query-agnostic curve ([Nagle et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15504)). *Mapping [I].* Ingest-time summaries and wiki pages are query-agnostic by construction, and read-time rerank → filter → compress is query-aware. Information should therefore be lost at query time, with ingestion kept lossless. Variable rate is optimal, which favors dynamic context budgets over fixed top-$k$. **The information bottleneck [P]** formalizes the query-time step: minimize $I(X;T) - \beta I(T;Y)$, a rate–distortion problem whose distortion "emerges from the joint statistics" of $X$ and a relevance variable $Y$ ([Tishby, Pereira & Bialek](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0004057)). RAG instantiations (IB filtering, RECOMP's compressor that may output the empty string, LongLLMLingua) replace mutual information with LM log-likelihoods ([Xu, Shi & Choi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04408); [Jiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06839)). RECOMP's empty output is the IB solution at low $\beta$, a formal version of "add no context when the passages carry nothing about the answer" [I]. *What breaks.* When $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$, the IB curve cannot be recovered by sweeping $\beta$ and trivial solutions exist everywhere on it ([Kolchinsky, Tracey & Van Kuyk](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.07593)). RAG answer extraction is close to that regime, so $\beta$ sweeps are heuristics. Self-information pruning such as Selective Context is query-agnostic and therefore dominated at equal rate.

**Why structure beats estimated boundaries [P, I].** Utiyama & Isahara cast segmentation as maximum probability with a description-length prior. Each of $m$ segment lengths costs $\log n$ bits, so the boundary code costs $m\log n$, which penalizes over-segmentation without training data ([Utiyama & Isahara](https://aclanthology.org/P01-1064/)). Entropy per word given *full* context is constant, so context-free surprisal rises with position in a document ([Genzel & Charniak](https://aclanthology.org/P02-1026/)). Surprisal chunkers using short windows therefore drift for reasons unrelated to topic, and need local thresholds such as EM-LLM's moving $\mu+\gamma\sigma$ ([Fountas et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09450)). The two "information-theoretic" chunkers disagree on the rule itself: EM-LLM cuts at surprisal *peaks*, Meta-Chunking at perplexity *minima* ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12788)). Head-to-head, "the computational costs associated with semantic chunking are not justified by consistent performance gains" ([Qu, Tu & Bao](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13070)). *Mapping [I].* Headings, list items and table edges are boundary codes the author has already paid for exactly, so when they exist no estimator can beat reading them. That is the information-theoretic reason structure-aligned chunking explained **73–88% of the retrieval gain** on legal corpora ([StrucChunk](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26313)) and a free heading-path prefix matched LLM enrichment once a strong embedder or reranker was in place ([Mangla 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23533)). Estimators should be fallbacks for heading-less text such as transcripts, narratives and OCR scans, kept only if a golden set confirms a gain.

**Match small, read large [P].** Luan et al. define the normalized margin $\mu(q,d_1,d_2) = \langle q, d_1-d_2\rangle/(\|q\|\|d_1-d_2\|)$. They show empirically that margins shrink with document length, "suggesting limitations in the capacity of fixed-length encodings to support precise retrieval of long documents". Their Theorem 1 proves that splitting a document into orthogonal segments and scoring by the maximum over segments can only *increase* the margin ([Luan, Eisenstein, Toutanova & Collins](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00181)). *Mapping [I].* Embed short units (steps, FAQ questions, sentences) and return the parent section, as SKB recommends. Real sections are only approximately orthogonal. **The chunk size has no theorem.** Smaller units raise margins but add vectors, which raises $n$ and the $\log n$ terms below. The 256–512-token range is consistent with that trade-off, but nothing derives it.

**Content-defined boundaries localize edits [P, I].** LBFS declares a chunk boundary when the low 13 bits of a rolling Rabin fingerprint hit a magic value, giving an expected chunk of 8 KB. Because boundaries depend on content rather than offset, an edit invalidates only nearby chunks ([Muthitacharoen, Chen & Mazières](https://pdos.csail.mit.edu/papers/lbfs:sosp01/lbfs.pdf)). *Mapping [I].* With a memoryless boundary process, an insertion changes the chunk containing it and, with high probability, at most the next one. Heading boundaries have the same locality, which is the theory behind ADL's exit criterion that editing 1% of documents re-embeds no more than 2% of chunks.

## Sparse indexes escape the geometry that limits dense vectors

**The methods.** SKB and ADL keep a lexical channel (breadcrumb + body + keywords + aliases, with field boosts on title, identifiers and altLabels) beside a dense index, tune fusion weights on a golden set, and add a domain-validated reranker. RA asks for a snapshot-tied BM25 and regex index as a first-class tool. DRC's determinism ladder runs SQL or key lookup → inverted-index term match → BM25 with frozen statistics → exact kNN over a pre-filtered set → ANN.

### Dimension, margin and precision bound what one vector can rank

**Theorem (Johnson–Lindenstrauss) [P].** For $0<\varepsilon<1$ and $k \ge 4(\varepsilon^2/2-\varepsilon^3/3)^{-1}\ln n$, any $n$ points map into $\mathbb R^k$ with all squared distances preserved within $1\pm\varepsilon$ ([Dasgupta & Gupta](https://doi.org/10.1002/rsa.10073)). *Proof sketch.* For a Gaussian projection, $\|Rx/\sqrt k\|^2/\|x\|^2$ is $\chi^2_k/k$, whose tails give failure probability at most $1/n^2$ per pair, and a union bound over the $\binom n2$ difference vectors finishes. **This is optimal [P]:** some point sets need $m = \Omega(\varepsilon^{-2}\lg n)$ dimensions under *any* map, linear or not ([Larsen & Nelson](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02094)). *Arithmetic [S].* For $n=10^7$ chunks and $\varepsilon=0.1$ the guarantee needs about **13,800 dimensions**, far above 768–4096. Learned embedders succeed only by preserving the geometry the query distribution needs, and fail where queries probe what they discarded [I]. Retrieval needs ranking, not distances, so the binding quantity is the margin. Luan et al.'s Lemma 1 bounds the pairwise ranking error of a random projection by $\beta \le 4\exp(-\tfrac k2(\varepsilon^2/2-\varepsilon^3/3))$ at margin $\varepsilon$, and for Boolean bag-of-words vectors $k \ge 24\, L_Q L_D \ln(4/\beta)$ suffices ([Luan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00181)). *Arithmetic [S].* With 5 query terms, 500 document terms and $\beta=0.05$, that is about $2.6\times10^5$ dimensions. A random projection cannot guarantee exact term-match ranking for long documents at practical sizes, while a sparse inverted index represents it exactly. Empirically, dense performance "decreases quicker than sparse" as the index grows, possibly to a tipping point where sparse wins ([Reimers & Gurevych](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14210)).

**Theorem (LIMIT, Weller et al.) [P], and the correction it needs.** Call a $k$-subset $S$ "realized with margin $\gamma$" if some unit query $u_S$ scores every member of $S$ at least $2\gamma$ above every non-member. If all $k$-subsets of $n$ unit document vectors are realized with margin $\gamma$, then $\binom nk \le (1+1/\gamma)^d$, so $d \ge \log\binom nk/\log(1+1/\gamma)$ ([Weller, Boratko, Naim & Lee](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21038)). *Proof sketch.* For $S\ne T$ pick $i\in S\setminus T$ and $j\in T\setminus S$. Then $\langle u_S-u_T, v_i-v_j\rangle \ge 4\gamma$ and $\|v_i-v_j\|\le2$, so $\|u_S-u_T\|\ge2\gamma$. The disjoint $\gamma$-balls around $\binom nk$ query points fit inside a ball of radius $1+\gamma$, and comparing volumes gives the bound. **Correction to SKB and ADL [I].** SKB's sentence that DeepMind "proved that single-vector embedders are bounded in which top-k sets they can represent" overstates the theorem. LIMIT's own v2 appendix notes that the Alon–Frankl–Rödl construction gives every qrel matrix sign-rank at most $2k$, which "requires infinite precision in general". Without a margin or a precision limit, about $2k$ dimensions suffice for any pattern. The binding versions are the margin theorem above and a precision theorem: with $B$ bits per coordinate, realizing every $k$-subset requires $B\cdot d = \Omega(k\ln N)$, and below $B^* = O(\ln\ln N)$ no dimension suffices, so in quantized vector stores "the embedding dimension and possibly the precision must grow with the corpus size" ([Okajima & Yoshida](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11780)). Theorem 1 alone is also weak in numbers: for $n=10^6$, $k=2$, $\gamma=0.1$ it gives only $d\ge11$ [S]. The decisive evidence is empirical. On LIMIT, BM25 reached Recall@100 of **93.6**, a multi-vector ColBERT 54.8, and the best single-vector model 18.9 ([Weller et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21038)). The practical conclusion survives: identifier, code and attribute-conjunction queries form the all-subsets family, which a $|V|$-dimensional sparse index handles trivially and a quantized fixed-$d$ dense index cannot at scale.

**Middle options [P, preprint].** MaxSim late interaction "can exactly replicate the inner product between any two non-negative $k$-sparse vectors" in $O(k)$ space, and Signed MaxSim raised nDCG@10 on negation-only queries from 0.008 to 0.788 ([Killingback, Ingale, Zamani & Musco](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05803), 2026 preprint). MUVERA gives single-vector $\varepsilon$-approximations of multi-vector similarity ([Dhulipala et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19504)). Learned sparse models keep inverted-index exactness, but their "wacky" weights defeat the dynamic-pruning optimizations that make BM25 cheap ([Mackenzie, Trotman & Lin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.11540)). SKB's "lexical indexes scale cheaply" therefore applies to BM25-like weights, not automatically to SPLADE [I].

### BM25 is a fitted model, and the fit says how to boost fields

**Derivation (Robertson & Zaragoza) [P].** The binary independence model gives each query term the weight $\log\frac{P(t\mid rel)(1-P(t\mid\neg rel))}{(1-P(t\mid rel))P(t\mid\neg rel)}$. With no relevance information, the Robertson–Spärck Jones estimator reduces to $\log\frac{N-n_i+0.5}{n_i+0.5}$, "a close approximation to classical idf". Term frequency enters through a hidden *eliteness* variable with a 2-Poisson model ($tf\mid$elite $\sim$ Poisson$(\lambda_1)$, $tf\mid\neg$elite $\sim$ Poisson$(\lambda_0)$). The resulting weight is 0 at $tf=0$, increases monotonically, and saturates at the BIM weight: a term cannot contribute more than observing eliteness itself would. BM25 *fits* that shape with $tf/(k_1 B + tf)$ and a soft length normalization $B=(1-b)+b\,dl/avdl$, which reconciles a "verbosity" and a "scope" hypothesis. "The model provides no guidance" on $k_1$ and $b$ ([Robertson & Zaragoza](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)). **BM25F [P].** If eliteness is a property of the document shared across fields, evidence should be combined "first streams, then terms": $\tilde{tf} = \sum_s v_s\, tf_s$ with saturation applied once. Scoring each field with BM25 and summing assumes independent eliteness per field, which the authors call "a little unreasonable". *Mapping and correction [I].* SKB's field boosts on title, identifiers and altLabels should be implemented as BM25F stream weights $v_s$ tuned on the golden set, not as a sum of per-field BM25 scores. Otherwise a term in both title and body is counted past its saturation point. Whether a given engine's multi-field query implements BM25F or per-field summation has to be checked per engine. The breadcrumb prefix is in effect a cheap high-weight stream folded into one field.

**Axioms catch a defect in small KB indexes [P].** Fang, Tao & Zhai's constraints (more occurrences raise the score; concave tf gain; penalize added non-query terms; and so on) are violated by classic BM25 when $df > N/2$, because the IDF term goes negative and "matching an additional occurrence of a query term could mean decreasing the score" ([Fang, Tao & Zhai](https://doi.org/10.1145/1008992.1009004)). Length-normalized TF is also not properly lower-bounded, so very long units are over-penalized, which a BM25+ lower bound fixes ([Lv & Zhai](https://doi.org/10.1145/2063576.2063584)). *Mapping [I].* In a small corpus where the product name occurs in most units, the toolkit must use a non-negative IDF variant and must not freeze negative weights into a snapshot. Structural chunks merged to a narrow length range also make BM25 less sensitive to $b$, while mixing page-level slide units with short FAQ units brings length heterogeneity back. **Frozen statistics as a determinism rung [I].** BM25's only corpus-dependent quantities are $N$, $n_i$, $avdl$ and $dl$. Freezing them with the snapshot and breaking ties by external ID makes scores a pure function of (query, snapshot). This is DRC's third rung, now with a reason.

**Why BM25 stays hard to beat [I, with evidence].** It has two parameters and no learned components, so there is no train–test shift. Its IDF is computed on the deployed corpus, so domain jargon gets domain-appropriate weight for free. Its space is $|V|$-dimensional, so the margin and precision limits above do not bind. BEIR found BM25 "a robust baseline" out of distribution ([Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08663)), while in-domain DPR beat it by 9–19 points of top-20 accuracy after task training ([Karpukhin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)). Weak baselines inflate neural gains ([Lin 2019](https://doi.org/10.1145/3308774.3308781)). **Compression makes the channel cheap [P].** A posting list is a monotone sequence with information-theoretic minimum $\log_2\binom Un \approx n(\log_2(U/n)+1.443)$ bits. Elias–Fano uses at most $2+\lceil\log_2(U/n)\rceil$ bits per posting, within half a bit of that bound, with constant-time expected access ([Vigna](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4300)). Real lists are clustered, and partitioned Elias–Fano measured 3.12–5.45 bits per docid on Gov2, ClueWeb09 and CCNews ([Pibiri & Venturini](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10598)). *Arithmetic [S].* A 768-dimensional float32 vector is 24,576 bits per chunk (6,144 at int8). A 300-token chunk with about 150 distinct terms costs roughly 600–900 bits of docids, before term frequencies, so the lexical index is about an order of magnitude smaller than even an int8 dense index. Assigning chunk IDs contiguously in document and heading order clusters each term's postings, the analogue of URL ordering, and also keeps snapshot diffs local [I].

### Fusion has theorems for linear combination and none for RRF

**What exists for RRF.** $\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_r 1/(k + r(d))$ with "$k=60$ … fixed during a pilot investigation". It beat Condorcet-fuse in 7 of 7 experiments and CombMNZ in 6 of 7, by 4–5% on average, and the paper offers no proof ([Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher](https://doi.org/10.1145/1571941.1572114)). **No theorem gives conditions under which RRF beats its best input.** **What exists for linear fusion [P].** For convex combination $f = \alpha\,\phi(f_{\text{sem}}) + (1-\alpha)\,\omega(f_{\text{lex}})$, every $\alpha$ under min–max normalization has a rank-equivalent $\alpha'$ under z-score normalization, and vice versa (Lemma 4.2). Under a uniform-rate expansion condition one $\alpha'$ works for a whole query set (Thm 4.5). In practice, "the learning of a CC fusion is generally agnostic to the choice of score normalization". RRF "disregards the distribution of scores" and is not Lipschitz. Contrary to earlier claims it is sensitive to its parameters, and convex combination beat it in and out of domain with only a small labelled set needed to tune $\alpha$ ([Bruch, Gai & Ingber](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11934)). *Mapping and correction [I].* SKB already says "tune the fusion, don't assume RRF". The theory sharpens it. Once a golden set exists, tuned linear fusion with one $\alpha$ is the principled default. RRF's real advantage is scale-freeness, which matters only when no labels exist or score scales drift between snapshots, for example after an embedder upgrade. This does not conflict with the bandit lower bound in the stopping section. Lai–Robbins says *online* learned routing cannot pay for itself at low query volume. Tuning one $\alpha$ *offline* on a few hundred golden queries is a different and much cheaper problem.

**When fusion helps at all [empirical law].** Lee measured overlap between pairs of runs separately for relevant and non-relevant documents and found "different runs retrieve similar sets of relevant documents but retrieve different sets of nonrelevant documents", the *unequal overlap* property that explains why combination works and why agreement bonuses (CombMNZ) help ([Lee 1997](https://doi.org/10.1145/278459.258587)). *Mapping [I].* This gives a diagnostic to run on the golden set before trusting a hybrid. Compute relevant-overlap and non-relevant-overlap between BM25 and dense top-$k$. If relevant overlap is much larger, fusion should help. If the two channels find *different* relevant units, agreement bonuses help less and a union followed by a reranker helps more. Lee measured TREC runs, not lexical-versus-dense pairs, so this needs confirming locally. **Three or more retrievers [P].** Kemeny-optimal aggregation is NP-hard even for four input rankings, while footrule aggregation is polynomial and a 2-approximation ([Dwork, Kumar, Naor & Sivakumar](https://doi.org/10.1145/371920.372165), read via [Hillebrand et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.11319)). When BM25, dense, alias-expanded BM25 and graph neighbours are all fused, exact consensus is out of reach, and footrule or sort-based Condorcet approximations are the tractable choices [I].

### Approximate search has no worst-case guarantee, so recall must be measured

**LSH bounds [P].** An $(r, cr, p_1, p_2)$-sensitive family solves $c$-approximate near-neighbour search with $n^\rho$ tables, $\rho = \ln p_1/\ln p_2$, in query time $O(dn^\rho)$ and space $O(n^{1+\rho})$ ([Har-Peled, Indyk & Motwani](https://doi.org/10.4086/toc.2012.v008a014); [Bruch, Thms 5.1–5.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09350)). The exponent is tight for data-independent hashing: $\rho \ge 1/c - o(1)$ in Hamming space and $1/c^2$ in Euclidean space ([O'Donnell, Wu & Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.0250)). Data-dependent hashing reaches $1/(2c^2-1)$ ([Andoni & Razenshteyn](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.01062)). Under SETH, $(1+\varepsilon)$-approximate closest pair needs $n^{2-\delta}$ time for small enough $\varepsilon$, which rules out near-exact ANN in strongly sublinear query time ([Rubinstein](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.00904)). **Graph indexes [P].** If a graph contains the Delaunay graph, greedy search finds the exact nearest neighbour, but in high dimension the Delaunay graph is nearly complete ([Bruch §6.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09350)). HNSW's logarithmic scaling is an empirical claim ([Malkov & Yashunin](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09320)). HNSW, NSG and fast DiskANN have instances needing linear query time, and DiskANN can take at least $0.1n$ steps before meeting any of the five true neighbours ([Indyk & Xu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19126)). A 2026 result shows no greedy search on a flat or hierarchical graph can be sublinear in the worst case "even for an arbitrarily large approximation factor" ([Daliri et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38133)). Positive guarantees exist only under bounded intrinsic dimension, navigability or Steiner points ([Prokhorenkova & Shekhovtsov](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00845); [Al-Jazzazi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15636)). *Mapping [I].* DRC's ladder, which puts exact kNN over a structurally pre-filtered candidate set before ANN, is the theory-consistent design. Exact search over $m=10^5$ candidates at $d=768$ is about $7.7\times10^7$ multiply-adds per query [S], cheap on a CPU. The pgvector failure DRC cites, about 4 rows returned for a 10%-selective filter under HNSW post-filtering, is what happens when a graph's navigability is cut by deleting 90% of its nodes. **Correction:** ANN recall is not a property of the index type. It is an SLO to be measured against exact kNN on the golden queries at every build, failing the build below a threshold. The curse-of-dimensionality premise (i.i.d. coordinates) fails for learned embeddings, so the relevant quantity is the corpus's intrinsic dimension, which nobody has measured for text-embedding corpora.

**Quantization error [P].** For product quantization, mean squared error adds across subvectors, and the mean squared distance error of asymmetric distance computation is bounded by the quantizer's MSE (twice that for symmetric computation) ([Jégou, Douze & Schmid](https://inria.hal.science/inria-00514462)). PQ has no bound on individual distance estimates and can "fail disastrously on some real-world datasets", which RaBitQ's error bound addresses ([Gao & Long](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12497)). The Gaussian rate–distortion function $D(R) = \sigma^2 2^{-2R}$ per dimension sets the floor. *Arithmetic [S].* A 64-bit code for 128 dimensions is 0.5 bit per dimension, which for an isotropic Gaussian means relative MSE of at least 50%. Real embeddings do better only because their spectra are skewed. *Mapping [I].* Keep full-precision vectors, the vector analogue of raw evidence, and rescore quantized candidates exactly. Okajima & Yoshida's precision bound adds that recall will drift down as the store grows at fixed $d$ and precision, so the per-build recall check is not optional.

### Glossary and alias expansion is a curated relevance model

**The methods.** SKB puts "an alias table first": concept units with SKOS preferred, alternate and hidden labels, a `define` tool, query expansion from the term dictionary, and capture of the requester's own words (KCS). The thesaurus-guided BMQExpander beat LLM-only expansion on hallucination in SKB's evidence.

**Formal backing [P, S].** Rocchio's update moves the query toward the relevant centroid and away from the non-relevant one. *Our sketch.* For unit-normalized documents, the objective $\langle q, \mu_R - \mu_{NR}\rangle$ over unit $q$ is maximized by $q^* = (\mu_R-\mu_{NR})/\|\mu_R-\mu_{NR}\|$ by Cauchy–Schwarz, and Rocchio interpolates the original query toward $q^*$, with the original-query weight anchoring against drift ([Rocchio summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocchio_algorithm)). Lavrenko & Croft estimate the relevance model from the query alone, $P(w\mid R) \approx \sum_M P(M)P(w\mid M)\prod_i P(q_i\mid M)$ over document language models, as "an effective formal method for estimating a relevance model with no training data" that addresses synonymy ([Lavrenko & Croft](https://doi.org/10.1145/383952.383972)). Pseudo-relevance feedback is the greedy optimizer of the perfect-precision objective ([Chen & Karger](https://doi.org/10.1145/1148170.1148245)), and it carries downside risk that robust optimization over uncertainty sets reduces "without sacrificing average gains" ([Collins-Thompson](https://doi.org/10.1145/1645953.1646059)). LLM expansion such as HyDE and query2doc helps (query2doc lifted BM25 by 3–15%; [Wang, Yang & Wei](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07678)), but it fails exactly when the LLM lacks knowledge of the query or the query is ambiguous ([Abe et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12694)). **Empirical laws [E].** The probability that two people choose the same term for an object is **below 0.20** across five domains, a single designer-chosen term fails 80–90% of the time, and "unlimited aliasing" gives several-fold improvements ([Furnas et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/32206.32212)). In 186 catalog searches, removing subject headings would have lost **35.9%** of keyword hits on average ([Gross & Taylor](https://distantreader.org/stacks/journals/crl/crl-15726.pdf)).

*Mapping [I].* A SKOS concept's label set is a hand-verified, near-zero-variance estimate of $P(w\mid R)$, grounded in the corpus rather than in model knowledge. It covers exactly the jargon and acronym cases where LLM expansion fails. Rocchio's anchor term, relevance-model interpolation and Collins-Thompson's risk control all say the same thing: *interpolate, don't replace*. Aliases should go in as a separate, down-weighted BM25F stream or sub-query. HyDE should be gated off when the query contains identifiers or aliases the LLM cannot know. RM3-style feedback over the KB itself is deterministic given frozen BM25 statistics, which suits DRC's ladder better than LLM expansion. Harvested phrasings stabilize: on Delicious, each tag's share became "a nearly fixed proportion" after about 100 bookmarks, a Pólya-urn process with a *random* limit ([Golder & Huberman](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0508082)). Frequent requester phrasings can therefore be promoted to altLabels once their share stabilizes, while controlled preferred labels guard against early phrasings locking in. Peyrard's informativeness, cross-entropy relative to background knowledge $K$ ([Peyrard](https://aclanthology.org/P19-1101/)), says a glossary should carry domain senses the model's prior lacks, not generic definitions. The SKOS Reference supplies lint rules: labels pairwise disjoint, at most one preferred label per language, `skos:broader` not transitive (only `broaderTransitive` is), `exactMatch` transitive but `closeMatch` not ([W3C SKOS](https://www.w3.org/TR/skos-reference/)). Query expansion must not walk `broader` chains transitively unless the scheme opts in.

## Relevance is a property of sets, which the ranking principle cannot optimize

**The methods.** SKB reranks with a domain-validated cross-encoder, dedups by canonical reference, expands hits to parent sections and citation closure, keeps contexts small ("long context is a working set, not the store"), and evaluates "sufficient context". RA's design implication 8 asks for compact results with novelty signals.

**Theorem (Probability Ranking Principle) [P], and its scope.** "If retrieved documents are ordered by decreasing probability of relevance on the data available, then the system's effectiveness is the best that can be obtained for the data", which Robertson & Zaragoza say holds for a range of measures and "is not, however, completely general" ([Robertson & Zaragoza](https://doi.org/10.1561/1500000019)). *Proof sketch (standard exchange argument).* With $p_i = P(\text{rel}_i\mid\text{evidence})$, linearity of expectation gives $\mathbb E[\#\text{relevant in top } n] = \sum_{j\le n} p_{\pi(j)}$ whatever the dependence between documents, and sorting by $p$ maximizes this for every $n$ at once. The argument needs an *additive* metric, in which a document's gain does not depend on what is ranked above it. Gordon & Lenk list the decision-theoretic conditions as calibration, independent assessment of relevance and certainty about the probabilities, and show the PRP is suboptimal when one fails ([Gordon & Lenk](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-4571(199201)43:1%3C1::AID-ASI1%3E3.0.CO;2-5)). **Where it fails [P].** For $k$-call at $n$ (at least $k$ of the top $n$ relevant; 1-call is "at least one useful passage"), the expected-value-optimal ranking differs from the PRP, exact optimization is NP-hard, a greedy algorithm beats the PRP baseline, and the PRP is not optimal for mean average precision either. Greedily maximizing $P(\text{at least one relevant})$ conditions each pick on the earlier picks being non-relevant, which produces diversity with no diversity term ([Chen & Karger](https://doi.org/10.1145/1148170.1148245)). Relevance labels are also a weak proxy for RAG usefulness: labelling documents by the reader's downstream output raised Kendall's τ with end-to-end quality by 0.168–0.494 ([Salemi & Zamani](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13781)). *Correction [I].* The PRP remains the right target for first-stage candidate generation. The final context-assembly step optimizes a set objective (sufficiency, coverage), so it needs set-level selection, and the golden set should score 1-call and nugget coverage beside nDCG.

**Theorem (diversification and submodular coverage) [P].** Agrawal et al. define Diversify($k$): choose $|S|=k$ to maximize $\sum_c P(c\mid q)\,[1-\prod_{d\in S}(1-V(d\mid q,c))]$, the probability that the average user over intents finds at least one satisfying document. It is NP-hard (Lemma 1) and submodular (Lemma 2), so greedy IA-Select is a $(1-1/e)$-approximation ([Agrawal et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/1498759.1498766)). The general Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher theorem: for non-negative monotone submodular $f$, greedy sets satisfy $f(S_\ell) \ge (1-e^{-\ell/k})\max_{|S|\le k}f(S)$. *Proof sketch.* Monotonicity and submodularity give $f(S^*) \le f(S_i) + k\,(f(S_{i+1})-f(S_i))$, so the gap shrinks by a factor $(1-1/k)$ per step and $\delta_\ell \le e^{-\ell/k}f(S^*)$. Letting greedy pick $5k$ items lifts the ratio from about 0.63 to about 0.99 ([Krause & Golovin](https://las.inf.ethz.ch/files/krause12survey.pdf); [Nemhauser, Wolsey & Fisher](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01588971)). The $(1-1/e)$ ratio is tight for max-$k$-cover unless P = NP ([Feige](https://doi.org/10.1145/285055.285059)). With token budgets, plain cost-benefit greedy "can still perform arbitrarily badly", the better of uniform and cost-benefit greedy achieves $(1-1/e)/2$, and partial enumeration recovers $(1-1/e)$ ([Leskovec et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/1281192.1281239); [Sviridenko](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-6377%2803%2900062-2)). **Two cautions [P].** MMR's objective is non-monotone submodular, so the monotone guarantee does not apply to it ([Lin & Bilmes](https://aclanthology.org/P11-1052.pdf)). Computing α-nDCG's ideal gain vector is NP-complete, so its normalizer is itself computed greedily ([Clarke et al.](https://doi.org/10.1145/1390334.1390446)). *Mapping [I].* The principled last step is budget-aware greedy selection over a probabilistic coverage of claims or sub-questions, $f(S)=\sum_a w_a[1-\prod_{d\in S}(1-P(d \text{ covers } a))]$. Its guarantee holds for the modelled objective, not for end-task accuracy. Portfolio theory adds the reason dedup by canonical reference is right even when it costs PRP-measured precision: near-duplicates have highly correlated relevance, so a second copy adds variance and zero marginal coverage ([Wang & Zhu](https://doi.org/10.1145/1571941.1571963)). SKB's null result for MMR and multi-query on a single-intent benchmark is what the theory predicts. It does not show diversity is useless for multi-facet questions such as "what applies here" over policies, procedures and exceptions.

**Cooper's logical relevance is "sufficient context" [formal definition, S].** Cooper defined a sentence $s$ as relevant to a need $r$ iff there is a premise set $M$ with $s\in M$, $M\models r$ and $M\setminus\{s\}\not\models r$, a minimal entailing set ([Cooper 1971 via Rakow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14355); [original](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0271(71)90024-6)). *Our derivation.* A retrieved set $C$ is sufficient for answer $a$ iff some $M\subseteq C$ entails $a$. Two consequences follow from the definition. Relevance is not decomposable over passages: a bridging passage can be logically relevant while topically unrelated to the query string, and a topically perfect passage can be logically irrelevant when it is redundant. Pointwise topical ranking therefore cannot guarantee sufficiency on multi-hop questions. And minimality gives a citation criterion: the cited evidence for a claim should be a minimal entailing set, so removing any cited span must make the claim unsupported. *Mapping [I].* This is a concrete check for SKB's claim-level evaluation and RA's evidence-returning hand-offs. The empirical side agrees. Large models "often output incorrect answers instead of abstaining" when context is insufficient, and selective generation on sufficiency signals raised accuracy among answered queries by 2–10% ([Joren et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06037)). Independent audits find only **39–77% of cited claims supported** by their source ([Cited but Not Verified](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06635)). Saracevic's strata (system, topical, cognitive, situational, motivational) map onto the evaluation stack. SKB's golden sets, claim diagnostics, calibrated judges and production gap signals cover the first four. Whether the answer served the user's actual goal can only be measured with user feedback ([Rakow on Saracevic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14355)).

**Why small, reranked contexts [P, toy models].** With logits bounded in $[m, M]$, every softmax weight lies in $[e^{(m-M)/\theta}/n,\ e^{(M-m)/\theta}/n]$, so each item gets $\Theta(1/n)$ attention as the context grows. Transformers over a finite vocabulary have bounded logits, so all global heads disperse at large enough $n$ ([Veličković et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01104)). *Our derivation [S].* To give one item weight at least $a$ against $n-1$ items at logit 0 needs $z_{\max} \ge \log(a(n-1)/(1-a)) = \Theta(\log n)$, the logit gap that grows without bound ([Nakanishi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19399)). The causal mask biases multi-layer attention toward early positions, and with distance decay this reproduces lost-in-the-middle and attention sinks ([Wu, Wang, Jegelka et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01951)). In in-context linear regression with retrieved examples, variance falls as $O(1/n)$ but bias plateaus, giving a finite optimal number of retrieved examples $n^*$, and noise that grows with rank makes low-ranked passages actively harmful ([Guo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03100), under review). One transformer layer cannot compose functions over a domain of size $n$ when $H(d+1)p < n\log n$, by a communication-complexity argument ([Peng, Narayanan & Papadimitriou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08164)). The measurements match: accuracy "significantly degrades" for evidence in the middle ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)), and GPT-4o fell from 99.3% to 69.7% at 32K tokens when needle and question share few words ([Modarressi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167)). *Mapping [I].* Dispersion and position bias act on exactly two variables, $n$ and position. Reranking that puts the best one or two units first and keeps $k$ small acts on both, and the optimal $k$ should be tuned on the golden set rather than fixed. *What breaks.* These are bounded-logit and linear-ICL models. They say nothing about *which* distractors raise competing logits, and one study found that adding random documents improved accuracy by up to 35% ([Cuconasu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.14887)), which no theory explains.

## Superseded content needs filters because evidence weight grows with copies

**The methods.** SKB filters superseded content out rather than ranking it down, models versions as chains, collapses duplicates and sends paraphrases to review, keeps conflicts in the data layer, and stops poisoning at the write path. RA's implication 5 makes status, effective dates, superseded-by, owner and trust tier filterable fields. The measured stakes are large. One outdated passage cut Llama-3.1-70B from 89 to 73 ([HoH](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04800)), and outdated evidence flipped 17–91% of otherwise-correct answers ([Stale-Document Poisoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31342)).

**Theorem (diet model, zero-one rule) [P, our proof sketch].** Rank source types by profitability $\pi_i = g_i/h_i$ (gain per unit handling cost). A lower-ranked type is worth including iff its profitability exceeds the rate $R_k$ already obtained from the better types, and the decision is all-or-none and independent of how often the type is encountered ([Pirolli & Card 1995](https://www.chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/papers/ppp_bdy.htm)). *Proof sketch.* $(A+\lambda g)/(B+\lambda h) > A/B \iff g/h > A/B$, and the encounter rate $\lambda$ cancels. *Mapping [I].* Whether to admit forum posts, tier-4 external content or long low-scent PDFs into candidate generation depends only on whether their gain per token beats what the better tiers already deliver, not on how many there are. Superseded content has $g<0$ for current-state questions and is never included. "Filter, don't rank down" is the zero-one rule.

**Theorem (token-level benefit and detriment, Tok-RAG) [P, with approximations].** Modelling RAG as fusion of the model's latent-concept mixture with the retrieved-text distribution $P_R$, the pull of the retrieved concept enters as $v(z) \approx -n\,[\mathrm{KL}(P_R\|p(\cdot\mid z)) - \mathrm{KL}(P_R\|p(\cdot\mid z^*))]$, where $n$ is the number of retrieved passages. The first term is "distribution completion" (benefit), the second "distribution contradiction" (detriment), and Theorem 3 says detriment outweighs benefit iff $1/D < 1/M$ for computable distances $D, M$ ([Xu, Pang, Shen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00944)). *Mapping [I].* The weight of a concept scales with the number of passages supporting it. $k$ near-duplicate copies of a stale clause multiply its pull, and down-ranking leaves that pull in place as long as the passages stay in context. Exclusion, by a hard validity filter plus duplicate collapse, is the only operation that sets the contribution to zero. In Xie et al.'s Bayesian account of in-context learning, evidence that is likely under a *wrong* concept shrinks the KL margin of their distinguishability condition, the same effect from another angle ([Xie et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02080)).

**Theorem (fixed conflict rules are dominated) [preprint].** Let $\pi$ be the share of contexts that are right. Every fixed policy's expected score is affine in $\pi$, while the best attainable score is their maximum and therefore convex, so every fixed rule ("trust context", "trust memory") is strictly beaten wherever it is not exactly optimal. Any statistic of the two next-token distributions that is independent of which source is right carries no information about direction, so divergence-weighted decoding "loses most where the divergence is largest" on wrong contexts. Under label shift, an additive log-odds prior shift gives the exact posterior at a new $\pi$ ([Pham Van & Tran](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36169), October 2026, not yet peer-reviewed). *Mapping [I].* Validity windows, `superseded_by`, authority tier and verification date are an estimate of $\pi$ per source. The theorem says this per-source prior is the missing input that no model-internal signal can replace, which is the cleanest formal argument for SKB's governance metadata over learned rankings. Its preprint status makes it strong but unreviewed support. LLMs do adopt incorrect retrieved content, "overriding their own correct prior knowledge over 60% of the time" ([ClashEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10198)).

**Consistent query answering [P].** A consistent answer is one true in every minimal repair of a database that violates its constraints ([Arenas, Bertossi & Chomicki](https://doi.org/10.1145/303976.303983)). Under primary keys, for self-join-free Boolean conjunctive queries, it is decidable whether certain answering is first-order rewritable, in P but not FO, or coNP-complete ([Koutris & Wijsen](https://doi.org/10.1145/3068334)). *Mapping [I].* Two extracted values for one entity's key attribute form a key violation. The certain answer to "is X's limit at least 10k?" is yes only if both values satisfy it. For simple key-join queries this is plain SQL, and coNP-hard query shapes should be flagged rather than answered. This is the formal meaning of "conflicts belong in the data layer": expose the conflict as data and answer only what holds in every repair. Calibrated per-field probabilities are exact under the Dalvi–Suciu semantics only when errors are tuple-independent, with a PTIME versus #P-hard dichotomy even then ([Dalvi & Suciu](https://doi.org/10.1145/2395116.2395119)). Correlated extraction errors violate that independence, so probabilities should drive ranking and review priority while certain answers drive gating [I]. For poisoning, isolate-then-aggregate decoding gives a certified quality bound against any attacker injecting $k'$ passages ([RobustRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15556)).

**FRBR/LRM gives the version model [conceptual model plus empirical distribution].** FRBR's Work, Expression, Manifestation and Item, consolidated in IFLA LRM, separate an abstract creation from its realizations, embodiments and copies ([UKOLN FRBR factfile](https://www.ukoln.ac.uk/bib-man/factfile/cataloguing-rules/frbr/index.html); [IFLA LRM](https://repository.ifla.org/handle/123456789/42?mode=full)). *Mapping [I].* Work = the policy as an abstract obligation ("Refund policy"). Expression = a revision or translation (v3.2-en), with supersession as an Expression-to-Expression relation inside one Work. Manifestation = a rendering (PDF, HTML, wiki export). Item = a connector copy (a SharePoint file ID). Exact and near duplicates are multiple Items or Manifestations of one Expression and should be collapsed. Different Expressions must not be collapsed; they form a chain with one current member per jurisdiction and audience. OCLC's WorldCat sample shows **78% of works with a single manifestation** and about **6% "complex"** works, which were over-represented among the most widely held ([Bennett, Lavoie & O'Neill](https://www.oclc.org/content/dam/research/publications/library/2003/lavoie_frbr.pdf)). The analogy predicts that version-chain machinery matters for a few percent of units, which happen to be the high-traffic, high-risk ones, and that metadata such as owner and facets can be inherited across a Work's members. Mizzaro's time dimension of relevance ([Mizzaro](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-4571(199709)48:9%3C810::AID-ASI6%3E3.0.CO;2-U)) is the reason applicability is a field and not a ranking feature. No RAG theory models temporal validity explicitly.

## Hashes and sketches turn determinism invariants into numbers

**The methods.** DRC's registry sets `doc_id = sha256(bytes)` and its first invariant is a zero-call rebuild with byte-identical checksums. SKB's write-time gates run SHA-256 and MinHash dedup and version-chain detection, and flag rather than delete paraphrases.

**MinHash [P].** For a uniformly random permutation $\pi$ of the shingle universe, the probability that $A$ and $B$ share the minimum is their Jaccard resemblance $J$, because the minimum of $\pi(S_A\cup S_B)$ is uniform over the union, so $P(\text{it lies in } S_A\cap S_B) = J$ ([Broder 1997](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spring13/cos598C/broder97resemblance.pdf)). *Our derivations [S].* With $k$ independent permutations the estimate is Binomial$(k,J)/k$, with variance $J(1-J)/k$, so at $J=0.8$ and $k=128$ the standard deviation is about 0.035. LSH banding with $b$ bands of $r$ rows flags a pair with probability $1-(1-J^r)^b$, a threshold near $(1/b)^{1/r}$. For $b=16$, $r=8$ that is 0.95 at $J=0.8$, 0.06 at $J=0.5$, and a threshold near 0.71. The dedup gate becomes a stated precision–recall operating point in config. **SimHash [P].** For a Gaussian hyperplane $r$, $P[\mathrm{sign}(r\cdot u)=\mathrm{sign}(r\cdot v)] = 1-\theta(u,v)/\pi$ ([Charikar](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr04/cos598B/bib/CharikarEstim.pdf)). A Hamming threshold of 3 on 64 bits corresponds to $\theta \le 8.4°$ under the Binomial model [S], and at web scale precision and recall were both "near 0.75" ([Manku, Jain & Das Sarma](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/33026.pdf)). *What breaks.* Both estimate *syntactic* resemblance, not semantic equivalence or version identity. A high-resemblance pair may be two Expressions of one Work that must not be collapsed, and embedding-based semantic dedup has no equivalence guarantee at all, so SKB's "flag, don't delete" is right. No published analysis bounds MinHash's false-merge rate on near-identical policy versions.

**Bloom filters, and a correction [P, I].** With $n$ items, $m$ bits and $k$ hashes, the false-positive rate is about $(1-e^{-kn/m})^k$, minimized at $k=(m/n)\ln2$. Any structure with false-positive rate $\varepsilon$ needs $m \ge n\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ bits, so Bloom filters are within a factor $\log_2 e \approx 1.44$ of optimal ([Broder & Mitzenmacher](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~michaelm/postscripts/im2005b.pdf)). The classical formula is in fact a strict *lower* bound on the true rate ([Bose et al.](https://cglab.ca/~morin/publications/ds/bloom-submitted.pdf)). *Arithmetic [S].* At 10 bits per item and $k=7$ the rate is about 0.82%. *Correction [I].* In an ingest "already seen?" filter, a false positive means a *new* document is skipped as a duplicate. That is silent data loss, and it breaks DRC's completeness and zero-call-rebuild invariants. Bloom filters belong only as negative pre-filters (a "no" is certain), followed by an exact hash lookup on "maybe".

**Birthday bound and collision resistance [P].** For $k$ i.i.d. uniform values in a set of size $N$, $P(\text{collision}) \ge 1-e^{-k(k-1)/2N}$, non-uniform values only raise it, and the union bound gives at most $k(k-1)/2N$ ([Boneh & Shoup, Thm B.1](https://toc.cryptobook.us/)). *Arithmetic [S].* 64-bit IDs over $10^9$ objects collide with probability about 2.7%, and at $2^{32}$ objects about 39%. 256-bit IDs over $10^{12}$ objects collide with probability at most about $4\times10^{-54}$. Collision resistance is a computational assumption, and SHA-1's failed: the first full collision took about $2^{63.1}$ compressions, "more than 100 000 times faster than a brute force search", and produced two PDFs with the same hash ([Stevens et al.](https://shattered.io/static/shattered.pdf)). Git is moving to SHA-256 because "SHA-1 is still weak" ([Git](https://git-scm.com/docs/hash-function-transition)). A Merkle tree built from a collision-resistant hash is collision resistant, since a root collision implies a node collision ([Boneh & Shoup §8.9](https://toc.cryptobook.us/)). *Mapping [I].* DRC's SHA-256 document IDs are justified, and 64-bit or SHA-1 IDs are contradicted. Framing should follow Git (type, length, a NUL byte, then content) over normalized content. A snapshot ID equal to the Merkle root over (record ID, record hash) pairs makes "identical repeat answers" checkable by comparing two roots, makes snapshot diffs cost $O(\text{changed}\cdot\log n)$, and makes any drifted or tampered record visible. Normalized compression distance with a real compressor ([Cilibrasi & Vitányi](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0312044)) is a cheap, model-free version-chain signal to run on candidate pairs after LSH blocking. Duplicates also waste the context window. A copy carries about zero new bits ($K(xx)\approx K(x)+O(1)$) but takes a top-$k$ slot, which is the information-theoretic reading of SKB's "duplicates outvote the canonical page" [I].

## Review gates carry guarantees that are marginal, pairwise and version-bound

### Entity resolution: Fellegi–Sunter minimizes review, but only pair by pair

**The method.** DRC and ADL resolve entities by exact keys, then probabilistic matching (Splink), then LLM selection among blocked candidates, then human review, and persist cluster IDs, alias maps and mention tables.

**Theorem (Fellegi & Sunter 1969, as restated by Winkler) [P].** For a comparison vector $\gamma$ with $m(\gamma)=P(\gamma\mid M)$ and $u(\gamma)=P(\gamma\mid U)$, the rule "link if $R=m/u > T_\mu$, review if $T_\lambda \le R \le T_\mu$, non-link if $R<T_\lambda$" minimizes the probability of the review region, under both matches and non-matches, among all rules with the same false-link and false-non-link probabilities ([Winkler 1990](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/NCRN/PUBLIC/RLClassFiles/HW/Winkler1990.pdf); [Fellegi & Sunter](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1969.10501049)). *Proof sketch (our reconstruction; the 1969 original was not retrieved).* Order comparison vectors by $R$. Fill the link region from the top until the false-link budget is spent and the non-link region from the bottom until the false-non-link budget is spent, randomizing at boundaries. This is the Neyman–Pearson exchange argument ([Neyman & Pearson](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.1933.0009)): any other ordering spends more error budget per unit of decided probability. With three conditionally independent fields, EM identifies $m$, $u$ and $P(M)$, and "the conditional independence assumption corresponds exactly to the naïve Bayes assumption". EM works best when more than about 5% of pairs are matches, and under field dependence "the linear weighting of the scores … is not optimal" ([Winkler 2006](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2006/adrm/rrs2006-02.pdf)). Splink builds on fastLink's EM for this model ([Linacre et al.](https://doi.org/10.23889/ijpds.v7i3.1794)). *Mapping [I].* The DRC ladder is the three-region rule, and putting the LLM inside the review band is a second-stage test on the undecided region, consistent with the theorem. *What breaks.* Misestimated $m/u$, dependent fields such as address and phone that move together, and blocking, which makes the error rates conditional on the candidate population. In each case the form of the rule survives and its optimality certificate does not. Thresholds can instead be set from labelled pairs with mixture calibration ([Belin & Rubin](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1995.10476563)) or with conformal risk control (below).

**Clusters need a global step [P, correction].** Pairwise decisions need not be transitive. Minimizing disagreements in correlation clustering is NP-hard ([Bansal, Blum & Chawla](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:MACH.0000033116.57574.95)). The random-pivot algorithm is a 3-approximation on complete graphs, with LP-based 2.5 and later 2.06 ([Ailon, Charikar & Newman](https://doi.org/10.1145/1411509.1411513); [Jafarov survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.12636)). Bayesian bipartite and latent-entity models yield transitive linkage natively and propagate uncertainty ([Sadinle](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.06630); [Steorts, Hall & Fienberg](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.4645)). *Correction [I].* DRC's persisted cluster IDs cannot come from pairwise Fellegi–Sunter decisions alone. They need connected components (fast, but prone to chaining), correlation clustering or a latent-entity model. Because pivot choice changes the clusters, a deterministic build must use a fixed pivot order, such as sorted record IDs, not a random permutation. One-to-one assignment constraints also reduce greedy errors within households ([Winkler 2006](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2006/adrm/rrs2006-02.pdf)).

### Multi-call extraction: correlated votes converge to the wrong answer

**The method.** DRC's L3 layer scores disagreement between two structurally different extraction calls, fused with OCR and layout signals. That design gave 99.1% accuracy at 80% coverage on DocILE (author-reported, single author; [ExtractConf](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24420)).

**Theorem (Condorcet jury theorem) [P].** If $2m+1$ voters act independently, each correct with probability $p>1/2$, majority accuracy increases monotonically to 1 as $m\to\infty$ ([Boland](https://doi.org/10.2307/2348873)). Asymptotic infallibility follows from the law of large numbers. With heterogeneous independent voters, the optimal rule is a weighted majority whose weights increase with competence ([Nitzan & Paroush](https://doi.org/10.2307/2526438), via [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/jury-theorems/)). *Our derivation [S].* The weighted rule is the likelihood-ratio test for the binary state, so with independent voters $\log LR = \sum_i \pm\log(p_i/(1-p_i))$ and the weights are log-odds of competence. Hoeffding gives $P(\text{majority wrong}) \le \exp(-2k(p-\tfrac12)^2)$ ([Hoeffding](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830)), which is loose: 0.41 at $p=0.8$, $k=5$, against an exact binomial tail of about 0.058. **Correlated voters [P, S].** Common causes "exist in abundance". Under conditional independence given the facts, limiting majority accuracy is the probability that the facts are truth-conducive plus half the probability they are neutral, so majorities "can be wrong even in asymptotically large bodies" ([Dietrich & Spiekermann](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzt074); [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/jury-theorems/)). *Our derivation.* If per-call correctness indicators are exchangeable with marginal $p$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, then $\mathrm{Var}(\text{fraction correct}) = p(1-p)[\rho+(1-\rho)/k] \to \rho p(1-p) > 0$, so the law of large numbers fails. Equivalently, give each item a latent per-call correctness $\theta_x$. Majority then converges to $\mathbf 1\{\theta_x>1/2\}$, and limiting accuracy is $P(\theta_x>1/2)+\tfrac12P(\theta_x=1/2)$. For LLM calls this is proved directly: for binary answers, voting accuracy over $K$ calls is a regularized incomplete beta function that rises in $K$ on easy queries, *falls* on difficult ones, and can be an inverse U on a mixture ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02419)). Across 350+ LLMs, models agree **60% of the time when both err** ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962)), and majority voting plateaus beyond several hundred samples without a verifier ([Brown et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787)).

*Correction and mapping [I].* For fields where the model is systematically wrong ($\theta_x<1/2$, such as a misread table header), more samples of the same model converge *confidently* to the wrong value, which is the mechanism behind ExtractConf's observation that self-consistency "collapse[s] toward all-positive behaviour". DRC's design is right to use two *structurally different* calls and to treat disagreement as a routing score to be calibrated, not as a vote. Calls should differ in prompt and reading strategy, provider or model family, or modality (OCR versus VLM) to cut $\rho$. If voting is used, weights should be log-odds competences per field type, estimated with Dawid–Skene on the golden set ([Dawid & Skene](https://doi.org/10.2307/2346806); minimax-optimal estimation in [Zhang, Chen, Zhou & Jordan](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.3824)). Dawid–Skene also assumes conditional independence, so its competences are biased upward when extractors share failure modes and must be checked against human labels. Nobody has measured per-field error correlation between extraction calls.

### Conformal thresholds: finite-sample, marginal, and void after an extractor change

**The method.** DRC's L3 sets a split-conformal threshold per field type, and L4 sends rejected fields to humans, whose corrections become versioned overrides. In clinical extraction, split conformal reached at least 90% coverage at 9–13% rejection even though miscalibration reversed direction between domains ([Shrestha & Kim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00924)).

**Theorem (split conformal) [P].** If calibration pairs and the test pair are exchangeable, the set $C(X)=\{y: s(X,y)\le\hat q\}$ with $\hat q$ the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$ empirical quantile satisfies $P(Y_{\text{test}}\in C(X_{\text{test}})) \ge 1-\alpha$, and with continuous scores coverage is at most $1-\alpha+1/(n+1)$ ([Angelopoulos & Bates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511); [Lei et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.04173)). *Proof sketch.* By exchangeability the rank of the test score among all $n+1$ scores is uniform, so $P(s_{\text{test}}\le s_{(k)}) = k/(n+1)$; take $k=\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$. Conditional on the calibration set, coverage is Beta$(n+1-l,\,l)$ with $l=\lfloor(n+1)\alpha\rfloor$. *Arithmetic [S].* At $n\approx200$ and $\alpha=0.1$, realized coverage has a standard deviation of about $\sqrt{0.9\cdot0.1/200}\approx$ 2 points. **Conformal risk control [P].** For losses $L_i(\lambda)$ non-increasing in $\lambda$ and bounded by $B$, choosing $\hat\lambda = \inf\{\lambda : \tfrac{n}{n+1}\hat R_n(\lambda)+\tfrac{B}{n+1}\le\alpha\}$ gives $\mathbb E[L_{n+1}(\hat\lambda)]\le\alpha$, tight to within $2B/(n+1)$ ([Angelopoulos, Bates, Fisch & Lei](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)). **Conformal selection [P].** Conformal p-values plus Benjamini–Hochberg select test units with false discovery rate at most $q$ ([Jin & Candès](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01408)), and conformal alignment applies this to foundation-model outputs "regardless of the foundation model or the data" ([Gui, Jin & Ren](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10301)). Conformal factuality backs off sub-claims to a calibrated level with $P(\text{correct}) \ge 1-\alpha$, reporting 80–90% guarantees from about 50 annotated inputs ([Mohri & Hashimoto](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10978)).

*Correction [I].* Plain split conformal guarantees that a *prediction set* contains the truth. It does not guarantee that "accepted single values are right at least $1-\alpha$ of the time", which is what a downstream SQL user experiences. The auto-accept gate matches its goal exactly when implemented as conformal risk control with loss $\mathbf 1\{\text{field accepted at }\lambda\text{ and wrong}\}$, or as conformal selection controlling the FDR among accepted fields. *What breaks.* **Exchangeability.** A new extractor model, prompt, schema version or document source invalidates the calibration set. The calibration set must be keyed to DRC's L6 extractor fingerprint. On a version bump, either recalibrate, or use non-exchangeable conformal with down-weighted old points, whose coverage loss is bounded by $\sum_i\tilde w_i\, d_{TV}$ ([Barber et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13415)), or adaptive conformal inference online once corrections stream in ([Gibbs & Candès](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00170)). **Marginality.** Exact distribution-free conditional coverage is impossible ([Barber et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.04684)), so a 90% guarantee per field type allows a few templates to be far worse. High-stakes fields need calibration stratified by template or source ([Cherian, Gibbs & Candès](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09714)). **Dependent fields.** No published guarantee covers multi-field extraction with constraints such as total = sum of line items. Field-wise guarantees combine only by union bounds.

**Proper scoring rules explain why conformal works on miscalibrated scores [P].** A scoring rule is proper iff it is generated by a convex function $G$, and strictly proper iff $G$ is strictly convex; Brier corresponds to $G(p)=\sum p_j^2-1$ and the log score to negative entropy ([Gneiting & Raftery](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/raftery/Research/PDF/Gneiting2007jasa.pdf)). The Brier score decomposes into reliability, resolution and uncertainty ([Murphy](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0450%281973%29012%3C0595:ANVPOT%3E2.0.CO;2)). Calibration alone is cheap: a randomized forecaster can be asymptotically calibrated on any sequence while knowing nothing ([Foster & Vohra](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/85.2.379)). *Mapping [I].* Gate quality depends on *resolution* (does the score separate right from wrong), thresholds on *calibration*. Conformal thresholds need only a ranking-quality score, which is why they hold when raw confidence is miscalibrated in opposite directions across domains. What conformal cannot fix is low resolution: if scores do not separate errors, the guaranteed threshold rejects almost everything. Field confidences should be reported as a proper score split into reliability and resolution, and calibration maps refit per extractor version. Verbalized confidence fails both tests for entity extraction, with calibration error of 0.53–0.94 ([Qian](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20417)).

### Judge calibration: PPI is valid whatever the judge, if the human sample is random

**The method.** SKB calibrates LLM judges weekly with prediction-powered inference on 150–300 fresh human labels, following ARES ([Saad-Falcon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09476)).

**Theorem (prediction-powered inference) [P].** The estimator $\hat\theta^{PP} = \frac1N\sum f(\tilde X_i) - \frac1n\sum(f(X_i)-Y_i)$ is unbiased for $\mathbb E[Y]$, and its CLT-based interval has asymptotic coverage at least $1-\alpha$ "without making any assumptions on the machine-learning algorithm". More accurate predictions give narrower intervals ([Angelopoulos et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09633)). *Proof sketch.* The judge's mean on unlabelled data minus the rectifier's expectation is $\mathbb E f - (\mathbb E f - \mathbb E Y) = \mathbb E Y$ when labelled and unlabelled items come from one distribution. The variance is driven by $\mathrm{Var}(f-Y)$. PPI++ adds power tuning so it never does worse than the classical interval from labels alone ([Angelopoulos, Duchi & Zrnic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01453)), and stratified PPI tightens intervals when judge quality varies by stratum ([Fisch et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04291)). *Mapping [I].* The theorem is what makes "150–300 human labels plus LLM labels" defensible. A judge correlated with the generator yields valid but *wider* intervals, not biased ones, which is PPI's advantage over raw judge scores. *What breaks.* The human-labelled items must be a random sample from the population the judge scores. Cherry-picked hard cases void the guarantee unless handled by stratification or explicit weights. Basic PPI is asymptotic.

**Golden-set design has its own theory [empirical, measurement theory].** Correlated judges "overinflate the accuracy of models that are less accurate" ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962)). Using the judge as every system's final reranker distorted Kendall's τ, a measured circularity ([Clarke & Dietz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17156)), so the evaluation judge should come from a different model family than any reranker and be blind to nugget templates [I]. When pools are small relative to the collection, judgments favour documents containing the topic's title words ([Buckley et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10791-007-9032-x)), and LLM judges are "highly influenced by the presence of query words" ([Alaofi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17969)). A golden set whose candidates came from BM25 alone will over-credit lexical matches, so the pool should include BM25, dense, alias-expanded and browse-found units, recording which pool found each gold unit [I]. For A/B gates, paired t, bootstrap and randomization tests agree, while Wilcoxon and sign tests have poor power and should be dropped ([Smucker, Allan & Carterette](https://doi.org/10.1145/1321440.1321528)). Transforming IR metrics to interval scales changed about 25% of significance decisions ([Ferrante, Ferro & Fuhr](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.02668)). Test collections also "expire" once systems reach assessor-level effectiveness ([Parry et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20937)), which supports DRC's versioned golden set with stale marking.

### Abstention: binary grading makes "I don't know" never optimal

**The method.** SKB's agent policy ends with "abstain with a gap report when evidence is insufficient", and its golden set includes insufficient-evidence questions scored for correct abstention.

**Observation (Kalai, Nachum, Vempala & Zhang) [P].** If a grader gives 1 for a correct answer and 0 for both wrong answers and abstentions, then for any belief over graders the optimal response is never an abstention, since abstaining scores 0 for sure and any guess scores at least 0 in expectation. The authors note this "holds for arbitrary language models, including those with RAG". **Their fix [P].** Tell the model that mistakes cost $t/(1-t)$ points, correct answers earn 1 and "I don't know" earns 0. A model with correctness probability $q$ then expects $q-(1-q)t/(1-t)$, positive iff $q>t$, so answering exactly when $q>t$ is optimal for every $t$ at once ([Kalai et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664)). OpenAI reports gpt-5-thinking-mini at 52% abstention, 22% accuracy and 26% error on SimpleQA, against o4-mini at 1%, 24% and 75% (vendor-reported, from a search summary of [OpenAI's post](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)). The same three-region structure appears in Chow's optimal reject rule, which rejects when the maximum posterior falls below a threshold ([Chow 1970](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1970.1054406)), in Wald's sequential test ([Wald & Wolfowitz](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177730197)) and in Fellegi–Sunter. *Correction [I].* A golden set scored by plain accuracy pushes the agent to guess whenever retrieval comes back empty or contradictory. The evaluation gate should score with an explicit penalty $t/(1-t)$, give credit for "evidence not found", and report accuracy, error and abstention separately. Conformal abstention and factuality then give a principled threshold with a bounded error rate on the calibrated distribution ([Yadkori et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01563)). Hallucination *detection* from positive examples alone is impossible for most language collections, and possible for every countable collection once expert-labelled negatives exist ([Karbasi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17004)). Golden sets should therefore contain labelled false and superseded statements, not only correct Q/A pairs [I]. *What breaks.* Verbalized confidence is a poor $q$. Isotonic calibration raised accuracy among committed answers by up to 41 points but cut overall accuracy by up to 17 points on MuSiQue, and a map fitted before retrieval degraded by retrieval depth three ([Chhikara](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26846)), so calibration maps must be fitted per loop depth.

## Stopping rules follow from rates, not from round counts

### Search depth: the theorems explain "a few rounds" but do not derive two

**The method.** SKB's agent policy does "at most two or three search-and-read rounds with a stopping rule", citing an ablation in which **two retrieval iterations captured 95% of the gains of five** ([Shaikh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21553)). RA describes the deep-research loop as persistence trained by RL and asks for novelty signals ("N already returned this session") to support diminishing-returns stopping.

**The secretary rule does not apply [P].** Its optimal rule (reject the first $\approx n/e$, then take the next relatively best) needs no recall, rank-only information and no per-step cost ([Ferguson ch. 2](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tom/Stopping/sr2.pdf)). Agent retrieval violates all three: passages can be recalled, scores are cardinal, and every call costs tokens. A "spend 37% of the budget exploring" heuristic has no basis [I]. **Theorem (monotone stopping) [P].** Let $A_n = \{Y_n \ge \mathbb E(Y_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n)\}$. If $A_0\subset A_1\subset\cdots$ almost surely (once myopic stopping is indicated, it stays indicated), then in a finite-horizon problem the one-stage look-ahead rule is optimal ([Ferguson ch. 5, Thm 1](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tom/Stopping/sr5.pdf)). *Proof sketch.* Backward induction gives $V_n = \max\{Y_n, \mathbb E(V_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n)\}$, and monotonicity makes the look-ahead stopping set absorbing, so it coincides with the optimal set. **The proofreading model [P].** Each pass costs $c_1$ and each undetected error $c_2$. If the error count is Poisson$(\lambda)$ and each remaining error is found with probability $p$ per pass, what remains after $n$ passes is Poisson$(\lambda(1-p)^n)$, and the optimal rule is the *fixed* count $N_1 = \min\{n : \lambda p(1-p)^n \le c_1/c_2\}$ ([Ferguson ch. 5, Example 5.4](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tom/Stopping/sr5.pdf)). *Worked example [S].* With $\lambda=2$ missing facts, $p=0.6$ and a missing fact costing ten rounds ($c_1/c_2 = 0.1$), expected new finds per round are 1.2, 0.48, 0.19 and 0.077, so the agent stops after three rounds. In a simpler geometric model where the answer is found each round with probability $q$, "two rounds = 95% of five" corresponds to $q\approx0.78$. *Mapping [I].* The model predicts more rounds when the per-round find rate $p$ is low (weak retriever, hard corpus), when there are more hops ($\lambda$ high) or when misses are costly, and fewer rounds when retrieval and reranking improve. That matches SKB's observation that moderate reranking often buys more than added search-time reasoning. *What breaks.* The fixed count requires monotonicity, meaning expected yield never rises from one round to the next. A reformulation that suddenly opens a new region of the corpus breaks it, and then an adaptive rule beats any fixed count. Nobody has tested monotonicity on logged agent trajectories.

**Pandora's box and value of information [P].** Weitzman's rule gives each source $i$, with reward distribution $D_i$ and opening cost $c_i$, a reservation value $\tau_i$ solving $\mathbb E_{v\sim D_i}[(v-\tau_i)^+] = c_i$. Sources are opened in decreasing $\tau_i$, and the agent stops when the best reward in hand exceeds every unopened $\tau$ ([Weitzman](https://doi.org/10.2307/1910412), restated in [Kalayci, Raman & Dughmi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01394)). The Gittins index theorem proves it, and a generalized Pandora rule is optimal for arbitrary objectives only in special cases ([Olszewski & Weber](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.09095)). A UCB-style Pandora algorithm matched best-of-N with 15–35% fewer generations ([Kalayci et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01394)). The value of free information is never negative: $\mathbb E_X[\max_a\mathbb E(U(a)\mid X)] \ge \max_a\mathbb E\,U(a)$, because the expectation of a maximum is at least the maximum of expectations ([Good 1967](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjps/17.4.319), via [Graves](https://doi.org/10.1086/289490)). The guarantee holds only in expectation, for Bayesian conditioning on *true* evidence with *free* processing. LLMs reading misleading passages violate both premises, so ClashEval's adoption of wrong content does not contradict it [I]. *Mapping [I].* Each candidate sub-query or source is a box. "Stop when the best evidence in hand beats every unopened option's reservation value" is a principled stopping rule beyond a round count. Pandora counts only the single best prize, though, and multi-hop questions need several complementary pieces.

**Multi-hop needs decomposition, by a counterexample [P].** Information gain is monotone but not submodular in general. With $X$, $Y$ independent fair bits and $Z = X \oplus Y$, observing $X$ or $Y$ alone gives zero information about $Z$, but both together determine it. If observations are conditionally independent given the targets, $F(A) = H(U) - H(U\mid A)$ is submodular and greedy gets $(1-1/e)$ ([Krause & Guestrin](https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.1394)). For one hidden object, searching the most inviting location is optimal, but for more than one "the optimal strategy may be very different" ([Assaf & Zamir](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1176349665)). *Mapping [I].* The XOR example is the formal signature of a multi-hop question: each hop alone says nothing about the answer, so greedy single-query retrieval can fail badly and returns can be *increasing*. Decomposition into sub-questions creates sub-targets with conditionally independent evidence, which restores the greedy guarantee per sub-question. That is a theoretical account of SKB's "make a first decomposed search", not a proven result about LLM agents. Bayesian search theory adds a source-selection rule: rank sources by $p_i a_i/c_i$ (probability the answer is there × probability of finding it if there ÷ cost) and after a miss update $p' = p(1-q)/(1-pq)$ rather than re-querying the same source ([Bayesian search theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_search_theory); [Stone](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.37.3.501)). **Bandits set a floor on learned routing [P].** Any consistent strategy has regret at least $\sum_i \Delta_i/\mathrm{kl}(\mu_i,\mu^*)\cdot\ln n$ ([Lai & Robbins, via Bubeck & Cesa-Bianchi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.5721)). With low per-domain query volume and similar arms, an online router cannot pay for itself, and some static rewrite strategies had higher cumulative regret than no rewrite at all ([Cho, Watson & Koppel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16697)). One-off configuration choices are best-arm identification on the golden set, not online regret minimization [I].

### Foraging theory says a good index means reading less of each source

**The methods.** SKB gives agents a small always-loaded index (`kb/index.md` of about 8–10 KB), `kb_read(unit_id, section?, range?)`, one level of progressive disclosure, and five consolidated tools. RA asks for compact results with caps and paging.

**Theorem (Charnov's marginal value theorem) [P, our proof].** Patch types $i$ are met at rate $\lambda_i$ per unit travel time, with increasing concave gain functions $g_i$ and residence times $t_i$. The long-run rate is $R = \sum_i\lambda_i g_i(t_i)/(1+\sum_i\lambda_i t_i)$ ([Charnov](https://doi.org/10.1016/0040-5809(76)90040-X); [Pirolli & Card 1995](https://www.chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/papers/ppp_bdy.htm)). *Proof.* With numerator $N$ and denominator $D$, $\partial R/\partial t_j = \lambda_j[g_j'(t_j)D - N]/D^2$, so at an interior optimum $g_j'(t_j^*) = N/D = R^*$ for every $j$: leave each source when its instantaneous gain rate falls to the best achievable average rate. Concavity makes this a maximum. *Comparative statics [S].* With one patch type and mean travel time $t_B$, the implicit function theorem gives $dt^*/dt_B = -g'(t^*)/[g''(t^*)(t_B+t^*)] > 0$. **Cheaper travel between sources means shorter optimal reading of each.** For $g(t) = G(1-e^{-t/\tau})$, an agent takes about 34% of a document's extractable value when $t_B/\tau = 0.1$, 68% when $t_B/\tau = 1$, and 93% when $t_B/\tau = 10$ [S]. **Information scent [validated model].** In SNIF-ACT, a link's scent sums ACT-R activations whose cue–goal associations approximate pointwise mutual information, and links are chosen by a softmax rule. The model fit link-selection counts with $R^2 = .91$ and $.69$ on two sites and predicted backing up with $R^2 = .73$ and $.80$. Site-leaving after a scent drop rested on 12 events and was not significant ([Fu & Pirolli](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/25938.pdf)).

*Mapping and correction [I].* Index files, progressive disclosure and fast search all reduce $t_B$. The MVT then predicts that an agent with a good index should read *less* of each file and open *more* files. That argues for chunk-level `read(range)` tools and against "read the whole file" defaults whenever search is cheap. It also explains why an extra disclosure layer adds nothing once a harness already makes $t_B$ small through grep. Index lines should carry the terms with the highest PMI against the questions each unit answers, meaning the domain's preferred and alternate labels and requesters' own phrasing (KCS "requestor's context"), not generic words. A rate-based stopping rule follows: stop when the marginal gain of the next read (new atomic claims, or coverage of decomposed sub-questions, per 1,000 tokens) falls below the session's running average. A complementary "frustration" rule (stop after $k$ consecutive non-contributing reads) best matched real searchers among query-level stopping rules ([Maxwell et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/42366798.pdf)). Tokens per correctness is the natural currency, and over-searching measurably harms abstention ([Xie et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05503)). InForage, the best-known IFT-inspired RL agent, uses a fixed per-step discount $\beta^{T-2}$, which is a tax and not rate matching, so it cannot adapt to each source's depletion profile ([Qian & Liu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09316)). **One-level disclosure has no theorem.** A second routing level "never helps and sometimes breaks accuracy" ([He et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17598)). Our hypothesis is that misrouting under a noisy softmax choice compounds multiplicatively across levels while each level adds its own travel cost. That is untested. Nobody has implemented a literal MVT patch-leaving rule in an LLM agent and compared it with fixed step budgets.

**Descriptive models supply the tool checklist [descriptive].** Bates' berrypicking, in which "the query itself … is continually shifting" and needs are satisfied bit by bit ([Bates 1989](https://pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/bates/berrypicking.html)), and Ellis's starting, chaining, browsing, differentiating, monitoring and extracting ([Ellis](https://doi.org/10.1108/eb026843)) map onto `related` and source-ref chasing, backlinks, `ls` over taxonomy folders, authority-tier filters, freshness subscriptions and verbatim-quote validation [I]. Kuhlthau's finding that at Presentation "relevance decreases and redundancy increases" ([Kuhlthau](https://wp.comminfo.rutgers.edu/ckuhlthau/information-search-process/)) is the sufficiency-side stopping signal: stop when new units mostly duplicate claims already in the working set. Taylor's levels of need make the clarify step a move from the compromised question back toward the conscious need ([Taylor 1968](https://doi.org/10.5860/crl_29_03_178)). One good clarifying question gave over 170% P@1 improvement in an oracle setting, an upper bound on real gains ([Aliannejadi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06554)). These are models of human seekers, and their transfer to LLM agents is unvalidated.

### Short loops and external verifiers follow from compounding error

**The methods.** DRC lets agents choose parameterized queries rather than write them, puts deterministic checks first, and makes joins and aggregations the database's job. ADL gives agents typed graph tools instead of raw text-to-Cypher. SKB keeps loops short and validates cited anchors in code.

**Theorems on expressivity [P].** Log-precision transformers without intermediate steps lie in uniform TC⁰. With $t(n)$ chain-of-thought steps they simulate any $t(n)$-time Turing machine, $\mathrm{TIME}(t(n))\subseteq\mathrm{CoT}(t(n))$ ([Merrill & Sabharwal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07923)). Constant-depth, constant-precision transformers without CoT lie in AC⁰ ([Li, Liu, Zhou & Ma](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12875)). **Theorems on error [P].** If a composite task needs $n$ independent applications of a step that errs with probability $\varepsilon$, failure probability tends exponentially to at least $1-c$, and with recovery probability at most $c$ per step, long-run accuracy is at most $c/(c+\varepsilon)$ ([Dziri et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18654)). Behaviour cloning with per-step error $\varepsilon$ can incur $T^2\varepsilon$ extra cost over $T$ steps, while training on the learner's own distribution brings it to $uT\varepsilon$ ([Ross, Gordon & Bagnell](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)). With constant per-step accuracy $p$ and no self-correction, the horizon at success rate $s$ is $H_s = \ln s/\ln p$, and per-step accuracy itself degrades as models condition on their own earlier errors ([Sinha et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09677)). *Our extension [S].* If an external verifier catches a fraction $r$ of errors and forces a successful retry, effective per-step accuracy is about $1-\varepsilon(1-r)$, which multiplies the usable horizon by about $1/(1-r)$. Self-critique has small or even negative $r$: GPT-4 showed "significant performance collapse with self-critique and significant performance gains with sound external verification" ([Stechly et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08115)). Deterministic checks (schema validation, exact match against the store, SQL constraints) have $r$ near 1 for the error classes they cover. With automatic verifiers, repeated sampling lifted SWE-bench Lite from 15.9% to 56% ([Brown et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21787)).

*Mapping [I].* CoT power grows with step count, but each step is a chance to err, so composition should move into a deterministic executor. A parameterized query replaces $T$ error-prone generated steps with one *selection* step, shrinking the exponent in $(1-\varepsilon)^T$. Peng et al.'s bound adds that a single layer cannot compose relations over a large domain at all, and iterated composition needs $\Omega(\sqrt{n/(Hdp)})$ CoT steps ([Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08164)). Pre-computed joins (typed edges, SQL views, semantic layers) turn an $n\log n$-bit in-context computation into one lookup. That explains why restricting agents to parameterized tools let 3B models match much larger ones ([Bogliolo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22063)). Self-conditioning also implies that loops should not carry their own failed intermediate outputs forward verbatim. *What breaks.* The expressivity results concern what is representable with idealized constructions, not what trained models learn. The compounding critique assumes the model learned an accurate next-step predictor in the first place, which fails on some planning tasks ([Bachmann & Nagarajan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)). No theorem covers error accumulation across *retrieval* rounds specifically.

## Power laws price the long tail, and practice theory limits capture

### Zipf, Heaps, Bradford and Lotka make coverage a cost curve

**The methods.** SKB and ADL keep lexical, exact and alias channels because dense retrieval fails on jargon and acronyms. SKB adds `related` links and a link graph, and ADL finds that graphs pay off only for multi-hop and corpus-level questions. RA logs usage as a ranking signal.

**Empirical laws with partial derivations.** Zipf's rank–frequency law $f(r)\propto r^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha\approx1$ fits "almost all of the variance", but language is only near-Zipfian, rank and frequency must be estimated from split samples, and Zipf curves arise even from random typing, so a fit identifies no mechanism ([Piantadosi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4176592/)). Heaps' law $V(n) = Kn^\beta$ has $\beta\approx0.4$–0.6 for English ([Heaps' law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaps%27_law)), and a refined finite-size Zipf-to-Heaps relation beat the asymptotic one on 34 of 35 datasets ([Lü, Zhang & Zhou](https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.3861)). *Zipf ⇒ Heaps [S].* Drawing $N$ tokens from $p_r \propto r^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha>1$, $\mathbb E[V(N)] \approx \sum_r[1-e^{-Np_r}]$. Types with $Np_r\gtrsim1$ contribute about $r_c = (N/\zeta(\alpha))^{1/\alpha}$, and the tail contributes a quantity proportional to $r_c$, so $\mathbb E[V(N)] = \Theta(N^{1/\alpha})$ and $\beta = 1/\alpha$. The tension is that $\alpha\approx1$ would give $\beta\approx1$, against measured 0.4–0.6, which finite-size effects only partly explain. *Rare items dominate [S].* A Zipf exponent $\alpha$ corresponds to a Lotka size-frequency exponent $1+1/\alpha$ ([Egghe](https://shop.elsevier.com/books/power-laws-in-the-information-production-process/egghe/978-0-12-088753-8)). At $\alpha=1$, the share of types seen exactly once is $1/\zeta(2) = 6/\pi^2 \approx$ **61%**. For $N=10^6$ entities, the share of mentions beyond rank $r$ is $1-H_r/H_N$: 0.64 beyond the top 100, **0.48 beyond the top 1,000** and 0.32 beyond the top 10,000. Even a perfect cache of the 1,000 most common entities misses about half of entity-bearing queries. These are exactly the facts where parametric knowledge is weakest: accuracy tracks the number of pre-training documents mentioning a question's entities, causally ([Kandpal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.08411)), and "scaling fails to appreciably improve memorization … in the long tail" ([Mallen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511)). *Bradford ⇒ cost [S].* If the source at rank $r$ yields $C/r$ relevant items, cumulative yield is $C\,H_r \approx C(\ln r+\gamma)$, the Leimkuhler–Brookes form, and equal-yield zones have $r_j = n\,r_{j-1}$, which is Bradford's $1:n:n^2$ ([Bates 2002](https://www.pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/bates/articles/SearchingBradford.pdf)). With $n=5$, the three zones need 1, 6 and 31 source units cumulatively, so **the last third of recall costs 25 times as many sources per item as the core**. Lotka's general form fits author productivity widely, but only 7 of 48 datasets fitted the inverse-square case, so exponents must be estimated, not assumed ([Pao](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.4630370105)).

*Mapping [I].* Bates' reading of Bradford gives each region its access path: browsing for the core, indexed search for the middle, and link-following for the tail. That maps onto the KB's small always-loaded index plus `ls`, the hybrid index, and `related` links, backlinks and source-ref chasing, which is a reason for SKB's combination rather than any one alone. It also predicts *when* graph links pay: for tail items, as with rare-entity and multi-hop questions, matching ADL's finding. Heaps' law means vocabulary maintenance never finishes. Each batch still adds new terms at rate $\beta K N^{\beta-1}$, so the glossary needs a continuous new-term candidate job, and a jump above the fitted Heaps curve after an ingest batch is a cheap anomaly signal for a new product line, an acronym flood or OCR garbage. **Cumulative advantage warns about usage signals [generative model].** Price's "success breeds success" urn generates Lotka- and Zipf-like distributions ([Price 1976](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/price/pricetheory1976.pdf)), and preferential attachment does the same for networks ([Barabási & Albert](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9910332)). Ranking boosts from `cited_count` or "used in a successful task" (RA implication 7) form such an urn, so they will lock in early winners. A newer, correct unit then cannot overtake an old, often-cited one, which is SKB's stale-page failure. Usage signals should be normalized by exposure, and newly verified units need exploration slots [I]. Lotka also predicts curation load: if about 60% of contributors write one unit, owner-based review queues concentrate on a few experts, a bus-factor risk to monitor [I]. Answer engines show the same shape externally, with per-engine citation Gini of 0.49–0.65 and **59.1% of cited domains cited once** ([Allaham & Diakopoulos](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23684v1)). No study has fitted these laws to enterprise SOP and policy corpora, so every number above assumes $\alpha\approx1$ and must be refitted per corpus.

### Tacit knowledge: extraction is combination, not externalization

**The methods.** SKB uses KCS as the operating model for curation, mines failed conversations and insufficient-context clusters into proposed units under human approval, and compiles SOPs into canonical step graphs with handoff and escalation steps.

**What the theory says [descriptive].** Nonaka's SECI model describes knowledge creation through socialization, externalization, combination and internalization ([Nonaka 1994](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Nonaka_1994.pdf)). Gourlay's review finds two modes plausible, "but none are supported by evidence that cannot be explained more simply", and notes that the framework omits inherently tacit knowledge ([Gourlay](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/339)). Brown & Duguid's ethnographies show that actual work differs substantially from manuals and job descriptions, and that noncanonical practice in communities bridges the gap ([Brown & Duguid](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ororsc:v:2:y:1991:i:1:p:40-57)). KCS creates knowledge "in the context of demand or use" through a Solve loop and an Evolve loop ([KCS v6](https://library.serviceinnovation.org/KCS/KCS_v6/KCS_v6_Practices_Guide/030/030)), and Argyris & Schön distinguish single-loop from double-loop learning ([infed](https://infed.org/dir/chris-argyris-theories-of-action-double-loop-learning-and-organizational-learning/)). *Mapping [I].* LLM extraction of SOP documents into step graphs is SECI *combination* (explicit to explicit). It cannot recover what Brown & Duguid show the documents omit. The tacit layer lives in tickets, chat threads, escalations and resolution notes, so the tacit path should mine resolved cases into candidate units and procedure deltas, flag divergences between the canonical step graph and observed resolution sequences ("noncanonical practice detected"), and route them to owners rather than auto-merge them. Gourlay's limit means some skill will never become text, so explicit handoff and escalation steps are required rather than an assumption that the KB is complete. KCS's Solve and Evolve loops correspond to single-loop fixes ("flag it or fix it" on one unit) and double-loop changes to the schema, facets or routing when clusters of insufficient-context queries reveal a systematic gap. That correspondence is our analogy; the KCS guide does not cite Argyris. None of this is a theorem, and no study measures what fraction of expert know-how LLM-generated SOPs capture.

**Information-quality frameworks fix the envelope boundary [empirical framework, I].** Wang & Strong separate intrinsic quality (accuracy, believability, reputation) from contextual quality (relevancy, timeliness, completeness), which "must be considered within the context of the task at hand" ([Wang & Strong](https://web.mit.edu/tdqm/www/tdqmpub/beyondaccuracy_files/beyondaccuracy.html)). Intrinsic dimensions belong in the unit envelope, while contextual ones cannot be stored and must be judged per query as relevance and sufficiency. Pipino, Lee & Wang aggregate a dimension by its weakest normalized indicator and score timeliness as $\max(0, 1-\text{currency}/\text{volatility})$ raised to a strictness exponent $s$ ([Pipino, Lee & Wang](https://doi.org/10.1145/505248.506010); the exponent form is from our knowledge of the paper and only partly verified). *Mapping [I].* Min-aggregation is "fail closed": an ACL sync older than its SLA makes a unit ineligible however good its content. The timeliness form gives `review_due` a formula. Queue a unit when $T<\theta$, that is at currency $=$ volatility $\times(1-\theta^{1/s})$, with volatility set per unit type from SKB's volatility classes.

## Open theoretical gaps worth testing

The weakest links in the chain are the places where a theorem's assumption meets an unmeasured property of the toolkit. Five are directly testable on the toolkit's own logs. First, **monotonicity of search yield**: Ferguson's fixed-round rule requires expected new evidence per round to be non-increasing, and logged trajectories can test that directly, with and without query reformulation. Second, **per-field error correlation between extraction calls**: the voting and Dawid–Skene results turn on $\rho$, and nobody has measured it for two prompts or two providers on the same document. Third, **the MVT rule versus fixed budgets**: a literal patch-leaving rule (marginal claims per token against the running average) has never been compared with fixed step caps in an LLM agent. Fourth, **contraction coefficients of LLM summarizers**: the strong-DPI decay argument against deep summary trees is a bound with no measured $\eta$, and a QA-based distortion would make it measurable. Fifth, **Zipf, Heaps and Bradford parameters for enterprise corpora**: every long-tail number here assumes $\alpha\approx1$.

Seven gaps are theoretical. No theorem gives conditions under which RRF beats its best input, and no capacity result covers the *union* of a sparse and a dense index. No worst-case theory exists for filtered graph ANN, the case behind the pgvector failure. No conformal guarantee covers multi-field extraction with arithmetic dependencies between fields. No end-to-end bound relates RAG hallucination to both the singleton rate and retriever recall. No RAG theory models temporal validity, with stale passages drawn from an earlier world state, as opposed to a generic wrong concept. No source proves that α-nDCG-style coverage is submodular in the retrieved set, although it is plausible. And the MDL route to schema induction has never been tried on LLM-extracted records rather than transactional data or knowledge graphs. Two measurement gaps bound the determinism argument: there is no published distribution of top-two logit gaps for extraction outputs, which would predict per-field flip rates, and no study of how batch-invariant serving changes field-level churn.

## Conclusion

The theory sorts the toolkit into two layers with different epistemic standing. The *store* layer (lossless evidence, external facts, normalized records, content hashes, snapshots, provenance, gated acceptance) is certified by theorems whose assumptions the toolkit can largely make true by construction: it can keep the source, pin the snapshot, fix the hash function and key the calibration set. The *access* layer (chunking, fusion, ranking, loop depth, disclosure) is governed by bounds that say what cannot work and by empirical laws that must be refitted per corpus, so its parameters can only be earned on a golden set. That changes what "state of the art" should mean for this project. Being principled does not mean replacing every heuristic with a theorem, which the theory does not support. It means placing information loss where a theorem says it is cheapest (query-aware, at read time), placing guarantees where their assumptions can be enforced (per extractor version, per snapshot), and measuring every build-time quantity no theorem controls, such as ANN recall, cross-call error correlation, per-round evidence yield and summary contraction.

The most consequential corrections are also the cheapest to adopt. A version-keyed calibration set, a fixed pivot order, BM25F field weights, tuned linear fusion, filter-not-downrank for superseded content, abstention-aware scoring and a rate-based stopping rule are configuration changes, not new systems. The larger opportunity is that the toolkit is unusually well placed to close the open gaps itself. It already logs trajectories, extraction runs, snapshot diffs and golden-set results, which are exactly the data needed to test monotonicity, measure $\rho$ and $\eta$, and compare an MVT stopping rule with fixed budgets. Publishing those measurements would turn several of this report's inferred mappings into tested ones.
