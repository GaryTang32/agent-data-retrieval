# Compile Documents, Graphs and Tools Into One Lake

As of late 2026, every layer needed to turn messy documents and tool catalogs into agent-retrievable data has a strong open-source component, but no project owns the layer that ties them together. Small specialized document VLMs now score **93 to 97 on OmniDocBench versus 78 to 86 for classic pipelines** ([OmniDocBench](https://github.com/opendatalab/OmniDocBench)). Independent benchmarks show graph retrieval wins on multi-hop and corpus-level questions but loses to reranked vector search on simple facts, and query-time token cost ranges from **about 1.15x vector RAG for HippoRAG2 to about 377x for GraphRAG global search** ([GraphRAG-Bench, Xiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05690)). Constrained decoding has made schema-valid JSON routine, but extraction versioning, grounding and permissions remain do-it-yourself. Treating tools as retrievable records cuts tool-definition context by about 85% in Anthropic's own tests ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/advanced-tool-use)). The gap that matters is an **open, engine-neutral record format** covering parsed elements, tables, entities and relations, embeddings, tool records, access-control lists and lineage. None of the dozens of open-source and commercial systems surveyed provides one; each keeps its own internal state, from GraphRAG's parquet files to Graphiti's Neo4j graph ([GraphRAG](https://github.com/microsoft/graphrag); [Graphiti](https://github.com/getzep/graphiti)). The new toolkit should be an Apache-2.0 "agent context compiler": a specification plus reference implementation that orchestrates existing parsers, extractors and stores. It should not try to be a parser, a graph database or a chat app. It should differentiate on five things: versioned and grounded extraction, cost-bounded incremental graph construction, tools stored as data next to documents, ACLs and lineage enforced in the data layer, and a neutral evaluation harness. The roadmap below has four phases, each gated by numeric exit criteria.

## Small document VLMs beat pipelines, but charts and grounding still break

### Accuracy has moved to sub-10B specialized models

Document parsing has split into two camps. The first is pipeline toolkits (Docling, the MinerU pipeline, Marker, Unstructured), which chain layout detection, OCR and table models. The second is compact document VLMs between roughly 0.1B and 9B parameters (PaddleOCR-VL, MinerU2.5, GLM-OCR, dots.ocr, DeepSeek-OCR 2, olmOCR 2, Chandra 2), which now lead the leaderboards. On OmniDocBench v1.6 the top entries are **OXR-1.0 at 97.03, PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 at 96.34, MinerU2.5-Pro at 95.75 and GLM-OCR at 95.22**. Large general models trail them: Gemini 3 Pro scores 92.91 and GPT-5.2 86.59. Pipelines sit lower still, with **MinerU-Pipeline at 86.47 and Marker at 78.44**, and Docling is not listed at all ([OmniDocBench](https://github.com/opendatalab/OmniDocBench)). A 0.9B specialist beating a frontier VLM is the central fact of 2026 parsing. It means accurate parsing is now cheap enough to run locally on a single GPU.

These numbers carry a caveat. Nearly every benchmark in this space is run by an interested party. OpenDataLab maintains OmniDocBench and also builds MinerU. Datalab publishes the Marker comparisons. LlamaIndex staff authored ParseBench. One 2026 review "found no neutral head-to-head" of Docling, MinerU and Marker at current versions ([Particula Tech](https://particula.tech/blog/docling-vs-mineru-vs-marker-pdf-parser)). OmniDocBench is also saturating, so 1 to 2 points among the top ten matter less than domain fit. Hugging Face's advice applies: "Your domain may not be well represented in existing benchmarks!" ([HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/ocr-open-models)). Rankings also change by benchmark. dots.ocr scores 90.77 on OmniDocBench and 85.2 on ParseBench tables, yet **0.9 on ParseBench charts** ([ParseBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08538v2)).

ParseBench is about 2,078 enterprise pages scored on more than 169,000 rules. It shows where parsing still fails. Most specialized parsers score **below 6% on charts** because they emit raw OCR text rather than structured data. Single-pass general VLMs score **below 10% on visual grounding**. Many parsers drop strike-through, superscripts and links; Docling scored 1.0 on semantic formatting. Content faithfulness tops out near 90% even for the best systems ([ParseBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08538v2)). Reading order is not solved either: top OmniDocBench models still show a reading-order edit distance of about 0.11 to 0.13, against 0.243 for Marker ([OmniDocBench](https://github.com/opendatalab/OmniDocBench)). More reasoning compute shows diminishing returns. Gemini 3.1 Pro scored below Gemini 3 Flash (minimal) on ParseBench at about **13x the cost** ([ParseBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08538v2)).

### Speed, cost and licensing decide the default stack

Throughput favors routing over any single parser. On one B200 host, Datalab measured Marker's no-OCR fast path at **23.7 pages/s**, Docling at 2.1 and MinerU at 0.54 ([Marker GitHub](https://github.com/datalab-to/marker)). These are vendor-run figures but still useful for orders of magnitude. VLM paths cost real money but are not prohibitive. In late 2025, olmOCR-2 ran at about **$178 per million pages** on an H100 at $2.69/hour ([HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/ocr-open-models)). The design that follows is a per-page router. Born-digital pages with a clean text layer take the fast path. Scanned, complex or low-confidence pages go to a document VLM. Charts and cross-page tables can optionally get a frontier-LLM repair pass, which is what Marker's `--use_llm` mode does ([Marker GitHub](https://github.com/datalab-to/marker)).

Licensing is where an open toolkit can most easily go wrong, because code and weight licenses often diverge.

- **Docling:** MIT code, and its default weights carry no revenue threshold or competitor clause ([Particula Tech](https://particula.tech/blog/docling-vs-mineru-vs-marker-pdf-parser)).
- **PaddleOCR and its VL variants:** Apache 2.0 ([FullStackCV playbook](https://fullstackcv.github.io/playbook/ocr/)).
- **MinerU:** moved from AGPL to a custom license in 2026. It requires a commercial license above **100 million MAU or USD 20 million monthly revenue**, mandates attribution for hosted services, and terminates automatically on breach ([MinerU LICENSE.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/opendatalab/MinerU/HEAD/LICENSE.md)).
- **Marker:** the code became Apache 2.0 in v2.0.0, but the weights use a modified OpenRAIL-M license. It applies above **$5 million in revenue or funding**, includes a competitor clause with no research carve-out, and lets the licensor restrict use remotely ([Particula Tech](https://particula.tech/blog/docling-vs-mineru-vs-marker-pdf-parser)).
- **PyMuPDF:** AGPL or commercial ([Particula Tech](https://particula.tech/blog/docling-vs-mineru-vs-marker-pdf-parser)).

The conclusion for the toolkit: make Docling plus PaddleOCR-VL the permissive default. Ship MinerU and Marker/Surya/Chandra as opt-in plugins. Record a model-license manifest for every weight repository and revision the toolkit downloads, because Hugging Face tags such as "openrail" or untagged GGUF repos do not reveal these terms.

### DoclingDocument is the best canonical model, but it is not enough alone

Markdown cannot be the stored truth because it discards bounding boxes and provenance. DoclingDocument is the most complete open alternative. It is a typed pydantic tree that separates body from furniture (headers and footers) and holds texts, tables, pictures and key-value items. Items link through JSON pointers, the body tree defines reading order, and each item carries a bounding box and provenance where available ([Docling docs](https://docling-project.github.io/docling/concepts/docling_document/)). Docling also covers the widest input range of any open converter: Office, ODF, iWork, HTML, EPUB, EML and MSG email, ICS, LaTeX, XBRL, images, and audio and video via ASR. It ships an MCP server and LangChain, LlamaIndex, CrewAI and Haystack integrations ([Docling GitHub](https://github.com/docling-project/docling)).

DoclingDocument lacks some fields a lake needs. Unstructured shows one missing piece: element IDs that are by default a **deterministic SHA-256 of text, position, page and filename**, plus `orig_elements` on chunks for lineage ([Unstructured docs](https://docs.unstructured.io/open-source/concepts/document-elements)). The toolkit should therefore define a DoclingDocument-compatible superset. It adds content-addressed IDs, source URI and version, parser and model identity per element, confidence, ACL principals, and links to graph and schema objects. Adapters from Unstructured, Marker JSON and MinerU/PaddleOCR-VL output keep backends swappable. Spreadsheets, audio and code need their own provenance analogs, because page-and-bbox does not fit them. Spreadsheets need typed cells and named ranges. Audio and video need time-coded segments. Code needs symbol-level chunks with a path and commit SHA. The surveyed document parsers do not cover code repositories at all.

### Chunking evidence favors simple structure plus context

Embedding-based "semantic" chunking does not earn its reputation. A NAACL 2025 study of 25 chunking configurations and 48 embedding models found fixed-size chunking outperformed semantic chunking on realistic documents ([arXiv 2410.13070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13070)). A February 2026 benchmark measured **69% end-to-end accuracy for recursive 512-token chunks against 54% for semantic chunks**, which fragmented to an average of 43 tokens ([Vecta](https://www.runvecta.com/blog/we-benchmarked-7-chunking-strategies-most-advice-was-wrong)). The best-evidenced add-on is contextual enrichment. Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval prepends 50 to 100 tokens of LLM-written context to each chunk. That cut top-20 retrieval failures by 35%, by 49% with contextual BM25, and by **67% with reranking**, at about $1.02 per million document tokens with prompt caching ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). This is vendor-run and dates from 2024, but it is the strongest public signal available. The toolkit should treat chunks as a re-runnable view over the canonical tree, not as stored truth. Chunks should respect section boundaries, keep tables and figures atomic, carry a deterministic heading-path header, and optionally add LLM context for high-value corpora.

## Graphs pay off only for multi-hop and corpus-level questions

### Extraction quality is modest, and entity resolution drives the difference

LLM-built knowledge graphs lose a lot of information. On the MINE fact-recoverability benchmark, secondary summaries put KGGen at about 66% against about 48% for Microsoft GraphRAG's extractor ([arXiv 2502.09956](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.09956)). The benchmark authors built KGGen, so this is not independent. Even so, the best open extractor loses roughly a third of the source facts. KGGen's main gain comes from clustering and de-duplicating entities, not from better extraction prompts. GraphJudge (EMNLP 2025) adds a separate LLM verification pass that filters triples ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.554.pdf)). The field has not settled how to measure entity resolution itself: ATOM notes that earlier work reported only the false discovery rate ([arXiv 2510.22590](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.22590)). Narrow, schema-constrained domains do much better. A chemistry extraction study reports **92.1% precision and 89.2% F1** ([ChemRxiv](https://chemrxiv.org/doi/pdf/10.26434/chemrxiv.10001546/v1)). That comparison is across different benchmarks, not head-to-head, but it shows why schema engineering should come before graph extraction. Since a third of facts can go missing, source chunks must stay in the lake next to the graph.

### Independent benchmarks favor routing and HippoRAG2-style designs

Two independent evaluations agree. In GraphRAG-Bench (Xiang et al.), on the novel corpus:

| System | Fact | Complex reasoning | Summarization | Prompt tokens per query |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vector RAG + rerank | 60.92 | 42.93 | 51.30 | ~879 (vanilla) |
| HippoRAG2 | 60.14 | **53.38** | 64.10 | 1,008 |
| MS-GraphRAG (local / global) | 49.29 | 50.93 | **64.40** | 38,707 / 331,375 |
| LightRAG | 58.62 | 49.07 | 48.85 | 100,832 |
| LazyGraphRAG | 51.65 | 49.22 | 58.29 | n/a |
| RAPTOR | 49.25 | 38.59 | 47.10 | 3,441 |

Source: [arXiv 2506.05690](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05690). The paper's text elsewhere gives about 4×10⁴ tokens for global search, so the 331,375 figure carries an internal inconsistency.

Plain RAG with reranking matches or beats every graph method on simple facts, where extra graph context adds noise. Graph methods win on reasoning and summarization. On the medical corpus, MS-GraphRAG falls to 38.63 on facts ([arXiv 2506.05690](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05690)). Han et al. reach the same split. RAG is stronger on single-hop detail and GraphRAG on multi-hop reasoning, neither wins consistently, and LLM-as-judge summarization scoring has position bias ([arXiv 2502.11371](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11371v2)). That bias also affects the original GraphRAG and LazyGraphRAG win-rate results.

Two design choices follow. First, a **query router that classifies question type** is a core component, not an add-on. Second, HippoRAG2 is the best default graph path. It treats passages as graph nodes, seeds a Personalized PageRank walk from dense retrieval, and delivers graph benefits at near-vector token cost ([arXiv 2502.14802](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14802)). Community summaries in the GraphRAG style are worth their cost only for global sensemaking workloads.

### Indexing cost and incremental updates are the top user complaint

Microsoft's own README warns that GraphRAG indexing "can be an expensive operation" ([DEV Community quoting README](https://dev.to/jangwook_kim_e31e7291ad98/lightrag-vs-microsoft-graphrag-in-2026-the-costs-we-could-verify-and-the-benchmark-numbers-we-5bci)). A 2026 preprint measured LightRAG at **more than 757M tokens on HotpotQA versus 62M for naive RAG**, calling it impractical for frequently updated knowledge bases ([arXiv 2609.10239](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.10239)). LazyGraphRAG shows a cheap path exists. It replaces LLM extraction with noun-phrase extraction at index time, and indexing then costs about **0.1% of full GraphRAG**, roughly the same as vector RAG ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag-setting-a-new-standard-for-quality-and-cost/)). That claim is vendor-run, and as of mid-2025 the blog did not place LazyGraphRAG in the OSS library.

Updates are the other half of the problem. GraphRAG re-clusters after each update ([EraRAG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.20963)). Its 1.0 `update` command reuses an LLM cache, but community restructuring still drives cost ([beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/graphrag-vs-vector-rag-indexing-query-cost-lightrag-lazygraphrag-2026)). Graphiti takes the opposite approach. It ingests "episodes" incrementally and uses **bitemporal edges** that separate when a fact was true from when the system learned it. On contradiction it invalidates the old edge instead of deleting it, which preserves history and supports point-in-time queries ([Graphiti docs](https://help.getzep.com/graphiti/graphiti/overview/)). The toolkit should adopt append-plus-invalidate edges with four timestamps, keep provenance to source spans on every node and edge, and recompute global artifacts such as communities lazily or on a schedule, never on every insert.

Agent-memory benchmarks deserve skepticism. Zep and Mem0 dispute each other's LoCoMo numbers, with Zep's own figure revised from 84% to 75.14% ([Zep blog](https://blog.getzep.com/lies-damn-lies-statistics-is-mem0-really-sota-in-agent-memory/)). Practitioners could not reproduce Mem0's published LoCoMo results with its own harness ([ATANT, arXiv 2604.10981](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10981)). Mem0's graph variant adds only about 2% over its non-graph memory ([arXiv 2504.19413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19413)). For conversational recall, graphs help mainly with temporal reasoning and contradiction handling, not raw recall.

### Store choice is a licensing and survival decision

Kuzu, the leading MIT-licensed embedded graph database, was archived in October 2025 after a reported Apple acquisition. Its community fork LadybugDB reached v0.15 in March 2026 ([The Data Quarry](https://thedataquarry.com/blog/from-kuzu-to-ladybug/); [LadybugDB](https://blog.ladybugdb.com/post/ladybug-spreading-its-wings/)). Server licenses vary sharply. **Neo4j Community is GPLv3 and single-instance, Memgraph is BSL 1.1, and FalkorDB is SSPLv1**, which bars offering it as a managed service. Apache AGE (a Postgres extension), NebulaGraph, ArcadeDB and HugeGraph are Apache 2.0 ([ArcadeDB comparison](https://arcadedb.com/blog/open-source-knowledge-graph-graphrag-databases-compared/)). These license facts come from vendor or secondary comparisons and should be checked against the LICENSE files. The Kuzu episode argues for storing the canonical graph as portable node and edge tables in the lake, with graph engines as replaceable query accelerators.

### Agents need typed graph tools, not raw text-to-Cypher

Zero-shot text-to-Cypher is unreliable. On CypherBench (ACL 2025), Claude 3.5 Sonnet reached **61.6%** execution accuracy and GPT-4o 60.2%, and no model under 10B parameters exceeded 20% ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.438.pdf)). On auto-generated enterprise schemas, 11 local models averaged **0.036** ([PIPE-Cypher](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08481v1)). Database-grounded correction raises accuracy substantially. CypherGuard reports lifting one frontier model from 71.76% to **94.68%** ([arXiv 2610.41755](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.41755v1)), though these results are author-reported. The evidence supports two layers. The first is typed tools such as `neighbors`, `path_between`, `facts_about(entity, as_of)` and `community_summary`. The second is a guarded Cypher fallback with schema injection, read-only enforcement, dry-run validation and repair.

## Schema engineering solved syntax but not semantics or versioning

### Constrained decoding makes valid JSON routine, and grounding is the new frontier

Native structured outputs now compile a JSON schema into a grammar and constrain decoding. In Anthropic's implementation the feature is stable across its API and the major clouds ([Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). The supported schema subset is narrow, though. It excludes recursive schemas, numeric and string-length constraints, external `$ref`, and any `additionalProperties` other than false ([Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). JSONSchemaBench's 10,000 real-world schemas proved a "significant challenge" for both LLMs and constrained-decoding frameworks ([arXiv 2501.10868](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.10868)). Schemas therefore need to target a lowest common subset, with ranges, lengths and cross-field rules enforced afterwards in Pydantic, Pandera or SHACL.

Semantic correctness is the harder problem. Google's LangExtract (Apache 2.0) maps every extracted value to exact character offsets and marks values it cannot ground with `char_interval = None` so they can be filtered out ([GitHub](https://github.com/google/langextract)). A competitor notes that span grounding "checks location, not correctness" ([Nutrient](https://www.nutrient.io/blog/langextract-vs-llamaindex-extraction-comparison/)). The largest 2026 extraction benchmark, ExtractBench (4,869 pages across 67 document types), scores value F1, record completeness and word- and page-level grounding F1. It finds that commercial VLMs "often truncate record lists" on long documents ([arXiv 2607.29677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29677)). The dataset sits under LlamaIndex namespaces and LlamaIndex's product ranks first, so the leaderboard is not neutral, but its metrics are the right ones. In the toolkit, every extracted value should carry `(doc_id, page, char_start, char_end, spec_id)`. List-valued fields should use multi-pass or chunked extraction by default.

### Schema induction is research-grade; LinkML is the practical authoring layer

Systems that propose schemas automatically are almost all 2025–2026 preprints with self-reported results. SchemaLoop/AnnoIndex induces hierarchical schemas from a raw corpus, extracts core fields eagerly and long-tail attributes lazily, and reports average F1 of 0.87 on three unnamed datasets ([arXiv 2608.13384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13384)). DocETL reports outputs 25–80% more accurate than engineered baselines on four tasks ([arXiv 2410.12189](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12189)). Abacus reports up to 23.6x lower cost through cost-based operator optimization ([arXiv 2505.14661](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14661v2)). The most useful human-factors evidence comes from DocWrangler's user study: users steered pipelines by turning open-ended operations into classifiers, and the authors flagged **"the need for better provenance tracking"** ([arXiv 2504.14764](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14764)). The practical workflow is to sample documents, have an LLM propose a schema, let a human edit it with in-situ notes, then extract eagerly for core fields and lazily for the long tail.

A schema needs one source and many targets. LinkML fits that role. OntoGPT/SPIRES already drives LLM extraction from LinkML and emits JSON, RDF and OWL ([OntoGPT](https://pypi.org/project/ontogpt/0.3.2)). From one LinkML definition the toolkit can generate JSON Schema or Pydantic for extraction, SHACL for RDF validation, a property-graph schema for graph storage (PG-Schema informed ISO GQL; see [arXiv 2211.10962](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10962)) and SQL DDL for tables. Formal ontologies pay off in specific cases. The best evidence is data.world's insurance benchmark. GPT-4 accuracy rose from **16.7% on raw SQL to 54.2% over an ontology-mapped graph** ([arXiv 2311.07509](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2311.07509)). An ontology-based query check plus repair raised it to 72.55%, including about 8% "I don't know" answers ([arXiv 2405.11706](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11706)). Ontologies earn their cost when they validate agent queries at runtime, not when they only document the data.

### Semantic layers add 17 to 31 points, refuse what they cannot model, and every study is vendor-run

Three vendor-run studies point the same way. Cube's 100-question study found a ~4 KB hand-written semantic document raised text-to-SQL from 45.5–50.5% to **67.7–68.7%**, with p<0.01 ([arXiv 2604.25149](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25149)). AtScale reported gains of **+20 to +31 points across seven LLMs** on 410 questions, larger than the gain from switching models ([AtScale](https://www.atscale.com/blog/semantic-layer-text-to-sql-accuracy/)). dbt Labs ran 11 questions × 20 runs. The semantic layer scored 100% on in-scope questions, but **0% on "too many hops" questions** where text-to-SQL scored 70–100%. The semantic layer errored out where text-to-SQL failed silently ([dbt](https://docs.getdbt.com/blog/semantic-layer-vs-text-to-sql-2026)). That coverage-versus-correctness trade-off argues for routing: try the semantic layer first, then fall back to flagged, lower-confidence SQL.

The standards are moving. The Open Semantic Interchange spec became **Apache Ossie (Incubating) in July 2026**, with Metric Language, Catalog and Ontology working groups and converters to MetricFlow and Polaris ([Snowflake](https://www.snowflake.com/en/blog/apache-ossie-open-semantic-interchange-incubator/)). dbt relicensed MetricFlow to Apache 2.0 in October 2025 ([dbt](https://www.getdbt.com/blog/open-source-metricflow-governed-metrics)). Ossie is pre-1.0, so treat it as an export target, not an internal model. The cheapest win is to auto-generate a "semantic README" for each extracted dataset, given that a plain markdown document drove Cube's gain.

### Catalogs expose discovery, not governance

Metadata platforms now ship first-party MCP servers. DataHub exposes `search`, `get_lineage`, `get_lineage_paths_between`, `list_schema_fields` and `get_dataset_queries`, with mutation off by default ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/mcp-server-datahub/)). OpenMetadata's server exposes 24 tools over Streamable HTTP with OAuth ([OpenMetadata docs](https://docs.open-metadata.org/v2.0.x/how-to-guides/mcp/connect.md)). Technical catalogs handle credentials and grants: Apache Polaris (a top-level project since February 2026), Unity Catalog OSS and Gravitino ([Merced](https://alexmerced.blog/blog/2026-09-02-metadata-platforms-in-2026.html)). As one guide puts it, a metadata MCP server "only finds tables. The technical catalog still has to grant read access" ([llms3](https://llms3.com/guides/choosing-a-lakehouse-catalog-control-plane/)). Neither kind of catalog models document-derived lineage, meaning the chain from table row to document span to extractor version.

### Extraction versioning is a genuine, measurable gap

No library or catalog surveyed treats an extraction run as a versioned entity, defined as schema version, prompt, model and source hash, with diffing and selective re-extraction. The drift is real. One pipeline that keys rows on `(entity, field, model_version)` found that **26.5% of 2,064 re-extracted values changed** across model versions ([arXiv 2606.00994](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.00994)). Dual-LLM cross-checking and human review with source highlighting exist, but in Unstract they are commercial-only features ([Unstract](https://unstract.com/unstract-editions/)). This is the clearest place for an open toolkit to lead. A content-addressed `ExtractionSpec` can make every value traceable and every schema change produce a precise re-extraction plan. A shadow run on a sample can report agreement rates before promotion.

## Agents retrieve best with lexical, hybrid and tool search side by side

### A two-tier open lake has emerged

Storage formats have stratified. Lance is the open format that combines file, table and catalog layers with built-in vector indexes and blob storage. Its v2.2 roughly halves storage and drops p50 blob-fetch latency from about 164 ms to 124 ms. LanceDB also concedes that Parquet stays strong for narrow scalar scans ([LanceDB](https://www.lancedb.com/blog/lance-format-v2-2-benchmarks-half-the-storage-none-of-the-slowdown)). All of these numbers are vendor-run. Iceberg v3 brings deletion vectors, row lineage and VARIANT ([Iceberg spec](https://iceberg.apache.org/spec/)). DuckDB 1.5.3 reads and writes Iceberg v3 including deletion vectors and MERGE INTO ([DuckDB](https://duckdb.org/2026/05/29/new-iceberg-features)). DuckLake 1.0 shipped as production-ready in April 2026 and keeps metadata in a SQL database ([DuckLake](https://ducklake.select/2026/04/13/ducklake-10/)). Iceberg-community writers already describe Lance and Iceberg side by side on one object store ([iceberglakehouse.com](https://iceberglakehouse.com/posts/2026-05-24-lance-iceberg-multimodal/)). For the toolkit, that means Lance for chunks, vectors and page images, and Iceberg or DuckLake for entities, edges, extracted tables, lineage and ACLs, all queryable from DuckDB. Vector-engine licenses are mostly permissive: Milvus, Qdrant and Vespa are Apache-2.0, Weaviate is BSD-3 and pgvector uses the PostgreSQL License ([Redis comparison](https://redis.io/blog/best-open-source-vector-databases-comparison/)). Pluggable server backends add little licensing risk.

### Multi-stage retrieval wins on hard queries, and grep is a serious contender

Reasoning-heavy retrieval rewards rerankers more than first-stage choice. BRIGHT's original best retriever scored **24.3 nDCG@10** ([arXiv 2407.12883](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.12883)). Multi-stage systems with LLM query expansion and reasoning rerankers later reported about 46.8 ([DIVER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07995)). Visual document retrieval is far from solved. On ViDoRe v3 the best ColQwen2.5 scores **0.592 nDCG@5**, against over 90 on the saturated v1 ([HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/QuentinJG/introducing-vidore-v3); [NanoVDR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.12824)). Each chunk or page should therefore store BM25 text, dense vectors, optional learned-sparse vectors, optional multi-vector embeddings and a page-image reference.

Agentic lexical search challenges vector-first designs. In "Is Grep All You Need?", grep with inline result delivery beat ANN plus reranking in every harness–model pair on a 116-question LongMemEval subset; Opus 4.6 scored **93.1 versus 83.6**. But when results were written to a file the agent had to read, grep for Codex/GPT-5.4 fell from 93.1 to 55.2. The same model also scored 93.1 in a custom harness versus 76.7 in Claude Code ([arXiv 2605.15184](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15184v1)). Pi-Serini reports a tuned BM25 in a ReAct loop at 83.1% accuracy on BrowseComp-Plus, and attributes earlier weak BM25 results to poor configuration ([arXiv 2605.10848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.10848)). These are small, single-benchmark studies, mostly on literal-span answers. They do not prove "grep is all you need". They do show that the toolkit must expose grep and paginated BM25 alongside semantic search, materialize the lake as a browsable file tree, and treat result delivery format as a tunable parameter. Anthropic's Programmatic Tool Calling, which keeps intermediate results out of context, cut tokens 37% on complex research tasks ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/advanced-tool-use)). This supports offering handle-based delivery for large results.

### MCP 2026-07-28 rewards stateless, handle-based tool design

The current MCP spec, released **July 28, 2026**, is stateless-first. It removes the initialize handshake and `Mcp-Session-Id` and carries protocol version and capabilities in per-request `_meta`. Servers that need state return explicit handles. It adds Multi Round-Trip Requests for clarification, `Mcp-Method`/`Mcp-Name` headers so gateways can route and meter without parsing bodies, and `ttlMs`/`cacheScope` on list results. It moves Tasks into an extension and deprecates Roots, Sampling, Logging, HTTP+SSE and dynamic client registration ([MCP blog](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28/)). Each of these maps onto a toolkit decision. Snapshot IDs and cursors become explicit handles. Ambiguous-entity clarification uses MRTR. Long ingestion jobs run through the Tasks extension. Header routing lets gateways apply per-tool ACLs. MCP and A2A cannot express document-level governance on their own ([arXiv 2606.31498](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.31498)), so permission enforcement must live in the data layer.

### Tools are a retrieval corpus, and generic retrievers handle them poorly

Treating tool definitions as data is validated from two sides. On the vendor side, Anthropic found 58 tools across five servers consumed about 55K tokens. Tool Search cut a comparable setup from about 77K to 8.7K tokens, **an 85% reduction**, and raised internal MCP-eval accuracy from 49% to 74% for Opus 4 and from 79.5% to 88.1% for Opus 4.5. Adding one to five usage examples per tool lifted complex-parameter accuracy from 72% to 90% ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/advanced-tool-use)). These are internal evals with no independent replication. On the academic side, ToolRet (43,215 tools) found every retriever **below 35% Completeness@10**, with strong general IR models such as ColBERT sometimes losing to BM25 ([arXiv 2503.01763](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.01763)). Tool-specific methods help. LLM document expansion plus Tool-Embed-4B reached N@10 52.23, and reranking raised it to 56.44 ([arXiv 2510.22670](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.22670)). Semantically colliding tools hurt more than random noise ([MDPI Algorithms](https://doi.org/10.3390/a19060447)), and dynamically retrieved descriptions open a tool-poisoning vector ([safeguard.sh](https://safeguard.sh/resources/blog/tool-selection-for-ai-agents-explained-large-tool-catalogs-tool-search-deferred-loading-and-poisoned-descriptions)). Tool records therefore need expanded descriptions, synthetic queries, usage examples, namespacing, signatures and the same ACLs as documents.

### Permissions must be pre-filters in every call

Relevance ranking ignores authorization, so cross-tenant leakage is a structural risk. A Red Hat preprint formalizes policy-aware ingestion and retrieval-time gating ([arXiv 2605.05287](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.05287)). Practitioners report that "incremental sync of permissions is significantly harder than incremental sync of content" ([Truto](https://truto.one/blog/how-to-maintain-document-level-rbac-in-enterprise-rag-pipelines/)). A 2026 production-RAG paper ranks application-layer filter bugs among the most severe and hardest-to-detect failures ([arXiv 2605.03275](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.03275)). Principals belong as indexed columns on every chunk, vector, node, edge, table row and tool record, resolved from the MCP auth token and injected into every search, grep, SQL and graph call. Grep over materialized files needs per-principal views, which remains an open design problem.

The commercial side has converged on "parse, index, agentic retrieve, expose over MCP", and each offering is locked to its own platform:

Consolidation adds lock-in risk. **Glean bought Aryn on March 11, 2026, and the DocParse cloud shut down on April 15** ([Aryn](https://www.aryn.ai/post/aryn-transition)). Reducto raised a $75M Series B ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/reducto-raises-75m-series-b-to-define-the-future-of-ai-document-intelligence-302581462.html)), which shows parsing is becoming a well-funded commodity that an open toolkit should orchestrate rather than rebuild.

Tool catalogs are moving toward tools as records, but separately from documents. SCOUT exposes a BM25-plus-dense `tool_search` meta-tool and reports about 99% tool-token savings at PayPal (self-reported) ([arXiv 2608.23992](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23992)). The agentic-community MCP Gateway & Registry offers hybrid tool discovery ([AWS Open Source Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/opensource/governing-ai-assets-at-scale-with-mcp-gateway-and-registry/)). Composio, IBM ContextForge and agentgateway are active open gateways ([Composio](https://github.com/ComposioHQ/composio); [ContextForge](https://github.com/IBM/mcp-context-forge); [agentgateway](https://github.com/agentgateway/agentgateway)). No surveyed project stores tools in the same graph and catalog as documents and entities, with relations such as "tool X writes entity type Y" or "document Z documents tool X". This absence comes from searches, not a definitive census. The researchers did not verify whether an emerging Linux Foundation or MCP effort targets a unified document, graph and tool format, so that check belongs at the start of Phase 1.

Taken together, the evidence ranks the open gaps as follows:

1. An engine-neutral intermediate format.
2. Cost-bounded incremental graph construction.
3. Tools as first-class data.
4. ACLs and lineage held as data rather than app logic.
5. Parser-agnostic ingestion with per-element quality signals.
6. Evaluation and diffing across versions.

The last is the weakest-evidenced but the cheapest to differentiate on, given how contested vendor benchmarks are.

## A reference architecture: an open agent-context compiler

The toolkit should behave like a compiler. Sources go in. A typed, versioned, permission-carrying intermediate representation sits in the middle and is stored on open formats. Multiple "backends" come out: indexes, graph projections, semantic models, file trees and MCP tool surfaces. The specification is the product, and the reference implementation proves it works.

### The unified record model

| Record | Key fields | Stored in |
|---|---|---|
| `Source` | URI, content hash, etag/version, connector, ACL principals | Iceberg/DuckLake |
| `Document` / `Element` | DoclingDocument-superset tree; content-addressed element ID; page, bbox, char span or time-code; parser and weight revision; confidence | Lance (blobs, page images) + Iceberg (element table) |
| `Chunk` (view) | ordered element IDs + offsets, heading path, optional context, chunker version | Lance |
| `Embedding` | model ID and version, dense / sparse / multi-vector | Lance |
| `Entity` / `Relation` | LinkML type, resolved canonical ID, bitemporal timestamps (valid from/to, recorded/expired), provenance spans, extractor `spec_id` | Iceberg node and edge tables, projected to a graph engine |
| `FieldValue` / extracted table row | value, `spec_id`, grounding span, validation status, judge score | Iceberg/DuckLake |
| `ExtractionSpec` | hash of schema version, prompt, examples, model and parameters, parser version | Iceberg (registry) |
| `ToolRecord` | name, JSON schema, expanded description, synthetic queries, examples, server, version, auth scope, side effects, cost and latency, signature, read/write edges to entity types | Lance + Iceberg + graph |
| `Principal` / ACL edge | user, group or tenant, versioned per source sync | Iceberg; mandatory filter column everywhere |
| `Snapshot` | table and Lance versions pinned for reproducible answers | catalog |

### The pipeline, layer by layer

**Ingestion.** Connectors detect change by etag and content hash. The parse router sends born-digital pages to Docling's text path. Scanned and complex pages go to PaddleOCR-VL, which is Apache 2.0 (its 1.6 release scores 96.34 on OmniDocBench). Charts and cross-page tables optionally get an LLM repair pass. MinerU, Marker/Chandra, Reducto, LandingAI and the cloud SQL parsers sit behind adapters, and a license manifest records which weights produced each element. Office, email and media go through Docling backends. Spreadsheets go to typed tables, and code repositories to an AST chunker. MCP servers and OpenAPI specs are ingested as tool sources.

**Schema engineering.** A LinkML schema is the single source. It generates Pydantic and JSON Schema in the constrained-decoding subset, SHACL, a property-graph schema, SQL DDL and an Ossie/MetricFlow semantic model with an auto-written semantic README. Schema induction proposes types and fields from samples for human approval. Extraction runs under a content-addressed spec with span grounding. A QA ladder then filters the results: constrained decoding and validators first, then a grounding check, then table-level Pandera suites generated from LinkML, then a sampled LLM judge with a review queue.

**Graph engineering.** HippoRAG2-style passage nodes link to entity nodes. Entity resolution clusters embeddings and uses LLM adjudication, and a judge pass filters weak triples. Extraction is schema-guided where a schema exists, with a LazyGraphRAG-style noun-phrase mode as the cheap default for unschematized corpora. Edges are bitemporal and append-plus-invalidate. Communities and summaries are computed lazily under a token budget.

**Storage.** Lance holds text, vectors and blobs. Iceberg (for interoperability) or DuckLake (for single-node simplicity) holds everything relational and graph-shaped, and DuckDB is the embedded engine. Optional accelerators are LadybugDB embedded, Postgres plus AGE plus pgvector, Neo4j, Qdrant or Vespa. Extracted tables register in an Iceberg REST catalog such as Polaris, Unity OSS or Gravitino, and descriptive metadata and lineage are pushed to DataHub or OpenMetadata.

**Access.** An MCP 2026-07-28 server exposes typed tools: hybrid `search` (lexical, semantic or hybrid, with filters and a snapshot), `grep` with deep paginated caching, `read` (text plus an optional page image), read-only `sql` routed through the semantic layer first, typed graph tools with a guarded Cypher fallback, `search_tools` and `load_tool`, and `describe_schema`. A query router sends factoid questions to hybrid search plus a reranker, multi-hop questions to the PPR graph path, global questions to community summaries, and metric questions to the semantic layer. Every call injects ACL filters and returns snapshot-pinned handles. An OpenAPI mirror serves non-MCP agents.

**Evaluation.** A built-in harness runs the evaluations listed in the roadmap below, on the user's own corpus as well as public sets. It reports cost (tokens, tool calls, dollars) next to accuracy.

### Where to differentiate, and where not to

Five choices set this toolkit apart from the surveyed field.

**An open spec with conformance tests.** No surveyed project defines one. Shipping exporters to Azure AI Search, Databricks/Unity, Snowflake, Bedrock and Neo4j turns hyperscaler bundling from a threat into a distribution channel.

**Versioned, grounded extraction.** The `ExtractionSpec` registry and shadow re-extraction diffs address the 26.5% drift finding and the provenance gap DocWrangler identified, which no open tool covers.

**Cost-bounded graph engineering.** Schema-first extraction, entity-level deltas, lazy communities and hard token budgets target users' top complaint directly.

**Tools as data.** Tool records live in the same graph, under the same ACLs and lineage, so a single retrieval can return a fact and the tool that verifies or acts on it.

**A neutral, reproducible evaluation harness.** In a field where parsing, extraction and memory leaderboards are mostly run by interested parties, a neutral harness carries credibility no vendor can match.

The toolkit should explicitly not build a parser, a graph database, a vector database or a chat UI. Those markets are saturated, well funded, or both.

Adoption depends on four conditions. The license must be Apache-2.0 with no enterprise carve-out. `pip install` should produce a first lake in minutes on embedded defaults (Docling, LanceDB, DuckDB). The toolkit should integrate as a LlamaIndex or Haystack component and as an MCP server rather than compete with them. And it needs foundation governance, because stalled projects such as R2R and fast-graphrag show what happens without it.

Graph engineering comes third because schema-first extraction makes it cheaper, and because the evidence shows graphs pay off only for a subset of questions that the router must first identify.

## Conclusion

The research changes what "state of the art" means here. Component quality is no longer the bottleneck. Small open models parse nearly as well as anything available, constrained decoding produces valid JSON, and MCP has settled the tool-transport question. The bottleneck is what happens between components. Provenance is lost between parser and chunk, extraction drifts silently between model versions, permissions are skipped between source and index, and tools are catalogued away from the data they act on. The largest measured effects in this research came from structural context, not bigger models. Semantic layers outperformed model swaps, reranking matched graph methods on facts, deferred tool loading recovered most of a context window, and harness design moved accuracy as much as retriever choice. A toolkit that engineers that context explicitly, with typed records, versions, edges and permissions, is aiming at the variable that actually moves outcomes.

Two uncertainties should shape the bet. First, demand for an open "agent data format" is inferred from product-direction signals and complaint patterns, not measured. Hyperscalers bundling "good enough" pipelines into existing contracts is the main adoption risk, so exporters into their platforms are a survival feature, not a nicety. Second, nearly every headline number in parsing, extraction, graph memory and semantic layers comes from an interested party. That cuts both ways. Claims that shape this design, such as HippoRAG2's efficiency or Lance's random-access speed, need re-testing on real workloads. The same weakness is the toolkit's opening: in a field run on vendor benchmarks, the project that ships the reproducible harness will be trusted on everything else it ships.
